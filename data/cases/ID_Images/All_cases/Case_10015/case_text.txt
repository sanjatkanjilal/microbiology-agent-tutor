History of Present Illness
IDSA 2010 Pediatric Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A pre-teen girl developed abdominal pain and vomiting.
A pre-teen girl was well until 2 days before presentation when abdominal pain and vomiting developed. The pain was initially located in the left lower quadrant, and migrated to involve the entire lower abdomen, associated with nausea. On the day of presentation, more than twenty episodes of non-bloody, non-bilious emesis occurred. She did not have fever or diarrhea.
Past Medical History
Menarche had occurred one year before presentation; her last menstrual period was one month earlier. She had no history of previous recurrent abdominal pain or menstrual irregularities.
Medications
None.
Epidemiological History
She was born in Mexico, and immigrated to the United States two years before. There was no history of animal exposure and she was not sexually active.
Physical Examination
The pulse was 100 beats per minute and blood pressure 110/64 mm Hg; the temperature was normal. The abdomen was tender to palpation, with rebound, in the right lower quadrant, and obturator and psoas signs were positive.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 19,000 per cubic millimeter with 90% neutrophils. The basic metabolic panel was normal. Urinalysis was negative for white cells, leukocyte esterase and nitrites. A urine pregnancy test was also negative. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen revealed an enlarged appendix with hyperemic mucosa, with surrounding periappendiceal inflammatory stranding, that was thought to be consistent with acute appendicitis. A calcified mesenteric lymph node was also noted.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was taken to the operating room for a laparoscopic appendectomy. Intra-operative findings included acute appendicitis, bilaterally enlarged fallopian tubes (left greater than right), adhesions from the omentum to the anterior abdominal wall, adhesions from the anterior surface of the liver to the anterior abdominal wall and yellow plaques on the liver surface. The Gynecology service was consulted to perform an intra-operative examination, which revealed a dilated, hyperemic appearance of the fallopian tubes, with fluid within the tubes appearing slightly purulent. Pelvic examination revealed copious yellow vaginal discharge, a nulliparous cervix, and a fimbriated hymen with no evidence of trauma. Cervical specimens were sent for Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) and cultures.
An endometrial curettage and biopsy were performed. Pathological examination of the tissue is shown (Figures 1 and 2).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Pathological examination of endometrial tissue revealed a proliferative phase endometrium; Figure 1 shows granulomatous inflammation, H&E stain, at 600 x magnification and Figure 2, with acid fast stain, demonstrates rare acid-fast bacilli at 1000 x magnification.
Cultures of the endometrium grew acid-fast bacilli, identified as Mycobacterium bovis by multiplex, real-time PCR assay. Testing for N. gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia NAAT was negative.
Antibiotic susceptibility testing of the Mycobacterium bovis showed sensitivity to isoniazid, rifampin and ethambutol and resistance to pyrazinamide.
The patient improved following surgery and the abdominal pain and emesis resolved. A skin test for tuberculosis (PPD) was positive at greater than 25 mm induration at 40 hours. A Quantiferon-Gold test was also positive. A chest radiograph showed no evidence of past or active pulmonary tuberculosis.
Additional history, obtained from the patient and family, revealed that there was no significant exposure to persons with known Mycobacterium tuberculosis disease. In Mexico, the family had a history of consumption of fresh unpasteurized cheese (queso fresco).
Treatment and Followup
The patient was started on isoniazid, rifampin, and ethambutol. After susceptibilities were known, isoniazid and rifampin were continued to complete one year of treatment. Throughout follow-up visits, the patient continued to do well. Follow-up laparoscopy two months after the end of therapy showed normal fallopian tubes and ovaries with minimal adhesions seen in the pelvis. The intraabdominal adhesions had resolved, however, a few plaques on the liver persisted.
Discussion
Mycobacterium bovis is one of the seven species in the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (Ernst et al. 2007). Tuberculosis due to M. bovis is a zoonosis, as it primarily infects cattle, which can transmit the pathogen to humans through consumption of unpasteurized, contaminated dairy products (CDC 2005).
In industrialized nations like the United States, tuberculosis caused by M. bovis is rare because of pasteurization of milk and testing and culling of infected cattle (Hlavsa et al. 2008). M. bovis is a major public health issue in developing countries, however the true burden of disease is likely underestimated due to shortage of laboratory capabilities to isolate and differentiate members of the tuberculosis complex (Cosivi et al. 1998).
Tuberculosis caused by M. bovis and tuberculosis caused by M. tuberculosis cannot be distinguished clinically, radiographically or pathologically and thus further laboratory diagnosis is required for identification. Pyrazinamide susceptibility can be used to screen for M. bovis as it is inherently resistant; however, M. tuberculosis with resistance to pyrazinamide alone has also been described (Am. Thor. Society et al. 2003).
While abdominal tuberculosis caused by M. bovis is a well-described extrapulmonary manifestation of infection, cases of pelvic tuberculosis due to M. bovis may be missed because of lack of laboratory resources for speciation. Pelvic tuberculosis is an important cause of infertility in the developing world because it causes tubal blockage, peritubal, pelvic and abdominal adhesions and atrophy of the endometrium. The prognosis for fertility is reported to be poor in women with genital tuberculosis due to intrauterine adhesions and blockage of fallopian tubes (Sharma et al. 2009, Sharma et al. 2008, Varma 1991).
A careful exposure history, including inquiry about the consumption of unpasteurized milk products, eventually led to the ultimate diagnosis. Rapid identification of M. bovis was essential given its intrinsic resistance to pyrazinamide so that therapy could be tailored appropriately.
Final Diagnosis
Abdominal and pelvic infection caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
