History of Present Illness
A teenaged female presented with eight days of progressively worsening fever, mid-epigastric abdominal pain, and body aches. Three days prior to presentation, she first noticed a nonpruritic, flat, faint pink rash over her entire body including the palms and soles. Three days prior to presentation, she had transient conjunctival injection and edema to her hands and feet. Two days prior to presentation, she had non-productive cough and shortness of breath. Initially, her symptoms improved with antipyretics. However, on the day of presentation her home temperature increased to 103.9 degrees Fahrenheit. She also noted progressive dyspnea which prompted her to seek care. On day of presentation, she endorsed one episode of non-bloody emesis. Three weeks prior to presentation, the patient had a mild upper respiratory tract infection that was self-limited. Her family member tested positive for COVID-19 a week prior to presentation.
Past Medical History
Mild intermittent asthma
Medications
None
Epidemiological History
She lived in the Pacific United States. A few months ago, patient noted a flea infestation in some clothing. She denied sexual activity, drug use, or travel.
Physical Examination
Vital signs were notable for blood pressure 91/41, heart rate 125, respiratory rate of 47, oxygen saturation was 88% on room air. Her temperature was 102.6 degrees Fahrenheit. She appeared tired. Eyes showed clear conjunctiva, pupils equal and reactive to light. The oral exam noted strawberry tongue and mildly erythematous cracked lips. No thrush or oral ulcers were noted. The neck exam did not show lymphadenopathy. Cardiac was normal rhythm, no murmurs. The pulmonary exam demonstrated fine crackles at bilateral bases. Her abdomen was slightly distended with mild epigastric tenderness to palpation without rebound or guarding. Her extremities were without edema and normal peripheral pulses. Her skin had a faint erythematous macular rash on her face, arms and legs, trunk, and palms of hands (Figure 1).

Studies
Her complete blood count showed hemoglobin of 11.5 gm/dL (reference range 11.4 - 16.0 gm/dL), white blood cells 5.5 x10 3 mcL with left shift (reference range 4.8 - 10.8 x10 3 mcL), and platelets 92 x10 3 mcL (reference range 130 – 440 x10 3 mcL). Procalcitonin was 3.51 ng/mL (reference range <.10 ng/mL). C-reactive protein was 19.3 mg/dL (reference range <0.7 mg/dL). Lactate was 1.7 mmol/L (reference range 0.5 -2.2 mmol/L). Her metabolic panel resulted with sodium 133 mmol/L (reference range 135-145 mmol/L), otherwise, creatinine and other electrolytes were within normal limits. Liver function testing showed aspartate transaminase 241 U/L (reference range 11-41 U/L), alanine transaminase 160 U/L (reference range 5-63 U/L), alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin were within normal limits. Brain natriuretic peptide was 622 pg/mL (reference range <99 pg/mL). D-dimer was 10.5 mcg/mL (reference range < 0.99 mcg/mL). Troponin was within normal limits. Ferritin was 1,431.2 ng/mL (reference range 10 – 291 ng/mL). Triglycerides were 255 mg/dL (reference range <149 mg/dL). Urinalysis demonstrated 1+ protein, 1+ ketones, 3+ hemoglobin with 11-25 red blood cells, trace leukocyte esterase and no bacteria. SARS-CoV-2, influenza A + B, and respiratory syncytial virus nasopharyngeal polymerase chain reaction tests were negative.
A chest radiograph demonstrated bilateral basilar infiltrates. (Figure 2). Computed tomography scan of the abdomen demonstrated mild fluid of the left lower quadrant and splenomegaly. Computed tomography scan of the chest demonstrated bilateral airspace opacities of the posterior lungs and moderate pulmonary edema (Figure 3). The echocardiogram showed mildly dilated left ventricle with normal systolic function, no coronary artery aneurysms, and trace pericardial effusion.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Over the next three days, she developed worsening hypoxia and increasing pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiographs. She was treated with intravenous immune globin, methylprednisolone, ceftriaxone, vancomycin, clindamycin, and azithromycin. She had intermittent temperature elevation to 102.4 degrees Fahrenheit. Her thrombocytopenia resolved. She developed leukocytosis with white blood cell count increasing to 14.2 x10 3 mcL with left shift (reference range 4.8 - 10.8 x10 3 mcL).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Her murine typhus IgM titer was >1:256 (reference range <1:64). Her murine typhus IgG titer was 1:64 (reference range <1:64). Her repeat SARS-COV-2 polymerase chain reaction test was negative in addition to negative nucleocapsid and spike antibody. Blood cultures demonstrated no growth. Group A Streptococcus DNA and antistreptolysin O titer were both negative. Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus serologies were negative. Leptospirosis antibody was negative. Human immunodeficiency virus testing was negative. Urine legionella and urine pneumococcal antigen were negative.
Treatment and Followup
She was treated with doxycycline for murine typhus. Two days after she started doxycycline, she was afebrile and saturating well off supplemental oxygen. She was discharged from the hospital four days later and completed a fourteen-day course of doxycycline. At outpatient follow-up visit five days after hospital discharge, she had continued symptomatic improvement. She did report significant fatigue. At outpatient follow-up visit one month after hospital discharge, she had completely recovered without any residual symptoms. Repeat laboratory studies and chest radiograph were normal.
Discussion
This patient presented with a multisystem inflammatory process that closely mimicked multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children secondary to SARS-CoV-2 infection with fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, rash, respiratory symptoms, and mucous membrane involvement with elevated C-reactive protein. Prior studies have shown that 2% of patients diagnosed with multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children have negative results on both SARS-CoV-2 polymerase chain reaction test and serology [1]. However, this case demonstrates the importance of including a broad differential diagnosis and obtaining a detailed social history which revealed her contact with fleas. Murine or endemic typhus is caused by Rickettsia typhi. It is typically transmitted by the rat flea, and less commonly transmitted by the cat flea or mouse flea. Murine typhus has worldwide distribution; within the United States most cases occur in Texas, California, and Hawaii. The most common clinical manifestations include faint maculopapular rash on the trunk, fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, headache, and myalgias [2]. It is usually self-limiting and therefore likely underdiagnosed [3]. This case presents the rare manifestations of severe disease in patients with delayed initiation of treatment for murine typhus. Her clinical presentation included respiratory disease with bilateral infiltrates. She also had gastrointestinal involvement with epigastric pain, splenomegaly, and elevated liver function tests. She demonstrated mucocutaneous involvement with maculopapular rash, transient conjunctivitis, and strawberry tongue. Other manifestations of severe murine typhus that have been previously documented include meningoencephalitis [4] and renal dysfunction [5]. Murine typhus should be considered in patients in appropriate geographic locations presenting with multisystem inflammatory syndrome or other syndromes of abnormal immune activation.
Final Diagnosis
Murine Typhus with multiorgan involvement
