History of Present Illness
A woman in her 40s with a history of multiple sclerosis on rituximab, hyperammonemia, and hypogammaglobulinemia presented with joint swelling and pain. The patient had suffered from joint pain for over 10 years. She was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis about 10 years prior via MRI and started on rituximab the following year. An IUD was placed a few years prior to presentation. There were concerns for peritonitis and peritoneal abscess soon after IUD insertion. The patient underwent an exploratory laparotomy; culture data was reportedly inconclusive. She was hospitalized a few months later for altered mental status and was noted to have hyperammonemia; she was referred for specialist evaluation given concerns of a potential urea cycle disorder. She continued to have joint pains and swelling. She predominantly noted swelling and pain associated with warmth and erythema in the right wrist, right sternoclavicular join, right hip, left wrist, left knee, and lower back. These symptoms had progressively worsened over the past year. She denied history of fevers, chills, chest pain, dyspnea, nausea, or rashes.

Epidemiological History
The patient resided in the southern United States. She was in a monogamous relationship with a man. The patient reported no travel outside of the United States or travel to northeastern or southwestern United States. She denied alcohol or illicit drug use. She gardens and has a cat. She denied known tick exposures.
Physical Examination
The patient’s vitals were as follows: temperature of 36.8°C, pulse of 78 beats per minute, blood pressure of 126/86 mmHg, respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. She was thin and chronically ill-appearing. Head and neck exam did not reveal thrush or oral ulcers. Cardiovascular exam was notable for regular rhythm and no murmurs. Respiratory exam was notable for clear auscultation in bilateral lung fields. Skin exam was notable for erythematous nodule at bilateral wrists (Figure 1); effusions noted at bilateral knees, right sternoclavicular joint (Figure 2), and left elbow. Abdominal exam notable for normal bowel sounds and no distention or tenderness. The remainder of his physical exam was unremarkable. Studies
Complete blood count was notable for white blood cells 13.8 x 10 3 /mcL (ref range 3.9-10.7 x 10 3 /mcL). Hemoglobin was 6.8 gm/dL (ref range 14.0-18.1gm/dL) with a mean corpuscular volume of 72 (ref range 81-98 fL). Platelet count was 486 x 10 3 /mcL (ref range 135-371 x 10 3 /mcL).
Comprehensive metabolic panel was notable for a creatinine level was 0.44 mg/dL (ref range 0.72-1.25 mg/dL). C-Reactive Protein of 266.4 mg/L (ref range 0.0 – 5.0 mg/L). Serum ammonia level of 97 mcmol/L (normal 18-72 mcmol/L).
An initial arthrocentesis of the left knee showed 194,000 nucleated cells (87% segmented), no crystals, and no bacteria on gram stain.
Imaging Studies:
Radiographs of the left elbow and bilateral knees revealed joint effusions. A right wrist radiograph demonstrated osteopenia and soft tissue edema. Bilateral hand radiographs were concerning for an underlying inflammatory arthropathy, most likely rheumatoid arthritis (Figures 3 & 4). Whole body MRI showed multiple joint effusions and synovitis involving the right sternoclavicular, bilateral hip and right knee joint.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Following admission, the patient’s leukocytosis fluctuated from 10.4 X 10 3 /mcL to 45.6 x 10 3 /mcL (ref range 3.9-10.7x10 3 /mcL). She remained afebrile throughout admission. She underwent multiple joint aspirations including both knees and right wrist. She received five days of empiric vancomycin and ceftriaxone with no changes in clinical status.
Regarding her multiple joint aspirations, no growth was demonstrated on standard bacterial, fungal, or AFB cultures. Multiple sets of blood cultures and an AFB blood culture remained negative. Further negative studies included urine and synovial Chlamydia and Gonorrhea PCR, Bartonella serologies and serum PCR, Treponemal IgG, serum Cryptococcus antigen, serum, and urine Histoplasmosis and Blastomyces antibodies, serum 1-3, beta-D-glucan, serum Coccidioides antigen. Immunoglobulin testing showed low levels of IgA, IgD, and IgG. Blood flow cytometry detected no B cells (which was expected given rituximab history).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A synovial fluid culture of the knee reported growth of 1,5000,000 CFU/mL of Ureaplasma urealyticum confirmed by PCR. A subsequent synovial fluid collection of the wrist confirmed Ureaplasma urealyticum by 16S rDNA PCR analysis.
Treatment and Followup
The patient elected to not undergo any irrigation and debridement of her afflicted joints and wanted to pursue antimicrobial therapy with close follow-up with her Infectious Diseases physician. An empiric regimen of moxifloxacin and doxycycline was decided upon. Ongoing consideration for surgical intervention was recommended upon discharge to the patient. She tolerated her antimicrobials upon the remainder of her hospital stay, but an extended course of antimicrobial therapy was recommended on discharge in the setting of her immunocompromised status and the extent of infection. Upon telephone communication a few weeks later, the patient noted mild improvement.
Discussion
Ureaplasma urealyticum is a pathogen belonging to the Mycoplasmatacea family, along with Mycoplasma species, that is typically associated with genitourinary infections. These pathogens are also associated with infectious arthritis – especially in the setting of immunocompromising conditions 1. Hypogammaglobulinemia is one such immunocompromising status that has been directly associated with both Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma related infectious polyarthritis 2. Moreover, immunosuppressive therapies that encompass prolonged B-cell depletion, like rituximab, are also associated with this disease process 3. In the absence of antibody-mediated immune protection, these organisms can survive within neutrophils and readily colonize mucosal surfaces. Disseminated disease can develop thereafter when infected neutrophils transport these pathogens to sites of inflammation and active neutrophil recruitment 4.
Cases of disseminated Ureaplasma urealyticum infection appear to mimic the manifestations of rheumatoid arthritis – particularly rheumatoid nodules 5. The presence of hyperammonemia can suggest disease due to IgA protease production by Ureaplasma. Diagnosis is often difficult given the challenges in microbiologic analysis and negative initial work-up. Specialized media of culture growth are performed in few reference laboratories within the United States. Advancements in molecular biological techniques, such as 16S rRNA polymerase chain reaction, microbial cell-free DNA, and specific PCR have allowed for improved diagnosis 1.
Treatment of Ureaplasma can be difficult given the ineffectiveness of common antimicrobial agents. For example, the absence of peptidoglycan cell wall and lack of folic acid synthesis render beta-lactams and sulfonamides/trimethoprim ineffective, respectively. Commonly utilized antimicrobial classes include macrolides, quinolones, and tetracyclines. Treatment durations rely on the degree of infection, host immune status, and response to treatment 1,3.
Final Diagnosis
Ureaplasma urealyticum native joint polyarticular septic arthritis in a patient with multiple sclerosis on multiple immunosuppressive agents and hypogammaglobulinemia
