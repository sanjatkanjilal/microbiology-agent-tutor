History of Present Illness
2012 September-October Featured Case: A woman in her seventies was admitted to the hospital because of nausea, vomiting, weakness, diarrhea, abdominal pain, fatigue and chills.
The patient had been well, with a history of diabetes mellitus and vascular disease, until approximately 4 weeks before when, in mid-summer, she was scratched and bitten numerous times on the arms, legs, and trunk by a stray or feral cat she had taken in.
She was seen at another hospital where amoxicillin-clavulanate, a tetanus booster, and rabies vaccination were reportedly administered, followed by additional rabies immunizations on days 4, 7, and 14. She did not receive a fifth dose on day 21 because she was told it was not needed (she does not recall why). She reported no subsequent warmth, swelling, pain, or swollen glands in the arms, legs, axillae, or groin following the incident. The cat was taken in by an animal society, which intended to observe it; the results of the observation period were not known.
One week before this presentation, in late summer, nausea, vomiting (without hematemesis), abdominal pain, watery non-bloody diarrhea, anorexia, weakness and fatigue developed, associated with intermittent chills. She came to the emergency department. She reported no known fever, headache, numbness, tingling, dyspnea, chest pain or rash.
Past Medical History
She had insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (with average capillary glucose levels of 160 mg/dl), peripheral neuropathy, multinodular goiter, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, peripheral vascular disease with recent angioplasty in the left leg, esophageal reflux, anemia of chronic inflammation, and chronic knee and wrist pain.
Medications
Medications included diltiazem, valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, insulin, acetaminophen and acetylsalicylic acid (81 MG).
Allergies
She was allergic to sulfa, gemfibrozil, and erythromycin, which had caused rashes.
Family History
There was a family history of diabetes mellitus and coronary artery disease.
Social History
She lived alone in New England and was retired.
Epidemiological History
She did not smoke, drink alcohol or use illicit drugs. She had not traveled recently.
Physical Examination
She was fully oriented. The temperature was 101.4°F (38.6°C), blood pressure 178/80 mm Hg, pulse 79 beats per minute, respirations 18 per minute and oxygen saturation 98% while breathing ambient air. There was a grade 1-2/6 systolic ejection murmur at the left lower sternal border, and heart sounds were otherwise normal. The abdomen was soft, minimally tender to palpation in left lower quadrant, without masses, and there were numerous scratches on her limbs and torso, without erythema or fluctuance. During the physical examination, she often repeated "I am tired" and "I can't remember". The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 12,500 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4500-11,000; leukocytes 85%, lymphocytes 11%, monocytes 4%), hematocrit 32.9% (ref 36.0-46.0); hemoglobin 11.8gm/dl (ref 12.0-16.0), platelets 142,000 per cubic millimeter (ref 150-400). Tests of coagulation were normal. The blood level of sodium was 133 mmol/L (ref 135-145), potassium 3.1 mmol/L (ref 3.4-4.8), carbon dioxide 22.4 mmol/L (ref 23.0-31.9), glucose 166mg/dl (ref 70-110) and urea nitrogen 26mg/dl (ref 8-25); the estimated glomerular filtration rate was 57mL/min/1.73m2 (ref abnormal if <60 mL/min/1.73m2). Levels of chloride, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, lactic acid, creatinine, were normal. Testing for antibodies to platelet factor 4 was negative. Urinalysis revealed 2+ glucose and protein, with few bacteria and squamous cells.
Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis with the administration of intravenous contrast was normal.
Culture of the stool grew normal enteric flora, without evidence of enteric pathogens. There was no evidence of Clostridium difficile toxin, protozoa, or helminth ova, and cultures of the blood and urine were sterile.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
She was admitted to the hospital. Intravenous fluids were administered for rehydration, and her other medications continued, with gradual improvement in most symptoms. Fatigue persisted, and on physical therapy evaluation, tremulousness and loss of balance were noted. On the fourth day, a neurological consultant obtained additional history that increased tremor was present for one week, and she had recent mid-to-lower back pain that did not radiate, without bowel or bladder incontinence, headache, neck stiffness or photophobia.
On examination, she was anxious, alert, oriented in full, and with preserved language. She registered 3 of 3 words and recalled 2 of 3 words with word choice. She exhibited spinal tenderness to palpation in mid lumbar region, diminished vibratory sense at the toes bilaterally, 3+ symmetric reflexes except for absent Achilles, a mild fine postural tremor, a positive Romberg test, and diminished vibratory sense at the toes.
On the fifth day, computed tomography (CT) of the head without the administration of contrast (Figure 1) revealed central volume loss and chronic microvascular changes, without evidence of acute disease. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbosacral spine with the administration of gadolinium showed focal T11-12 disc enhancement, thought to be related to degenerative joint disease, without abnormal enhancement. A lumbar puncture was performed; analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid revealed a glucose level of 99 mg/dl and total protein level 26 mg/dl, with 20 white cells per cubic millimeter (96% lymphocytes and 4% monocytes) and 4 red cells per cubic millimeter in Tube 1. Gram stain of the CSF showed very few mononuclear cells and no organisms.
Infectious disease consultation was obtained. Her relatives reported that her speech was slower and word-finding difficulties more severe than at baseline, and that she had been relatively confused on admission, with partial improvement since admission.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Testing of the CSF for West Nile virus (WNV) IgM and the West Nile virus plaque reduction neutralization test was positive, compatible with recent infection.
Testing for antibodies (IgM) to Eastern Equine encephalitis (EEE) was negative. Testing of the CSF for Herpes simplex virus (types 1 and 2) DNA and enterovirus RNA was negative, as was testing the blood for antibodies to Bartonella henselae and Bartonella quintana (IgG and IgM) and Treponema pallidum. Culture of the CSF was sterile. Stool specimens were negative for C. difficile toxin and viruses.
Treatment and Followup
Before the results of the testing were known, doxycycline and rifampin (14-day course) were administered empirically for treatment of possible cat scratch disease. The patient's symptoms improved, and she was discharged on the 9th day, to a short-term rehabilitation facility for physical and occupational therapy to improve her gait, balance and activities of daily living.
Results of the positive diagnostic test results were received three weeks after discharge.
Discussion
Among the possible diagnoses considered, Bartonella henselae meningoencephalitis was given strong consideration, as so-called “cat-scratch encephalopathy” was compatible with her exposure history, symptom time course, epidemiology, and laboratory findings. This entity is described as an encephalopathy of varying severity with paucicellular mononuclear pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid, with total WBC count generally less than 50 [Baylor P, et al. 2007; Pinto Jr VL, et al. 2008; Marra CM 1995]. Though rare, in patients with cat-scratch disease, encephalopathy is 6-fold more common in patients over 60 years of age than in younger patients, while lymphadenitis (which this patient did not have) is less common in elderly patients [Ronen B-A, et al. 2005]. However, her serological testing was negative for Bartonella exposure, which would be unusual given the duration of symptoms and time since the cat exposure.
While rabies is a consideration in a patient with encephalitis after this type of exposure to a wild animal, this is a rare entity in the US, and furthermore cats are an atypical vector. Additionally, she was appropriately vaccinated for this exposure, which decreases the likelihood of acquiring disease. Finally, her indolent course with improving encephalitis symptoms was incompatible with rabies encephalitis. Pasteurella multocida would be expected to cause a more fulminant illness. It is a gram-negative bacillus that can be transmitted by animal bites or scratches, typically causing localized inflammation with systemic toxicity and often high leukocytosis, and though disseminated infection can result in meningitis, this typically occurs in very sick patients, and more proximal to the animal exposure rather than the slow, indolent course this patient experienced [Oehler RL, et al. 2009; Green BT, et al. 2002]. While this patient lived in an endemic area for Lyme disease, she did not exhibit other signs of Lyme disease that typically precede neuroborreliosis. Eastern equine encephalitis (EEE) is clinically compatible with this patient’s presentation, and indeed there is extensive overlap between this flaviviral syndrome and that of the patient’s ultimate diagnosis, West Nile virus encephalitis. However, serologies for EEE were negative, while those for West Nile virus were positive. Listeria and herpes simplex virus infections were also considered; testing for these pathogens was negative.
West Nile virus is a mosquito-borne RNA virus of the Flavivirus genus, first described in the 1930s as a cause of an influenza-like febrile illness; by the 1950s, it was also recognized to cause meningoencephalitis in rare cases [Vaughn DW, et al. 2010]. It is one of the most widely distributed of the arthropod-borne viruses (arboviruses), found in Africa, southern Europe, the Middle East, Australia, and (more recently) the Americas, particularly North America. The virus exhibits an enzootic life cycle in birds and mosquitoes. Humans are exposed by the bite of an infected mosquito and along with other mammals (notably horses) are thought to be dead-end hosts, incapable of transmitting the disease due to low-level viremia insufficient to re-infect mosquitoes [Vaughn DW, et al. 2010]. Cases of human transmission are only reported in the context of blood transfusions or transplanted organs [Pealer LN, et al.2003; Ravindra KV, et al. 2004]. Given the mosquito vector, the disease exhibits a classic seasonality, peaking in the late summer and early fall. The emergence of this illness in North America is relatively new, with the first reports occurring in 1999 with 62 confirmed human cases in New York [Nash D, et al. 2001]. Following its introduction to North America, it rapidly spread across the continent, reaching a peak of 10,000 clinical cases with 286 cases of neurologic disease in 2003. This year (2012) has to date the highest incidence of cases of West Nile virus infection reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) since that peak in 2003, and the second-highest on record [CDC 2012]. However, serological surveys of blood donor samples suggest that the incidence of antibodies to West Nile virus is considerably higher than previously recognized, leading to the recognition that only approximately 20% of infected people develop symptoms, and only 1 in 150 develops central nervous system disease [Pealer LN, et al.2003; Ravindra KV, et al. 2004]. However, whether due to reporting bias or a shifting epidemic, fully half of the reported cases to the CDC this year are neuroinvasive disease [CDC 2012].
Clinically, West Nile virus infection can present anywhere from asymptomatic infection (the majority of cases), to the classic symptoms of an influenza-like illness with fever, often with some combination of nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, eye pain, and pharyngitis, as well as a rash in less than 50% of patients, to neuroinvasive disease such as meningitis and/or encephalitis [Zou S, et al. 2010]. It has an incubation period of between 2 and 14 days after exposure to an infected mosquito, though this may be longer in an immunocompromised host. Though the acute phase of illness is typically only 3-6 days, patients often report persistent symptoms, with up to half complaining of fatigue lasting more than a month [Petersen LR 2012]. Nervous system involvement may take the form of meningitis (more common in younger patients) or encephalitis (more common in the elderly). Risk factors for neuroinvasive disease include old age, alcohol use, and diabetes mellitus. Distinguishing features of West Nile encephalitis may include prominent extrapyramidal symptoms, or a poliomyelitis-like syndrome with acute flaccid paralysis that may occur before, during, or after the encephalitic phase of illness (Sejvar JJ, Leis AA, et al. 2003; Sejvar JJ, Haddad MB, et al. 2003]. Neuroinvasive disease often portends a protracted course of illness, with appreciable mortality (up to 15%) and, more strikingly, persistent neurological deficits reported in more than half of patients one year after symptom onset [Weiss D, et al. 2001; Loeb M, et al. 2008; Carson PJ, et al. 2006]. Fortunately, the patient described in this case report has enjoyed a full recovery, with no neurological complaints on follow-up with her primary care physician.
Diagnosis is most commonly achieved by serologic testing in a patient with an appropriate clinical syndrome in an endemic region at a plausible time of year. In addition to the clinical syndrome described above, laboratory studies can show normal or elevated total leukocyte counts. Cases of meningitis or meningoencephalitis typically show a lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (though neutrophilia has been described as well), often with elevated total protein and normal glucose [Tyler KL, et al. 2006]. The most appropriate specific diagnostic test is serum and CSF antibodies, both IgM and IgG, often detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). These typically seroconvert quite early in illness, with IgM appearing by 4 days and IgG by 8 days of illness. IgM is positive in nearly all patients by 10 days and may remain positive for up to 6 months or more [Busch MP, et al.2008; Roehrig JT, et al. 2003], and CSF IgM may be the first detectable antibody in patients with neuroinvasive disease. Acute illness can also be detected by nucleic acid amplification testing, which may be a useful adjunct in endemic areas where an IgM may reflect prior infection [Tilley PA, et al. 2006]. The plaque reduction neutralization test, which was done for our patient, is a more specific test for flavivirus antibodies and can be used to rule out false-positive ELISAs.
Treatment of West Nile virus infection is largely supportive, including seizure control, intubation, and monitoring and management of cerebral edema as appropriate for neuroinvasive disease. Several immunomodulatory treatment modalities are being explored, but none is yet supported by clinical trials. Case reports suggest possible benefit to interferon alpha, though causality is in question [Sejvar JJ, Haddad MB, et al. 2003; Kalil AC, et al. 2005]. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) is thought to be possibly helpful in immunosuppressed hosts [Planitzer CB, et al. 2007; Agrawal AG, Petersen LR. 2003], and further investigations of this therapy are underway at the time of this publication. Ribavirin is to be avoided because of evidence of possible harm in uncontrolled studies [Chowers MY, et al. 2001]. While vaccines have been approved for use in horses and are under investigation for possible use in humans, the mainstay of prevention at present is mosquito avoidance, both by individual practices and by public health measures. In addition, the US and Canada have adopted blood donor screening because of concerns about transmission of illness from asymptomatic viremic donors [Busch MP, et al. 2005].
Final Diagnosis
Meningoencephalitis caused by West Nile virus.
