History of Present Illness
A woman in her sixties with 5 months of weight loss, poor appetite, and increased abdominal girth was referred to gynecology after being found to have a large right adnexal mass (7x6x6 cm), masses in liver and peritoneum, cardiophrenic and retroperitoneal nodes, ascites and elevated CA-125. Gynecology requested a consultation for granulomatous inflammation noted from the peritoneal biopsy. GMS and AFB stains were negative. There was no evidence of malignancy. She also reports 7 months of intermittent dry cough. She reported no fever.
Past Medical History
1. Hypertension 2. Treated for latent tuberculosis infection with isoniazid about 30 years ago 3. Asthma
Medications
Lisinopril
Epidemiological History
1. Originally from Pacific islands 2. Moved to the US in early 2000’s 3. Spent a few months in Southern and Western states within the past 10 years
Physical Examination
Tmax: 37.5°C, BP: 146/96, HR: 68, RR: 109, O2 saturation: 97% on room air. Pleasant. In no distress. Not ill-appearing. Remainder of exam normal.
Studies
White blood cell count 9.6 (Eosinophil 3.3 (34%)). Hemoglobin 11.9. Platelets 395,000. Calcium 10.5. Parathyroid hormone 3 (nl 12-88). 1, 25-OH-Vitamin D 136 (nl 12-72). Rest of the chemistries were normal. Liver enzymes normal. CA-125 556 U/ml (normal < 35).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Negative studies: HIV screening, histoplasma urine antigen, serum aspergillus galactomannan, coccidioides antibodies, schistosomiasis antibody, strongyloides antibody, ANA, ANCA, stool ova and parasites, AFB blood culture, fungal blood culture, midnight blood smear for microfilariae. Positive studies: TB quantiferon, TB growth from sputum at 13 days (smear and PCR negative), TB growth from repeat peritoneal biopsy at 16 days. The TB isolate was pan-susceptible to rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol (RIPE) regimen. BAL aspergillus galactomannan 4.15 (normal <0.5).

Treatment and Followup
The patient finished 6 months of RIPE, then was switched to INH/RIF standard treatment, which she tolerated well. She gained 20 lbs and both CA-125 elevation and eosinophilia resolved. Subsequent CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis showed much decreased size of pulmonary, perihepatic, abdominal and pelvic masses.

Discussion
Before the final diagnosis, we considered tuberculosis, coccidioidomycosis, and lymphatic filariasis for her syndrome of infection with eosinophilia [1-7]. These are based on her epidemiological risk factors. We also considered non-infectious causes, such as autoimmune diseases, but the ANA and ANCA were negative and the patient did not have other symptoms associated with autoimmune disorders. We then discussed that TB can be associated with peripheral eosinophilia, although not as common as infections with helminth or fungi [2, 3]. The patient did spend some time in Southern and Western states. Coccidioidomycosis can be associated with peripheral eosinophilia [1], but peritoneal infection is very rare [4, 5], and most patients were reported with positive antibody by immunodiffusion. We were surprised to see the positive BAL aspergillus galactomannan, as the patient’s presentation was not consistent with aspergillus infection. After literature review, we found that histoplasmosis (present in the Pacific Islands but the true burden is unknown) can cross react with aspergillus galactomannan test and yield false positive results [8-10]. Pelvic histoplasmosis is rare in immunocompetent patients, but has been reported [11]. We excluded the possibility of disseminated histoplasmosis based on a strong alternative diagnosis, negative urine histoplasma antigen, and response to anti-TB treatment. Finally, very rarely lymphatic filariasis (endemic in the Philippines) can cause tropical pulmonary eosinophilia, lung lesions, and pelvic lesions [6, 7]. However, the patient had not returned to the Philippines for more than 10 years. It was thought unlikely to sustain a parasitic life cycle, and that the extent of the disease was difficult to be explained by filariasis. We then discussed the nonspecific association between CA-125 elevation and ovarian cancer, given that the patient was initially referred to a gynecologic oncologist [12, 13]. It is also interesting that one study reported potential clinical utility of CA-125 in monitoring treatment effect of pulmonary TB, especially in females, and those with more significant pulmonary involvement [14].
Final Diagnosis
Disseminated TB with pulmonary, hepatic, and peritoneal involvement
