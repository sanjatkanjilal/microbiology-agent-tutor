History of Present Illness
IDSA 2010 Pediatric Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A teenager presented with increasing anorexia, weight loss, cough and headache.
A teenaged boy was seen in an Emergency Department because of progressively increasing anorexia, fatigue and 43-pound weight loss (19.5 kilograms) during the previous 2 months. Two weeks before presentation, cough, post-tussive emesis, headache, and drenching night sweats developed, without documented fevers. He had occasionally taken acetaminophen for the symptoms.
During the previous week, discomfort occurred in the region of the lower ribs, posteriorly, on the left. His family noted that he was more pale and spent most of his time lying in bed. He had one episode of self-resolving epistaxis, and reported no dyspnea, phlegm production, or urinary or gastrointestinal complaints.
Past Medical History
At age 2, a peri-rectal abscess occurred, which healed appropriately after drainage and antibiotics. He also had mild, intermittent asthma.
Medications
Medications included acetaminophen every 1-2 days during this illness, and albuterol as needed.
Epidemiological History
He was Asian-American, lived in a large, multi-generational household, and attended high school in California. He reported no sexual activity or intravenous drug use. Approximately five months before presentation, he traveled to urban and rural Vietnam for 3 weeks. His younger brother had anemia of unclear etiology for which he took iron supplements. Relatives with whom he lived had recently had respiratory illnesses.
Physical Examination
On examination, he appeared pale and fatigued, lying in bed. The weight was 57 kg (124.4 pounds), temperature 102.5°F (39.2°C), the blood pressure was 115/77 mm Hg, pulse 122 beats per minute, respirations 22 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 99% while breathing room-air. There were occasional end-expiratory wheezes, moderate tenderness in the abdomen with deep palpation on the left laterally, without hepatosplenomegaly, and mildly tender inferior, posterior ribs on the left; the remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 12,500/mm³ (reference range 5,000-10,000) with 83% neutrophils, hemoglobin 8.1 g/dL, and platelets 505,000/mm³ (ref. 145,000-400,000). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 62 mm/hr (ref. 0-10), and the C–reactive protein (CRP) 8.4 mg/dl (ref. 0 - 0.9 mg/dl), total protein 10 gm/dL (ref. 6.0-8.0), and the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 1027 U/L (ref. 313-618). Other routine laboratory tests and a chest radiograph were normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen revealed a 6-7 cm heterogeneous mass in the upper pole of the left kidney, which did not appear calcified (Figure 1). The renal mass was associated with infiltrative changes in the surrounding fat and lymphadenopathy, and there was thickening of the diaphragm and spleen (Figure 2). A small, discrete lesion in the lower pole of the left kidney was also noted (Figure 3). Additionally, multiple pleural-based densities were detected.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A tuberculin-skin test, testing for antibodies to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and cultures of the blood and urine were negative; results of a urinalysis were not available. A percutaneous, ultrasound-guided biopsy of the left kidney mass was performed. Pathological examination of the tissue with hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) staining of the specimens revealed inflammatory cells characteristic of acute and chronic pyelonephritis, including infiltration of lipid-laden (foamy) macrophages throughout the renal parenchyma, including the glomerulus (Figure 3) and renal tubule (Figure 4), consistent with a diagnosis of xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis. Culture from renal tissue grew methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).

Treatment and Followup
After the pathology and culture results from the renal biopsy were known, vancomycin was started intravenously, with prompt defervescence and constitutional improvement. An inpatient voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) was unsuccessful as the introduction of the catheter was met with resistance and bleeding. After the fifth day of vancomycin therapy, ciprofloxacin, to which the organism was also sensitive, was administered for an additional 12 days intravenously. He was then discharged, and completed a total 6-week course of antibiotics with oral ciprofloxacin. Serial ultrasonographic examinations of the abdomen demonstrated gradual resolution of the pyelonephritis and decreasing size of his left kidney. At follow-up 1 month after completing his antibiotic course, he had regained 20 pounds (9.1 kilograms) and was asymptomatic.
Discussion
Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XPN) is a rare and poorly understood progressive, inflammatory disease of the kidney. Mainly seen in adult females over the age of 40, the condition has been described in children as young as 21 days-old, and recently has been increasingly reported in the pediatric population; a review from 2009 stated that there had been about 240 childhood cases reported in the literature [Gupta et al 2009]. Considered to be a morphological and clinical variant of chronic pyelonephritis, the hallmark pathological feature of XPN is diffuse renal parenchymal destruction and replacement with lipid-laden macrophages (foam cells).
Genitourinary (GU) tract obstruction - particularly by urolithiasis - along with urinary tract infection, are the 2 most common predisposing factors associated with xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis. Older pediatric reviews found 75% of XPN cases to be associated with calculi, but obstruction may also be due to congenital anomalies, tumors, and of a functional nature; in this case, though no obvious GU abnormalities or stones were detected, it may be noteworthy that the patient’s VCUG proved unsuccessful due to an inability to fully insert the catheter. Other risk factors for XPN, including diabetes, malnutrition, abnormal lipid metabolism, and immunologic disturbances, have been postulated but remain uncertain [Brown et al 1995]. The clinical features of XPN are inconsistent and generally non-specific, with two-thirds of cases having malaise, anorexia, fever, flank pain, or weight loss [Levy et al 1994]. Symptoms related to the urinary system, such as dysuria or hematuria, are infrequent. The duration of illness prior to diagnosis is often subacute to chronic, with 80% of patients being ill for greater than 1 month, and 40% for greater than 6 months. [Brown et al 1994]. Loin tenderness is a common physical exam finding, and occasionally a mass may be palpable. Anemia, leukocytosis, elevated inflammatory markers, and pyuria are common laboratory findings - but renal parameters are usually normal. Urine cultures may be positive in 50-70% of presentations; nevertheless, cases in which tissue culture is positive while the urine is found to be sterile are reported. Escherichia coli and Proteus species - often associated with staghorn calculi - form the bulk of recovered isolates, with other gram-negative pathogens and staphylococcal species also rarely being identified [Al-Hwiesh 2007]. Radiological imaging findings are variable, and may include an enlarged kidney with an expanding mass, raising concern for an oncologic etiology or tuberculosis. The diagnosis of XPN ultimately relies on renal biopsy: grossly the kidney is enlarged, with streaky, yellow granulomas and a thickened capsule, while microscopic histopathology demonstrates the defining dense infiltrate of foamy macrophages replacing normal parenchyma. Historically, XPN has been managed by total or partial nephrectomy, which is considered to be curative [Grainger et al 1982]. However, with the increasing availability of highly-sensitive radiologic techniques, and potential for percutaneous biopsy and non-operative diagnosis, some authors have advocated for attempts at medical management prior to approaching surgical resection in certain cases. For example, Brown et al. describe their experience with a 27-months-old boy, with focal upper pole XPN in an otherwise well-functioning kidney, who demonstrated excellent clinical and radiological response after 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics [Brown et al. 1996]. Similarly, a number of other pediatric case reports support that a course of parenteral followed by oral antibiotics may be successful in cases of focal XPN [Gupta et al. 2009].
Final Diagnosis
Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis, with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infection.
