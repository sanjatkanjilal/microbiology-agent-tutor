History of Present Illness
A woman in her 40s presented with six weeks of progressive exertional dyspnea, seven kilograms (about 15 pounds) of weight loss and subjective fevers. She denied any other symptoms including no joint or back pain or swelling, gastrointestinal upset or visual changes.
A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed mild tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension and an aortic valve vegetation. She was admitted for further evaluation.
Past Medical History
No recent hospitalisations, surgeries, antibiotics.
Medications
None.
Epidemiological History
This patient lived in rural Australia with her husband and dog. She had a 30-pack-year history of smoking and consumed approximately six standard drinks per day. She had never used recreational drugs nor travelled outside Australia or to the Northern Territory. She denied recent camping or caving, recent insect or animal bites or exposures.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well and had a blood pressure of 126/74, a regular heart rate of 82 and was saturating 98% on room air with 14 breaths per minute. On admission, she had a single fever to 38.5°C (101.3°F) but subsequently was afebrile. She had poor dentition and marked clubbing of her nails, but no Janeway lesions or Osler’s nodes. There was a single splinter haemorrhage on the third right toe. She had a waterhammer pulse, her heart sounds were dual with pansystolic murmur at the apex and diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge. Auscultation of the chest was clear. Her abdominal examination was unremarkable, with no palpable organomegaly. She had no peripheral oedema.
Studies
On admission, the patient was thrombocytopenic 98 x 10 9 /L (reference range 150-450), with haemoglobin within normal limits 117g/L (reference range 115-165), C-reactive protein 52 mg/L (reference range <3). She had normal liver enzymes and renal function. Five sets of blood cultures were all negative at five days incubation. A transoesophageal echocardiogram (Figure 1) demonstrated:
- Large vegetation on aortic valve with severe aortic regurgitation and moderate to severe mitral regurgitation secondary to AMVL prolapse
- Large pedunculated mass 36 x 0.7 mm attached to aortic valve. Multiple smaller masses or vegetation attached to all coronary cusps with destruction of aortic valve architecture

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
After 5 blood cultures were taken, when the large aortic valve vegetation was discovered, she was commenced on intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone and IV benzylpenicillin for culture negative endocarditis and transferred to a larger hospital for operative management.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Further workup revealed:
- HIV Antigen/Antibody and Treponema pallidum Antibody negative
- Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) negative, Hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb) negative, Hepatitis B core antibody (HBcAb) negative
- Hepatitis C Antibody screen negative
- Antinuclear antibody (ANA), extractable nuclear antibody (ENA), double stranded DNA (dsDNA), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-citrullinated protein (anti-CCP), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) antibodies all negative, C3, C4 within normal limits
- Negative cryoglobulins
- Anti-phospholipid antibodies negative
- Brucella, Bartonella, Coxiella, Mycoplasma, Legionella serologies negative
- Urine casts negative
- CT brain, chest, abdomen and pelvis: Wedge infarcts in kidneys bilaterally, no evidence of malignancy.
Treatment and Followup
The patient continued antibiotics and underwent aortic valve replacement 10 days after presentation. Intra-operative findings were as follows:
- Aortic valve almost completely destroyed with large vegetations on all leaflets - Ulcerated area of infection at septum under right coronary cusp - Anterior mitral valve leaflet prolapse with tethering of secondary chord and small vegetations on leaflet and chordae
Conventional bacterial culture was negative at five days. Histopathology of the aortic valve revealed “vegetations and valvular destruction, consistent with endocarditis. Both the aortic valve and mitral chordae also show numerous foamy histiocytes containing granular PAS-D-positive material of uncertain significance, and the possibility of Tropheryma whipplei endocarditis (Whipple disease) cannot be excluded.”
Considering these findings, the patient underwent small bowel biopsy to assess for classic Whipple’s disease, and the team organised 16S rRNA gene sequencing on valve culture. 16S rRNA gene sequencing on valvular tissue identified Tropheryma whipplei.
The patient received 2 weeks of IV ceftriaxone 2g daily, followed by oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole 160/800mg twice daily to complete a total of six months of antibiotic therapy. At four months follow up the patient remains clinically well with normal cardiovascular function. Duodenal biopsy subsequently returned negative for PAS-D staining and Tropheryma whipplei culture.
Discussion
The differential diagnosis for culture negative endocarditis is broad. In this immunocompetent, previously well patient with minimal antibiotic or healthcare exposure and no overseas travel, the main differentials are pathogens from environmental exposure such as Coxiella burnetti, Legionella, Mycoplasma, HACEK organisms given her poor dentition and less likely, Tropheryma whipplei. Fungal or mycobacterial pathogens are less likely given she is immunocompetent and does not have relevant exposures. Non-infective causes are possible, but she did not have any evidence of autoimmune disease or solid organ malignancy.
Tropheryma whipplei is an environmental, slow-growing gram-positive rod [1,2]. It can be found in soil and is thought to be common in the human gut, where it is taken up and replicates in macrophages. However, only a small percentage of carriers develop infection. Illness typically occurs in Caucasian people and is rare in Asian and African populations despite common carriage, which is thought to be due to HLA genotype and other genetic differences [2,3].
Whipple’s disease is rare, with prevalence thought to be three in one million based on duodenal biopsy prior to PCR. Since 1999, the organism has been more readily isolated in other specimens, and the incidence is now estimated to be one to six new cases per ten million worldwide [1]. Whipple’s disease typically presents in mid-life (age 48 to 54 years), with a male preponderance of 8:1, largely associated with occupations in farming, building or outdoor exposure, or exposure to animals and soil. In more recent studies, women now account for 25% of diagnoses.
There are 4 clinical syndromes [1,3]:
1. Classic Whipple’s disease which is characterized by diarrhoea, weight loss and joint pain
2. Localized chronic infection
3. Acute infection, often diarrhoeal disease or pneumonia
4. Asymptomatic carriage
Localised chronic disease including endocarditis, osteoarticular disease and encephalitis, has been increasingly recognized since increased availability of polymerase chain reaction (PCR). These patients do not develop systemic involvement, so as with our case, stool, saliva specimens and duodenal biopsy are often negative [4]. Whipple’s endocarditis is reported to contribute to 1.9 to 6.3% of culture negative infective endocarditis cases. It involves intracellular infection of the valves as opposed to biofilm-like superficial colonization [5]. Diagnosis is most commonly by PCR of valvular tissue [6]. As in this case, Periodic acid-Schiff (PASD) stain, used to detect polysaccharides (glycogen) and mucosubstances (glycoproteins, glycolipids, mucin) can be positive (Figures 2,3). However, it is non-specific for Whipple’s, as it can also be positive in infections with fungi, mycosis fungoides, and Mycobacterium avium [1, 5].
Optimal treatment is not known, but recommendations include initial intravenous phase with ceftriaxone then switch to oral cotrimoxazole and sulfamethoxazole or doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine [6].
