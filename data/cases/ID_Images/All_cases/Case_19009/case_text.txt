History of Present Illness
A girl in her late teens with systemic lupus erythematosus and celiac disease presented during the winter season with ten days of dry cough. Over the few days before admission, her cough worsened and became productive with yellow, thick sputum. She denied having fevers, night sweats, hemoptysis, runny nose, chest pain or shortness of breath. She lost 40 lbs (18 kg) of weight in three months and reported a few episodes of diarrhea over the last few weeks. Her family attributed her weight loss to depression. She denied sick contacts, including anyone with tuberculosis.
Past Medical History
-Systemic lupus erythematous (SLE)
-Depression.
Medications
-Hydroxychloroquine
-Fluoxetine
-Received one dose of methylprednisolone (80mg) for SLE flare 2 weeks ago.
Epidemiological History
She is a college student. She drinks city water and has not had recent contacts with hot tubs, swimming pools or gardening. She has not been homeless or incarcerated and has not traveled outside the US. She smokes a half pack of tobacco per day. She denied illicit drug use.
Physical Examination
Vital signs: Blood pressure 98/56mmHg, pulse 110 beats per minute, temperature 99.8° F (37.7°C), and respirations 20 breaths per minute. Oxygen saturation: 95% on room air. Patient appeared thin with muscle wasting. No respiratory distress. She had a flat affect. There were coarse breath sounds bilaterally. Heart sounds were dual with no murmurs. Her abdomen was soft and non-tender with no hepatosplenomegaly. There was no lymphadenopathy. Her ankles and wrists were tender to palpation with no appreciable synovitis. The rest of examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 6,200/μL (ref 3.7 - 11 K/μL) with lymphopenia of 0.12 K/μL (ref 1 - 4 K/μL) and a left shift, hemoglobin 8.9 g/dL (ref 11.5 - 15.5 g/dL) and a platelet count 218,000/μL (ref 150 - 400 K/μL). Serum chemistry showed creatinine 0.43 mg/dL (ref 0.58 - 0.96 mg/dL), sodium 133 (136 - 144 mmol/L), ALT 9 (ref 7 - 38 U/L), AST 57 (ref 13 - 35 U/L), alkaline phosphatase 173 (ref 34 - 123 U/L), albumin 2.1 (ref 3.9 - 4.9 g/dL). Rest of chemistry was normal. A chest radiograph showed prominent opacity in the right paratracheal region (Figure 1).
A chest computed tomography (CT) with contrast (Figure 2a and 2b) showed a 4 cm focal consolidative process in the medial right upper lobe with a large central area of necrosis and early cavitation that extended from the right lung apex to anterior of the right hilar region. There was also evidence of patchy nodular opacities surrounding the cavitary lesion. There were a few small mediastinal and hilar nodes. CT abdomen/pelvis showed evidence for hepatic steatosis.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Sputum stain/culture studies for bacterial, mycobacterial and fungi were ordered however the patient was not able to produce good sputum samples. Legionella urine antigen was negative. No antibiotics were started as she remained stable and the pulmonary team was consulted for a bronchoscopy. C-reactive protein 12.9 (<0.9 mg/dL), erythrocyte sedimentation rate 66 (0 - 20 mm/hr), C4 level 10 (13 - 46 mg/dL), C3 level 41 (86 - 166 mg/dL), dsDNA antibodies 859 IU/ml (<30 IU/ml), ANCA negative, proteinase-3 antibody negative, and myeloperoxidase antibody negative. Blood cultures were negative. QuantiFERON - TB Gold PLUS was indeterminate (and there was no prior TB screening documented). 4th generation HIV test was non-reactive. Serum galactomannan was negative. Serum complement fixation antibodies were negative for Aspergillus, Histoplasma and Coccidioides, but positive for Blastomyces at 1:16.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A bronchoscopy was performed but there were no endobronchial lesions or secretions. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) were done. Gram stain and culture were negative on the BAL but showed moderate neutrophils. Pneumocystis jirovecii polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from BAL was negative. Legionella pneumophila PCR from BAL came back positive.
Treatment and Followup
The patient was started on oral levofloxacin 750 mg once per day with improvement of symptoms. Oral prednisone (10mg daily) was started for concomitant SLE flare.
Legionella culture from BAL were negative. Histoplasma urine antigen and serum blastomyces immunodiffusion antibody were negative. BAL and TBNA studies for AFB stain/culture, Nocardia, fungal stain/culture, and cytology were negative. BAL galactomannan was negative. Histopathology showed evidence of acute inflammation and granular proteinaceous material. Tissue acid-fast bacilli (AFB) stains, Grocott's methenamine silver (GMS) stain and gram stains were negative.
Patient was discharged with a plan to complete additional 14 days of levofloxacin. She was seen in infectious disease clinic one month later and reported resolution of her respiratory symptoms. A repeat CT chest (Figures 3 a and 3b) performed 1 month after completion of treatment showed complete resolution of right upper lobe necrotizing pneumonia with a residual 2.0 x 2.5 cm thin-walled cystic space.

Discussion
Legionella cavitary pneumonia is an atypical presentation of Legionnaire’s disease and more commonly described in patients with impaired cellular immunity secondary to corticosteroids (particularly high-dose steroid therapy), immunosuppressive therapy, organ transplant, and HIV infection.[1,2,3,4,5].
Cavitation has been associated with L pneumophila serotypes 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8 as well as other Legionella species (L. micdadei, L. bozemanae, L. dumoffii, and L. longbeachae).6,7 L. pneumophila serotype 1 is the main agent of Legionnaire’s disease. Infections by L pneumophila non-serotype 1 and non-pneumophila Legionella species occur in severely immunocompromised hosts.[4,6,7,8,9]
Our patient presented in winter and was not receiving an intensive immunosuppressive therapy when compared with two prior cases of SLE patients complicated with Legionella pulmonary abscess.[10,11,12] However, her lupus was active and complicated with lymphopenia which may have predisposed her for this severe presentation.
Legionella urinary antigen and culture were negative in our patient. In some settings, Legionella PCR is becoming a preferred test for the diagnosis of Legionnaires' disease due to its rapid turn-around time and better performance regardless of Legionella species and serotypes.[13] Our institution’s laboratory developed a Legionella PCR which detects different serotypes of L. pneumophila by targeting the mip (macrophage infectivity potentiator) gene and it has not shown cross-reactivity in our validation studies with other non- pneumophila species.[14]
Other etiologies for cavitary pneumonia include mycobacteria, fungi, nocardia, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella, anaerobes, cancer and vasculitis.[9] The positive Blastomyces serology was most likely a false positive result. TBNA initially showed possible hyphae however fungal stain/cultures were negative suggesting possible contamination.
Treatment of Legionnaires' disease in immunocompromised patients is longer (14 to 21 days) than for many other causes of pneumonia due to risk for both severe infection and relapse.[6] Our patient had rapid resolution of symptoms and marked radiological improvement after treatment with Legionella-specific therapy. This case highlights the importance of having a high suspicion for Legionella infection in the differential diagnosis of cavitary pneumonia in immunocompromised patients to assure prompt diagnostic work-up and treatment.
Final Diagnosis
Legionella pneumophila cavitary pneumonia
