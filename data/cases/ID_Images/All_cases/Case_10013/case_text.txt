History of Present Illness
2010 IDSA Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A man presented with rash and confusion.
A man in his sixties was well until the morning of admission when he awoke feeling lethargic. Over the course of the day, his hands and feet grew cold and numb; he had multiple dark bowel movements, and developed a rash that spread to his thighs, arms, nose, ears, and scalp. The rash was not pruritic or painful. His wife noticed that he became “incoherent” and his nose became dark red. She called emergency medical services, and he was transported to an emergency department.
Past Medical History
He had had bacterial pericarditis as a teenager.
Medications
None.
Epidemiological History
The patient lived with his wife in Northern California, in a rural community, and worked in a business occupation. He smoked cigarettes for 30 years. He drank alcohol, and had a history of occasionally “blacking out”. He reported no use of illicit drugs.
The day before admission, he had used a “weed-whacker” in his yard. He owned horses, which he often rode and fed, and several dogs. His dogs were in good health and were known to eat horse manure.
Three days before admission, he had been bitten on his right hand by one of the dogs while breaking up fight among the dogs. He reported no insect bites or mountain lion exposure. He regularly drank well water and, four days prior to admission, he had eaten grilled medium-rare hamburgers.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared uncomfortable. His temperature was 38.7°C (101.7°F), blood pressure 115/78 mm Hg, pulse 110 beats per minute, respirations 24 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 99% while on supplemental oxygen (6 liters by nasal cannula). Patches of purpura were confluent on his scalp and bilateral upper pinnae, and were not palpable. Reticular lesions were present on the lower abdomen, back, penis, scrotum, and upper thighs (Figure 1). Three indurated puncture wounds (2-3 mm in diameter) were on the dorsal aspect of his right hand, without swelling, discharge, or surrounding erythema. The nose was markedly edematous and cyanotic (Figure 2), and skin lesions were diffusely present on the scalp (Figure 3). Mucous membranes were dry without mucosal lesions. The hands and feet were cyanotic (Figure 4) and cold, with decreased sensation. The strength was normal. The dorsal pedis pulses were normal.

Studies
The white cell count was 21,800 cells per microliter (µl) (reference range 4100-10,900), with 94.9% neutrophils (ref 35-80), hematocrit 35.9% (ref 40-52), platelet count 34,000 per µl (ref 140,000-450,000), INR 1.5 (ref 0.9-1.2), and partial thromboplastin time (PTT) 44.0 seconds (ref 18-28). The serum level of potassium was 5.2 mEq/L (ref 3.6-5.0), urea nitrogen 50 mg/dL (ref 7-21), creatinine 3.47 mg/dL (ref 0.5-1.4), alanine aminotransferase 356 U/L (ref 7-56), aspartate aminotransferase 959 U/L (ref 38-126), alkaline phosphatase 50 U/L, total bilirubin 1.7 mg/dL (ref 0.2-1.3), fibrinogen 283 mg/dL (ref 170-420 mg/dL), lactate dehydrogenase 1883 U/L (ref 50-150), creatinine kinase 45,000 ng/ml (ref 38-120), and troponin I 1.18 ng/mL (ref <0.4). A troponin I level was repeated, and measured greater than 22 ng/ml. Testing for antibodies to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) was negative. Urinalysis showed muddy brown casts, but no dysmorphic red blood cells or red cell casts.
The electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia, right axis deviation, and left ventricular hypertrophy with repolarization abnormalities. A chest radiograph showed no evidence of infiltrate or volume overload.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit and intravenous fluids, ceftriaxone and vancomycin were administered. Continuous renal replacement therapy was begun. A transthoracic echocardiogram showed global hypokinesis and reduced left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 35%. There was no evidence of vegetations or thrombus. Abdominal ultrasonography showed a normal liver and spleen, and diffusely echogenic kidneys, without evidence of hydronephrosis. Computed tomography (CT) of the brain demonstrated a small subdural hemorrhage.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
After 4 days incubation, cultures of the blood cultures grew anaerobic gram-negative rods, which were identified as Capnocytophaga canimorsus.
Treatment and Followup
The patient slowly improved over the next 10 days, with gradual resolution of the purpuric rash and improvement of organ function. Peripheral cyanosis and dry necrosis of his upper and lower extremities were monitored for potential amputation.
The initial antibiotic regimen was narrowed to ceftriaxone after the gram-negative rod was isolated from blood cultures. The patient improved clinically and was discharged to a skilled nursing facility for rehabilitation and wound care. He remained on hemodialysis at the time of discharge.
Discussion
According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), approximately 4 million dog bites occur annually in the United States. Of these, 300,000 people seek care in the emergency room, and there are 13,000 hospitalizations and 20 deaths annually. A study of wound culture data from patients seeking care in the emergency room found that both dog and cat bites were often polymicrobial, with both aerobic and anaerobic organisms recovered from cultures. Pasteurella species are most commonly found in wound cultures, followed by streptococci, staphylococci, Moraxella, Corynebacterium and Neisseria species. The most frequently occurring anaerobic organisms cultured from these wounds include Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, Porphyromonas, and Prevotella [Talan et al 1999].
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a facultative, fastidious gram-negative bacillus found in the mouth flora of dogs, cats, and humans. Its name is derived from the Greek for carbon-dioxide loving (capnocytophaga) and dog bite (canimorsus).
C. canimorsus is often mistaken for other species of gram-negative bacilli. It may take days to identify this long, thin, slow-growing bacteria, and cultures may require at least 14 days incubation. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques are an option as well, though these are currently restricted to research laboratories.
Since this bacteria was first described in the literature more than 30 years ago, more than 100 cases of C. canimorsus infection have been described, with a mortality rate of about 30% [Lion et al 1996].
C. canimorsus infection occurs more frequently in males than females. Many patient are between age 50 to 70-years old. Risk factors include alcohol abuse, asplenia, immunosuppression, and corticosteroid treatment. However, in a case series of 56 patients in California, only 10% of patients with C. canimorsus sepsis were asplenic, and none had reported alcohol abuse in their medical charts [Janda et al. 2006]. In the same series, the median time from dog bite to onset of symptoms was 3 days. Eighty-five percent of patients presented with fever, while 32% had sepsis and 13% had disseminated intravascular coagulopathy or septic shock. C. canimorsus sepsis can present with rash, cellulitis, arthritis, gangrene, meningitis or endocarditis [Janda et al 2006].
Final Diagnosis
Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection.
