History of Present Illness
A man in his fifties with a history of deceased-donor renal transplant for diabetic and hypertensive nephropathy presented with two weeks of progressive shortness of breath, fatigue, and bilateral lower extremity swelling. The patient’s renal transplant was two years prior to his presentation. He was originally from Western Africa and had moved to the U.S. over a decade ago.
Past Medical History
Primary hypertension
Coronary artery disease status post percutaneous coronary intervention
Insulin dependent diabetes mellitus type II
Medications
Aspirin, Carvedilol, Mycophenolate mofetil (360 mg twice daily), Tacrolimus (2 mg twice daily), Prednisone (5 mg daily), Nifedipine, Simvastatin.
Epidemiological History
Non-smoker. He was previously a white-collar worker.
Physical Examination
Heart Rate: 76 beats per minute and regular, Respiratory Rate: 20 breaths per minute, Temperature 98.6°F (37°C), Blood Pressure 145/95 mmHg, Oxygen Saturation: 100% on room air. He was not distressed and spoke in full sentences. There was no jugular venous distension, murmurs or gallop present. He had good air entry bilaterally with no wheezing or crackles. There was mild 1+ pitting edema in his lower limbs. There was no rash or lymphadenopathy.
Studies
There was a small ill-defined nodule in the right mid-lung and a 3cm round mass in the left perihilar region on Chest X-Ray (Figure 1). CT chest without contrast revealed multiple bilateral round nodules involving all lobes some with central cavitation, with the largest in the left hilum, measuring 3.1 x 2.9 x 1.8 cm (Figures 2a and b). A lytic lesion was noted in the L1 vertebral body.
Figure 1. Chest X-Ray showed a 3cm mass near the left hilum and an ill-defined opacity in the right mid lung
Figure 2. Chest CT showed multiple bilateral nodular opacities some with cavitation. The largest nodule was seen in the left hilum (arrow), measuring 3.1 x 2.9 x 1.8 cm.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Bronchoscopy with fine needle aspiration biopsy of the left upper pulmonary node was performed. Thick-walled, budding yeast were observed upon microscopic examination (Figure 3 and 4) and Histoplasma capsulatum var. duboisii was recovered from the tissue culture (Figure 5). His Histoplasma urine antigen was weakly positive. Cryptococcal antigen was negative in both serum and CSF; Blastomyces and Coccidiodes serum antibodies were negative; (1,3)-beta-D-glucan and Aspergillus galactomannan serum markers were undetectable.

Figure 4. Direct tissue-KOH preparation demonstrating a thick-walled yeast with a diameter of 11.43 µm
Figure 5. GMS Stain from culture plates of left hilar node demonstrating thick walled conidia resembling Histoplasma var. duboisii
Treatment and Followup
He was started on oral itraconazole 200 mg twice daily and referred to Infectious Diseases clinic for follow-up. Due to a lack of clinical and radiological response with standard itraconazole therapy, he was switched to posaconazole 300 mg daily. His respiratory symptoms improved and a repeat Chest CT after 4 months showed a decrease size of the left peri-hilar mass and left upper consolidations (Figure 6). Figure 6. Discussion
The clinical presentation of Histoplasma capsulatum var. duboisii exhibits some differences from that caused by H. capsulatum var. capsulatum. African histoplasmosis usually involves the skin, subcutaneous tissues, lymph nodes and bones unlike classical histoplasmosis, which generally involves lungs and mucosa and less commonly cutaneous or bone lesions. The predilection of duboisii histoplasmosis for the bone, particularly the elements of marrow is characteristic of the disease [1]. Two forms of African histoplasmosis are typically described as a localized chronic form that involves skin, subcutaneous tissue, bones, or lymph nodes; and a disseminated rapidly evolving form, which can also involve the spleen, liver, lungs, and abdominal viscera through hematogenous invasion [2]. African histoplasmosis is not commonly reported in immunosuppressed hosts, however reports in HIV-infected individuals are emerging [3-4]. We did not find any previous reports of African histoplasmosis in solid organ recipients.
Fungal culture remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of histoplasmosis [5]. Differentiating this organism from Cryptococcus is not difficult based on morphology and antigen testing. The cryptococcal encapsulated yeast size diameter is about 5 to 10 µm, and is usually surrounded by empty spaces that reflect the capsule while the yeast form of Histoplasma capsulatum var. duboisii is typically larger (diameter up to 15 µm). Blastomyces dermatididis is similar in size to H. capsulatum var. duboisii and both exhibit a thick cell wall. However, the diagnosis of blastomycosis requires identification of broad-based buds and/or multiple nuclei compared to Histoplasma capsulatum var. duboisii which has a narrow-based bud and is uni-nucleated [6]. Histoplasma var. duboisii is also unique in that in vivo it appears similar to B. dermatitidis but in vitro it grows with mycelia similar to H. capsulatum with large, thick-walled round tuberculate macroconidia. Histoplasma antigen detection in serum and urine is a sensitive test but has been developed only for the variety capsulatum, and is not validated for species duboisii [7]. PCR assays have been developed but are not yet routinely used [8].
The treatment of moderate pulmonary histoplasmosis as per the IDSA 2007 clinical practice guidelines is with oral itraconazole; in case of disseminated disease, amphotericin followed by itraconazole is the recommended regimen [9]. Posaconazole has been successfully used as salvage therapy in a pediatric patient with disseminated African histoplasmosis [10,11].
Final Diagnosis
Pulmonary histoplasmosis caused by Histoplasma var. duboisii, in a renal transplant patient.
