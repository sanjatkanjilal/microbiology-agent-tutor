History of Present Illness
2016 IDWeek Fellows' Day Case Presentation: A man in his thirties was admitted with a painful mass in the right lower abdominal quadrant, just above the inguinal ligament, of two days' duration. A diagnosis of necrotizing lymphadenitis was made, and the lesion was debrided emergently in the operating room. An infectious disease consultation was requested for antibiotic recommendations.
On examination, the patient was found to have an incidental mass in right axilla and pruritic rash involving his chest, back and extremities. The rash had reportedly worsened during the previous nine months. He reported suffering from this condition intermittently since age 15. When previously treated with antimycotic agents and topical steroids, the skin lesions would improve but the rash relapsed after cessation of treatment. He had never been given a specific diagnosis.
Past Medical History
The patient had had asthma during childhood and had had one episode of bronchitis/pneumonia between 10 months and 18 months of age. He reported retention of primary dentition until age fifteen, recurrent skin rashes and onycholysis of nails. A left groin abscess had been drained a few years earlier, without complication.
Medications
He was on no medications.
Family History
There was no history of consanguinity or reported cancers. His mother had had similar recurrent rashes on her feet and anterior thighs since childhood, which also improved with antifungal treatment. His pre-teen son had a similar recurrent pruritic rash and reported retention of primary dentition.
Social History
He lived in Mississippi and worked in construction. He did not use intravenous drugs or tobacco, and had no history of alcohol abuse. He was of Irish and French Creole (Native-American and French) ancestry.
Physical Examination
On examination, there was a markedly raised papillomaform rash on the upper chest, trunk, and proximal upper extremities and right axilla, which was erythematous and tender with areas of lichenification, scaling and weeping.The axillary mass was exophytic, erythematous and friable (Figures 1 and 2). He also had involvement of the back with thick scaling and crusting and a few scaly papules (one-centimeter in diameter) scattered over both legs, onycholysis of all nails of the left hand and left great toe, a well-healed scar in the left groin from the previous incision and drainage, and evidence of a surgical debridement procedure in the right lower quadrant of the abdomen.

Studies
The white blood cell count was 29,000 per microliter on admission, with 84% granulocytes, 11% lymphocytes, 1% monocytes, 1% eosinophils, and 3% band forms. Toxic granulations were present. Testing for HIV and a viral hepatitis panel were negative. Results of other routine laboratory were normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A skin biopsy was performed and cultures obtained.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stains of punch biopsies from the chest, right axilla and left thigh showed pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia of the epidermis with superficial and deep chronic inflammatory infiltrate. Fungal hyphae and round spore-like elements were present within the stratum corneum, epidermis and were noted to be invading the full thickness of the dermis (Figures 3 and 4). The Gomori methanamine silver (GMS) stain revealed numerous pleomorphic fungal hyphae (Figures 5 and 6).
The specimens were inoculated onto Sabouraud’s dextrose agar (SDA) and incubated. After approximately 14 days, ivory white colonies with a cottony surface were observed. Inspection of the reverse side of the culture plate showed that the colonies produced a deep red pigment characteristic of Trichophyton rubrum.
Confirmation of this identification was performed by microscopic examination of the stained preparation as follows:
-Dermatophytic growth was examined weekly via touch prep and stained with Lacotphenol Cotton Blue
- Isolates were observed with a light microscope under low-power (10 x) and high-power (40 x) lens.
-Fungal identification was based on features of hyphae organization. Microscopy revealed small tear or drop-shaped microconidia arranged along the sides of narrow septate hyphae. These findings are characteristic of Trichophyton rubrum.
The bacterial cultures grew Staphylococcus aureus, Streptoccocus pyogenes and Prevotella species within 48 hours. Viral culture of the tissue was negative.
Genetic testing for immunodeficiencies were sent to identify potential mutations or variants of genes associated with chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC), hyper-immunoglobulin E syndrome (HIES), and caspase recruitment domain-containing protein (CARD9). This included the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) deficiency, dedicator of cytokinesis 8 deficiency (DOCK8), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and caspase recruitment domain-containing protein (CARD 9) genetic testing. Results revealed compound heterozygous mutations in CARD9 through next generation sequencing. The remainder of testing was negative.

Treatment and Followup
The patient received antibiotics for treatment of his necrotizing lymphadenitis and was begun on oral fluconazole. Improvement began within a few days of anti-fungal treatment. After three weeks of therapy the patient continued to show reduction in size of the skin lesions and decreased hypertrophy of the skin lesions (Figure 7). After 8 weeks of oral anti-fungal therapy, the fungating axillary mass had decreased to nearly 25% of its original size; at 12 months, the mass had completely resolved (Figure 8). Additional therapy was extended for a minimum of three months.

Discussion
This case has clinical features suggestive of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC) including chronically recurring skin lesions that may present as plaques and verrucous growths and onycholysis, all of which responded to anti-fungal therapy at least temporarily [Sandhu, 2016; Kirkpatrick, 1994]. Invasion of the skin by Candida species can cause scaling, hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, and dyskeratosis but usually remains in the superficial layers of skin and does not pass the stratum corneum. In patients with CMC, there is a gain of function STAT1 gene mutation which leads to deficient Th17 and Th1 immune response and increased invasive fungal infections [Van de Veerdonk et al., 2011]. CMC is an inherited disorder which can result in disseminated infection affecting internal organs and the central nervous system may occur. However, this patient’s histopathology and culture showed that the infection was due to a filamentous organism and cultures ultimately confirmed the diagnosis of invasive Trichophyton rubrum rather than Candida species.
Invasive Trichophyton species infections are rare but are well documented in the literature though most cases have been described in immunosuppressed patients [Blake et al., 1991; Lanternier et al., 2013; Tejasvi et al., 2005; Nir-Paz et al., 2003]. This patient had no clear evidence for generalized immunosuppressive disease. The patient’s complement levels were normal and vaccine titers for both pneumococcal disease and tetanus were determined to be protective. However, IgE levels were found to be elevated to over 3000 IU/ml. A primary immunodeficiency associated with recurrent fungal infections is hyper-immunoglobulin E syndrome (HIES) commonly referred to as Job’s syndrome
Elevated IgE levels can be found in a variety of conditions including HIES which is characterized by a dysfunction in immunity, recurrent dermatitis, cyst-forming pneumonia, elevated IgE levels, retained primary dentition, connective tissue and musculoskeletal abnormalities [Holland et al., 2007; Grimbacher et al., 2005]. The genetic abnormalities associated with this syndrome are autosomal recessive mutations in the DOCK8 gene and an autosomal dominant mutation in the STAT3 gene [Holland et al., 2007]. The infectious diseases triad associated with STAT3 deficiency consists of recurrent staphylococcal skin infections, recurrent sinopulmonary infections and chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, though there are phenotypic variants [Grimbacher et al., 2005; Alves de Medeiros et al., 2016]. The DOCK8 mutation results in a milder form of disease.
The use of the clinical scoring system using both immunologic and non-immunologic features helps create an index of suspicion for HIES. This patient has clinical characteristics consistent with HIES including an elevated IgE level, retention of primary teeth, three to four skin abscesses in his lifetime, one other serious infection, the highest eosinophilia of greater than 800, and presence of onchomycosis. Dermatophyte infections are rare in HIES but there is at least one case described in the literature [Chandesris et al., 2012]. In that case the infection was not extensive as in our patient’s [Capucine Picard, person communication, March 29, 2016]. Our patient’s features give him a National Institutes of Health-HIES score [Grimbacher et al., 2005] of 34 which falls into the indeterminate category.
Elevated IgE levels can also be observed in immunocompetent patients with severe local dermatophyte infections in the absence of HIES [Grimbacher et al., 2005; Alves de Medeiros et al., 2016]. Cure of infection in these cases usually results in normalization of these findings. In our patient, the IgE levels decreased only slightly from 3715IU/ml to 2773 IU/ml after treatment even though his post-treatment biopsy revealed no evidence of recurrent infection in the tissue. This suggests that our patient has intrinsic hyper IgE levels rather than simply reactive hyper IgE elevations.
Recently descriptions of deep dermatophyte infections in patients of North African descent sometimes involving the brain and internal organs led to the discovery of a genetic mutation known as autosomal recessive caspase recruitment domain-containing protein (CARD9) deficiency [Lanternier et al., 2013]. CARD9 is an adaptor protein in the signaling pathway for fungal recognition and immunity [Lanternier et al., 2013]. Deficiency of CARD9 results in a selective defect in host defense resulting in impaired phagocytosis and allowing for invasive fungal infection. Other clinical manifestations of CARD9 include invasive infections of the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract but these are rare [Lanternier et al., 2015]. Our patient does not have North African ancestry. However, a few similar cases of deep dermatophytoses in patients not known to be immunosuppressed have been reported in the United States so it is possible that rare CARD9 mutations may occur in other populations [Chastain et al., 2001]. Preliminary genetic analyses of the CARD9 gene for this patient revealed that he is compound heterozygous for CARD9.
Features of Trichophyton species which may contribute to pathogenicity in these syndromes include the production of haemolysins, manan glycoprotein and dermatophyte derived inhibitor factors not yet identified which suppress human immune responses [Tejasvi et al., 2005; Nir-Paz et al., 2003; Ahmed, 1982; Jones et al., 1974]. Some of the extracellular enzymes such as keratinase allow for diffusion through the cornified layer of skin which allows for deeper infection. The release of chemotactic factors by dermatophytes produce irritation and an allergic contact dermatitis [Lanternier et al., 2013; Tejasvi et al., 2005]. This type of reaction manifests as an erythematous, pruritic rash that can be misclassified as eczema and will often initially respond to topical corticosteroids.
This patient responded very well to prolonged fluconazole treatment and likely will require lifelong suppressive anti-fungal therapy [Nir-Paz et al., 2003]. At the time of writing this paper, the patient continues to be evaluated by the National Institutes for Health.
In summary, this patient has a chronic and recurrent Trichophyton rubrum deep invasive dermatophytosis, which is well described among North African patients with homozygous recessive CARD9 deficiency. This patient is the first to be reported with T. rubrum invasive infection associated with compound heterozygous CARD9 mutations. Of considerable interest is the fact that our patient has some clinical characteristics of HIES including a history of retention of primary dentition and recurrent skin infections. Genetic testing for HIES associated gene variants is still pending.
Final Diagnosis
Chronic and recurrent Trichophyton rubrum deep invasive dermatophytosis secondary to compound heterozygous CARD9 deficiency.
