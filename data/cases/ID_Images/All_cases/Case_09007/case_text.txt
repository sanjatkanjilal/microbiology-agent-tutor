History of Present Illness
2009 July Featured Case and Medical Student Curriculum Case Summary: A 16-month-old toddler developed acute respiratory distress.
A 16-month-old toddler had been well until approximately 2 days before admission when clear rhinorrhea developed, and occurred intermittently thereafter, without fevers or other symptoms. On the morning of admission, ibuprofen was administered for presumed teething pain. During the late morning, the patient's mother noted increasing irritability and work of breathing, mild drowsiness, and a hoarse cry, without fever, drooling, or inability to drink.
In the afternoon, the child was brought to the pediatrician's office. On examination, there was respiratory distress, with stridorous breath sounds, and no meningismus. Dexamethasone, 0.6 milligrams per kilogram weight, was administered intramuscularly, emergently. The patient was transferred to the emergency department of a nearby hospital, and vomited once en route.
Past Medical History
The patient was the product of a full-term gestation, and an uncomplicated delivery. Episodes of ear infections requiring antibiotic treatment had occurred four times in the previous 5 months, and ear tubes were scheduled to be inserted in the upcoming weeks. There was no history of suppurative complications, sinopulmonary infections, headaches, visual or hearing deficits, chest or abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, dysuria, myalgias, arthralgia, or skin rashes.
Medications
All recommended childhood immunizations had been administered through age 14 months; doses of Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine had been given at ages 2, 4, and 6 months on schedule; however the fourth dose had been withheld due to the national shortage and prevailing guidelines at that time. As noted above, ibuprofen for teething discomfort was given the morning of admission.
Allergies
There were no known allergies.
Family History
There was no family history of immunodeficiency.
Epidemiological History
The patient lived with an older sibling and parents, and did not attend daycare. Diet included breast milk and table foods.
Physical Examination
At the hospital, the temperature was 101.3°F (38.5°C), with stridorous breathing. The weight was 8.9 kg. The pharynx was not exudative, and no foreign body was seen.
Studies
The white cell count was 13,100 cells per cubic millimeter (reference range 6,000-17,500), with 87% polymorphonuclear leukocytes (ref. range 25-49%), lymphocytes 9% (ref. range 60-67%), and monocytes 4% (ref. range 4-11%). The hematocrit was 30.7% (ref. range 33.0-39.0%) and hemoglobin 10.3 gm/dl (ref. range 10.5-13.5).
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Racemic epinephrine by nebulizer was administered. A specimen of the blood was cultured, followed by the administration of ampicillin-sulbactam (500 mg), and normal saline intravenously. Radiographs of the chest and neck were obtained (Figures 1 through 4). Humidified oxygen and additional racemic epinephrine treatments were administered by nebulizer.
Contact with the child was minimized while otolaryngology and pediatric anesthesia consultants were called emergently. The toddler demonstrated severe inspiratory stridor with labored breathing, head bobbing, supraclavicular retractions, increased work of breathing and possible tiring. The cry was hoarse. The patient was taken urgently to the operating room for airway management.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The lateral neck radiograph revealed swelling of the epiglottis in the upper airway (Figure 5, arrows), also known as the "thumb sign," diagnostic of epiglottitis. There was also some narrowing of the trachea, possibly suggestive of tracheitis; there were no foreign objects visible in the radiographs.
The patient was examined in the operating room. Examination by rigid bronchoscopy demonstrated a thickened and obstructing, cherry-red epiglottis, consistent with epiglottitis. There were increased secretions, which were cultured. The glottis and subglottic regions were not well visualized. The anesthesiologist was able to obtain a grade 1 view during intubation. Initially, the airway was intubated with a 3-0 uncuffed tube, which was replaced with a 3-0 cuffed tube because of an air leak. There were also findings consistent with acute otitis media in the right ear, and ear tubes were inserted bilaterally.
Gram stain of the secretions demonstrated very rare polymorphonuclear leukocytes, very rare squamous epithelial cells, few gram-positive cocci in pairs, and few gram-negative rods of mixed morphologies.
Within 24 hours, the blood culture grew organisms that were initially interpreted as gram-positive coccobacilli. The following day, the gram stain was reviewed, and gram-negative coccobacilli reported; the organism was identified as Haemophilus influenzae type b, shown in culture on chocolate agar (Figure 6) and on gram stain (Figure 7). Cultures of the secretions taken in the operating room grew mixed flora.

Treatment and Followup
Ceftriaxone and vancomycin were administered, and dexamethasone continued in a tapering dose. The patient was paralyzed and sedated, and the trachea remained intubated for 4 days while the inflammation in the epiglottis resolved. After the initial culture results were known, vancomycin was stopped, and ceftriaxone alone was continued. On the 5th day, amoxicillin alone was administered for a total antibiotic course of 10 days. Ciprofloxacin-dexamethasone otic drops were also given.
One month after discharge, the patient returned for follow-up with the infectious disease consultant. Levels of T-cell lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulin were normal. The level of immunoglobulin G antibody to H. influenzae type b (Hib) was 1.95 mg per liter (reference range greater than or equal to 0.15); other tests of immunity to vaccine antigens were also consistent with a normal immune response. Additional Hib vaccination was administered as recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics (The Red Book) for children younger than 2 years of age with invasive Hib disease.
Discussion
The causes of acute respiratory distress in a child may include non-infectious and infectious etiologies, as indicated by the differential listed above. Clues to the diagnosis are provided by the history, with an acute time course over several hours leading to respiratory distress and a hoarse voice suggestive of obstruction or swelling around the level of the larynx, without the barking cough more typical of croup. Physical examination should be performed in a manner that minimizes distress because children can decompensate and obstruct their airways rapidly. If epiglottitis is strongly suspected, examination of the mouth and pharynx should be avoided. Exudative pharyngitis caused by Epstein Barr virus (EBV) or Group A Streptococcus does not typically cause a hoarse voice, and is usually less pronounced in a child of this age. In this case, a foreign object was not seen in the mouth, posterior pharynx or on radiographs. The lateral neck radiograph demonstrated swelling at the level of the epiglottis, also known as the “thumb sign”, which is diagnostic of epiglottitis; the findings were confirmed by bronchoscopic examination. Although the radiographs were diagnostic in this case, they are often not performed when epiglottitis is suspected because of a concern of rapid clinical deterioration and, particularly in children, efforts instead are directed to immediate visualization of the upper airway, with intubation and establishment of a secure airway. The time course of croup is generally over several days, and radiographic findings may show gradual subglottic swelling, also called the “steeple sign”. The causes of life-threatening upper airway infections in pediatric populations in the United States have changed markedly since the late 1980s, when the Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine became widely available. According to one study at a pediatric hospital, the rate of epiglottitis fell from 3.5 cases per 10,000 admissions (57 cases total, with a mean age of 4.1 years), of which 84% (48 cases) were caused by H. influenzae type b during 1969-1977, to 0.3 cases per 10,000 (2 cases, both 18-years-old), which were caused by Streptococcus species, during 1995-2003 [Faden 2006]. In a second study, admissions to a pediatric intensive care unit for respiratory distress between 1997-2006 were caused by bacterial tracheitis 48% of the time (17 cases, of which 83% progressed to respiratory failure), compared with 46% due to croup (16 cases, 19% with respiratory failure), and 6% due to epiglottitis (2 cases, both with respiratory failure). Culture results were positive for Staphylococcus species 60% of the time, and other bacterial organisms identified included Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Streptococcus group A; influenza viruses, were also identified as co-infectious agents in several cases. One additional patient died before arrival at the hospital; autopsy results revealed bacterial tracheitis and cultures grew H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae [Hopkins et al 2006]. Haemophilus influenzae type b is a small gram-negative pleiomorphic coccobacillus (1 µm by 0.3 µm). It is responsible for causing severe morbidity and mortality throughout the world, particularly in children less than 5 years of age. H. influenzae type b may cause septicemia, as well as infections of the meninges, epiglottis, lungs, joints, and bones. Before the introduction of the Hib vaccine, it was the leading cause of bacterial meningitis in children less than 5 years in the United States. An analysis of reports of Hib meningitis has shown a case-fatality rate of 3.2% in industrialized countries compared to a rate of 17.3% in resource-poor countries (up to 27.6% in Africa) [Bennett et al, WHO report 2002]. Following the introduction of the Hib vaccine in the United States, the incidence of Hib disease in children less than 5 years, reported to be previously up to 20,000 cases per year, decreased by 71%, and that of H. influenzae type b meningitis by 82% [Adams 1993]. Distribution of the Hib vaccine to resource-poor countries remains a top priority of the World Health Organization [Danovaro-Holliday et al 2008]. This case illustrates that, although the incidence of serious H. influenzae type b infections has markedly decreased since the advent of the Hib vaccine because of the benefits of individual and herd immunity, the risk has not disappeared.
Final Diagnosis
Acute epiglottitis caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b infection in a toddler.
