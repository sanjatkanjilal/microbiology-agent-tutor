History of Present Illness
2012 IDWeek Case presentation: A man in his thirties with neurosarcoidosis developed stuttering and drooling.
The patient was well until approximately three days before admission when stuttering and drooling developed. On presentation to a local hospital, he reportedly had a seizure. Blood tests were obtained and a diagnosis of diabetic ketoacidosis was made. He was stabilized and was transferred to another hospital.
Past Medical History
Diagnoses of pulmonary and neurosarcoidosis had been made two years earlier, and he had been on glucocorticosteroid therapy chronically. He also had Type 2 (insulin-dependent) diabetes mellitus, diabetic neuropathy, hypertension and chronic hearing loss in the right ear.
Medications
Medications included dexamethasone (6 mg twice daily, orally), metformin (1000 mg twice daily, orally), insulin (glargine and lispro) and gabapentin.
Social History
He was exposed to paint fumes at work, and had been incarcerated in the past. He owned a dog.
Epidemiological History
He smoked marijuana and tobacco (1 pack for 15 years) daily.
Physical Examination
The patient was alert, oriented, and appropriately interactive, and appeared well. The blood pressure was 124/69 mm Hg, pulse 80 beats per minute, temperature 98.9°F (37.2°C), and peripheral oxygen saturation 92% while breathing ambient air. There was mild flattening of the left nasolabial fold. The abdomen was protuberant, with abdominal striae. There was hearing loss in the right ear and a mild sensory loss in both feet. The remainder of the examination, including ophthalmologic and motor examinations, was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 8400 cells per cubic millimeter (differential including neutrophils 77%, lymphocytes 16% and band forms 3%); hemoglobin 11.2 gm/dL and platelets 164,000 per cubic millimeter. The blood level of glucose was 418 mg/dl, sodium 123 mmol/L, anion gap 17 mmol/L; the remainder of the routine chemistry profile and tests of liver function were normal.
A radiograph of the chest (Figure 01) showed an opacity in the right base. CT of the chest (Figure 02) with the administration of contrast showed a small focal airspace opacity within the lateral aspect of the right lower lobe, which contained a small air fluid level associated with a small right pleural effusion and small mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with contrast enhancement showed six ring-enhancing lesions: two in the right fronto-temporal region, two in the left frontal region, one in the right parasagittal frontal lobe and a left gyrus rectus multi-compartment ring-enhancing lesion (see Figures 03 and 04).

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
He was admitted to the intensive care unit.
The patient’s HIV serology, quantiferon-TB gold test and serum galactomannan were negative and blood cultures remained sterile. An echocardiogram showed no vegetations. Cerebrospinal fluid demonstrated 2 white cells per cubic millimeter, and normal levels of protein and glucose; Gram stain, India ink and cryptococcal antigen testing were negative; culture was sterile.
A bronchoalveolar lavage (without biopsy) was normal. An image-guided biopsy of a lesion (Figure 05) was performed. Histopathological examination of the tissue showed neutrophils, plasma cells, and lymphocytes, consistent with acute and chronic inflammation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stain (Figure 06), GMS stain (Figure 07) and Calcofluor stain (Figure 08) are shown.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
GMS stain showed numerous fungal hyphal forms (Figure 07). Calcofluor stain showed narrow filamentous hyphal forms with acute branching angles (Figure 08). CNS Aspergillosis was suspected and the patient was started on voriconazole.
Two weeks later, his culture grew a pigmented fungi, which was identified as Curvularia sp. Figure 09 shows wet mount microscopy of Curvularia.
Figure 9. Wet mount microscopy with multicellular conidia of Curvularia.
Treatment and Followup
An MRI scan performed 3 weeks later showed no change in the size of the lesions, but there was a mild increase in the surrounding vasogenic edema. He was readmitted to the hospital where he was continued on voriconazole and liposomal amphotericin was added.
To eradicate this infection, he underwent a two-staged surgical approach to remove the six lesions in both hemispheres through two separate craniotomies (Figures 10 and 11). All surgical specimens grew Curvularia sp. that were susceptible to voriconazole (MIC <= 0.06). Dual antifungal therapy was continued intravenously for 3 weeks; he was then discharged to home on oral voriconazole with the plan to treat for 6 to 12 months. A follow-up MRI 3 months later showed a stable post-surgical appearance of his brain with no new regions of enhancement.

Discussion
Phaeohyphomycosis infections of the central nervous system (CNS) have been reported in both immunocompetent and immunosuppressed patients. Most of the immunocompromised patients with this type of infection had malignancy, solid organ transplant or neutropenia. Phaeohyphomycosis has rarely been reported in patients with suppressed immune system from steroids [Revankar et al. 2004]. This case illustrates the fact that patients with pharmacologic immunosuppression related to chronic steroid therapy may be at increased risk of developing CNS phaeohyphomycosis, and that this diagnosis should be included in the differential diagnosis of brain abscesses in such patients.
In CNS phaeohyphomycosis, the most frequent dematiaceous fungi species isolated is Cladophialophora bantiana [Revankar et al. 2004]. Only 6 cases of CNS phaeohyphomycosis due to a Curvularia spp. have been reported in the literature [Carter and Boudreaux 2004; de la Monte and Hutchins 1985; Ebright et al. 1999; Friedman et al. 1981; Lampert et al. 1977; Revankar et al. 2004; Singh et al. 2008].
The main postulated mechanism of disease is hematogenous spread from a possible subclinical pulmonary focus; direct extension from associated sinus disease is another possibility. In our case, hematogenous spread was the most likely route of dissemination to the brain given the pulmonary lesion, multifocal cerebral lesions and the absence of any history of sinus disease.
CNS phaeohyphomycosis is a severe disease with high mortality rates. Its management is difficult and involves a combination of surgical debridement and antifungal treatment, typically with amphotericin B or an azole antifungal therapy with good CNS penetration [Revankar et al. 2004].
Final Diagnosis
Multifocal cerebral phaeohyphomycosis due to Curvularia.
