History of Present Illness
IDSA 2007 Fellows Day Case Submission Summary: A man with fever and skin lesions.
A man in his thirties developed fever, chills and a non-pruritic eruption 2-3 weeks before admission. The lesions initially appeared on his face, spreading to his back, groin, scalp, and extremities over 1-2 days. During the previous six months, he noted malaise, anorexia, and weight loss of 40 pounds (18 kilograms).
Past Medical History
There was no significant medical history.
Medications
None.
Allergies
There were no allergies.
Epidemiological History
The patient was from the Midwestern United States. There was no recent travel, animal bites, or tick exposure. He had a remote history of sex with men. At the time of presentation, he reported being monogamous with a female partner of two years.
Physical Examination
The patient was cachectic. The temperature was 39°C (102.2°F). White plaques were present on the oral mucosa. There was a diffuse, crusted, papular and nodular eruption on the skin. Individual lesions ranged between 0.5-3.0 centimeters in size and were located over the back, chest, arms, legs, groin, face (including lips), and scalp, notably sparing of the palms and soles (Figures 1 through 4). The remainder of the examination was normal.

Studies
Laboratory results revealed a white blood cell count of 5,500 cells/mm³ (69% neutrophils, 20% lymphocytes, 10% monocytes), hemoglobin 12.9 g/dL, and platelet count 199,000 cells/mm³. Serum chemistries and liver function tests were within normal limits. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) EIA and Western blot were positive. An absolute CD4 T-lymphocyte count was 109 cells/mm³; HIV-1 RNA was 520,000 copies/ml.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A biopsy of a representative papule was performed. The hematoxylin and eosin stain of the specimen demonstrated lymphocytic and plasma cell infiltrate at the dermal-epidermal junction (Figures 5 and 6).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A silver stain of the biopsy material revealed treponemes (Figure 7 with arrows). A rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test was positive at 1:8 dilutions, and a fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption test (FTA-Abs) was reactive.
Figure 7. Silver stain of biopsy.
Treatment and Followup
Benzathine penicillin, 2.4 million units, was administered intramuscularly once weekly for three weeks. Antiretroviral therapy was also initiated. The patient's symptoms resolved within 3 weeks.
Discussion
When an initial syphilitic chancre is left untreated, treponemes proliferate and disseminate hematogenously, leading to a secondary eruption. Such lesions generally appear 4 to 10 weeks after the primary infection, presenting as generalized macules, papules, pustules, or rarely, vesicles. Secondary syphilitic eruptions are often accompanied by malaise, fever, and diffuse lymphadenopathy [Baughn and Musher 2005].
Lues maligna (malignant, or rupioid syphilis) is a rare and severe form of secondary syphilis. Initially described in 1864, lues maligna is characterized by multiple, pleomorphic, noduloulcerative lesions [Dubac 1864]. Patients present after an incubation period of 6 weeks to 1 year with a subacute prodrome of fevers, arthralgias, headache, and photophobia.
On examination, the lesions initially appear as papules, and evolve into pustules within a few days. Central necrosis, clean, well-demarcated ulcers, and a surrounding halo of erythema characterize lesions as they progress. Terminal ulcers are covered with layers of crust. Classically, the trunk, mucosal membranes, palms and soles are spared.
Histopathologic findings in lues maligna resemble those of other lesions of secondary syphilis. A band-like, lichenoid infiltrate of lymphocytes and plasma cells appears at the dermal-epidermal junction (Figures 5 and 6). There may be hyperkeratosis or necrosis in the epidermis [3]. On occasion, treponemes are visible on silver stain, as in Figure 7.
While the pathogenesis of lues maligna is still unclear, host factors likely play an important role; patients are characteristically malnourished, cachectic and, increasingly, HIV-positive [Kumar and Muralidhar 1998, Don et al 1995]. Untreated or incompletely treated patients may have a recurrence of constitutional symptoms and severe, scarring ulcers, leading to the description of such lesions as “malignant”.
In this case, the patient’s RPR was positive at 1:8 dilutions and FTA-Abs was reactive. He received benzathine penicillin (2.4 million units) intramuscularly weekly for three weeks, and had complete resolution of his symptoms immediately after treatment. His HIV viral load became undetectable on highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART). He was transiently lost to follow-up. Ten months after treatment, the RPR was non-reactive.
Final Diagnosis
Lues maligna (secondary syphilis) caused by Treponema pallidum in a patient with HIV infection and AIDS.
