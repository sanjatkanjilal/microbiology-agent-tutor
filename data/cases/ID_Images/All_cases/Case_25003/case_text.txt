History of Present Illness
A man in his late 60s was referred to the hospital by his primary care physician due to a significant change in cognition and behavior as well as an unintentional weight loss of 40 lbs over several months. The patient was noted to have become forgetful and confused and had made delusional statements. He was noted by his daughter to have developed difficulty performing activities of daily living and behavioral changes. He additionally reported malaise, intermittent headache, neck pain, and occasional muscle spasms. He denied fever or chills. He had been diagnosed with a urinary tract infection a few weeks prior based on dysuria and pelvic discomfort and these two symptoms had resolved with antibiotic treatment.
Past Medical History
The patient's medical history included well-controlled HIV, genital herpes simplex virus, hepatitis C virus that cleared without treatment, cleared hepatitis B virus, remote deep vein thrombosis, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and chronic pain. His surgical history included penetrating keratoplasty, vitrectomy, and corneal transplant several years prior related to an open globe injury.
Medications
His most recent antiretroviral regimen included darunavir, ritonavir, lamivudine, and doravirine. He was taking acyclovir for herpes simplex prophylaxis. He had also been recently treated for a urinary tract infection with ciprofloxacin.
Epidemiological History
He had last smoked cigarettes and used substances thirty years prior, with no substance use since that time. Twenty years prior to presentation, he had been incarcerated for one year and received tattoos during that time. He previously worked as a painter. He denied animal exposure or international travel. He lived in a house in the mid-Atlantic United States. He had not been sexually active in many years.
Physical Examination
On presentation, the patient was nontoxic but appeared mildly frustrated. His vital signs were within normal limits. He did not display nuchal rigidity though restricted mobility of the neck with tight muscles was noted. He was oriented to person and place, but not to time. Cranial nerves were normal aside from asymmetric and irregular pupils. Muscle tone, strength, sensation, reflexes, coordination, cerebellar testing, and gait were normal. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score was 11 out of 30 points, including deficits in visuospatial function, naming, abstraction, and delayed recall. The examination was otherwise normal.
Studies
He was mildly anemic with hemoglobin 11.5 g/dL (reference range 13.9-16.3 g/dL), with otherwise unremarkable blood counts. Complete metabolic panel showed chronically elevated creatinine of 1.5 mg/dL (reference range 0.6-1.3 mg/dL), which was at baseline, and was otherwise unremarkable. Urinalysis showed small leukocyte esterase (reference range: negative) with 6 white blood cells per high powered field (reference range < =5 per high powered field). Urine toxicology screen was consistent with adherence to prescribed oxycodone. His ammonia level was normal. His CD4+ cell count was 1,121/mm^3 with undetectable HIV viral load. Cerebrospinal fluid collected via lumbar puncture contained 1 white blood cell/cu mm (reference range 1-5/cu mm), normal glucose of 51 mg/dL (reference range 50-75 mg/dL), and elevated protein of 56.8 mg/dL (reference range 15-45 mg/dL).
Computed tomography of his head showed mild areas of periventricular and subcortical white matter hypoattenuation in the brain. Computed tomography angiogram of the neck showed multilevel degenerative changes in the cervical spine most pronounced at C5-6 where there was moderate canal and severe bilateral foraminal stenosis. Computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was unrevealing.
Electroencephalography showed rhythmic slow activity and intermittent generalized periodic discharges bilaterally. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed diffuse cortical restricted diffusion in the bilateral cerebral hemispheres sparing the mesial temporal lobes, insular cortices, and posterolateral frontal lobes associated with fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) hyperintensity without associated enhancement (Figure 1, Figure 2).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
There was concern for Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease based on the appearance of his brain magnetic resonance imaging. He underwent a second lumbar puncture to obtain additional studies. Cerebrospinal fluid studies showed 1 white blood cell/cu mm (reference range 1-5/cu mm), normal glucose of 63 mg/dL (reference range 50-75 mg/dL), elevated protein of 49.3 mg/dL (reference range 15-45 mg/dL), a markedly elevated 14-3-3 protein level of 21194 AU/mL (reference range: less than 30-1999 AU/mL), elevated T-tau protein level of 5377 pg/mL (reference range: 0-1149 pg/mL), and positive real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC, a highly sensitive and specific test for prion disease), consistent with prion disease. Additional microbiologic testing from cerebrospinal fluid was negative, including for herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, JC virus, venereal disease research laboratory (VDRL), cryptococcal antigen, toxoplasma antibodies, and bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures. Blood cultures and Treponema pallidum antibody were negative.
Treatment and Followup
The patient was connected to palliative and supportive home services. He was ultimately enrolled in hospice care as he experienced progressive, expected decline. He passed away three months after discharge.
Discussion
The differential diagnosis of infectious causes of cognitive and behavioral changes in persons living with HIV is broad and includes opportunistic processes such as cryptococcosis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, and toxoplasmosis. In patients without depressed CD4+ cell counts, however, non-opportunistic pathogens must be considered. Late neurosyphilis, HIV-associated dementia, and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease can cause progressive cognitive and psychiatric deterioration, though the latter is rare. A cerebrospinal fluid profile that includes elevated protein with normal glucose and absence of pleocytosis can be seen in all three conditions, though pleocytosis can be seen in neurosyphilis and HIV-associated dementia (1,2). CSF VDRL may be positive in neurosyphilis, though sensitivity is poor (1).
Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is a uniformly fatal cause of neuropsychiatric deterioration. Neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as behavior changes, memory deficits, mood changes, or psychotic or delusional features, occur in all cases. Myoclonus is often, but not always, present. This patient developed right upper extremity myoclonus two months after his initial diagnosis after experiencing further neurologic decline. Pyramidal, extrapyramidal, or cerebellar symptoms may also occur (3).
There are multiple categories of prion disease. The most common is the sporadic form, but genetic and acquired forms also exist. Iatrogenic CJD, a subset of acquired CJD, has been reported after cadaveric pituitary hormone administration, neurosurgical procedures including grafting of dural mater, and corneal transplant (3).
Our patient had undergone corneal transplantation seven years prior to diagnosis, raising concern for iatrogenic CJD. Iatrogenic CJD related to corneal transplant has been reported in the literature, though without diagnosis of CJD in the donor, the possibility of the much more common sporadic CJD cannot be ruled out (4).
Cerebrospinal fluid analysis is typically non-inflammatory with normal glucose and can demonstrate increased protein in about one-third of patients (5). 14-3-3 or tau protein are commonly elevated, though neither is disease-specific (6). More recently, real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) has become available as a superior test with 92% sensitivity and 100% specificity for prion disease (7). In RT-QuIC, shaking induces a conformational change in recombinant prion protein by the disease-associated prion protein (PrPSc) in a chain reaction, resulting in aggregation of misfolded proteins which bind to fluorescent dye and are quantified in real time.
MRI frequently demonstrates signal hyperintensity in the cerebral cortex (“cortical ribboning”) or caudate/putamen on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) or fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), as demonstrated in Figures 1 and 2 on our patient's MRI (8). “Hockey stick sign,” formed by medial and pulvinar thalamic hyperintensity, can be seen in variant CJD. On EEG, periodic sharp wave complexes (PSWCs) or generalized periodic discharges (GPDs) can be seen (9).
Definite diagnosis of CJD requires neuropathologic confirmation. The probable diagnosis of CJD can be ascertained from a positive RT-QuIC test plus neuropsychiatric features. If RT-QuIC is not available, the CDC defines a probable diagnosis as progressive dementia with two of four characteristic clinical features (akinetic mutism, myoclonus, visual/cerebellar signs, or pyramidal/extrapyramidal signs), a consistent diagnostic test (EEG with PSWCs, positive 14-3-3 assay in a patient with less than 2 years of symptoms, or brain magnetic resonance imaging with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) or fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) showing increased signal in the caudate/putamen or at least two cortical regions), and lack of diagnostics that suggest an alternative diagnosis (8).
There is no effective treatment for CJD. Management is focused on supportive and palliative care. Genetic testing can be offered to family members for genetic forms of the disease, though appropriate counseling should be provided. Prognosis is poor and most patients pass away within one year of diagnosis. To our knowledge, there have been 7 prior cases reported of confirmed or probable CJD in persons living with HIV; data is therefore limited in this population (10-15).
Final Diagnosis
Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease
