History of Present Illness
A usually well woman in her seventies presented with 3 days of fatigue and 1 day of fever. She participated in a sporting tournament only three days prior, felt extremely fatigued afterwards, but noted no other symptoms. On the day of presentation she felt febrile, measured at 104° F (40° C) and was brought to the emergency department.
Past Medical History
Osteoarthritis
Medications
Ibuprofen as needed
Epidemiological History
She was a keen traveler, and last travelled to Cuba 3 years prior. She had house cats (both outdoor/indoor). She had had dental work performed twice in the last three weeks including a tooth extraction and a crown fitting.
Physical Examination
At presentation, she was noted to be confused and febrile, 102.7º F (39.3° C); tachycardic at 106 beats per minute; blood pressure was 127/80 mm Hg; and respiration rate was 18 per minute with a saturation of 94% without supplemental oxygen. Her heart rhythm was irregularly irregular. She was noted to have a 5 mm erythematous papule with central crusted erosion over her right forearm (not shown). On further questioning, she indicated that the lesion started as a papule that then became a pustule 2 months prior. She also had an excoriation on her left forearm with no palpable lymphadenopathy. The rest of her physical exam was normal.
Studies
Her admission laboratory studies that included a complete cell count and renal and liver function tests were normal. Chest radiograph did not show a pneumonic process and CT chest showed trace bilateral effusions and enlarged lymph nodes in the right axillary, periportal and aortocaval chains, largest measuring 11 mm (not shown). A transthoracic echocardiogram did not show any valvular abnormalities. An electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Blood cultures were drawn and testing for Bartonella (serum PCR and serology), Coccidiodomycosis, Cryptococcus, Histoplasma, Brucella and Q fever were performed. She was empirically started on vancomycin and piperacillin/tazobactam and after one day was switched to levofloxacin. She was also started on metoprolol and apixaban given new-onset atrial fibrillation. She was planned for electrical cardioversion, but spontaneously converted to sinus rhythm and was discharged after 1 day of admission.
The next day she returned due to ongoing fever to 102.2° F (39° C) and fatigue, now accompanied by night sweats. On exam, she was noted to have mild swelling of the right calf. No thrombosis was noted in the lower extremities by Doppler ultrasound.
Given her persistent fevers while on antibiotics, a CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast was performed that showed multiple hypovascular liver lesions, some with rim enhancement and central hypodensity, the largest in the left hepatic lobe measured 23 x 10 mm (Figure 1). The scan also demonstrated multiple small splenic lesions and upper abdominal lymphadenopathy with the largest node measuring 16 mm. An ultrasound confirmed at least three focal hypoechoic liver lesions the largest of which was located in the left lateral segment and measured approximately 12 mm. She was started on ceftriaxone and metronidazole due to concern for hepatic abscess.

The scan also demonstrated multiple small splenic lesions and amorphous upper abdominal lymphadenopathy with the largest node measuring 16 mm.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Given concern for malignancy a liver biopsy was performed showing marked mixed inflammation and focal necrosis most consistent with an infectious etiology (Figure 2). Additionally, a biopsy of the lesion on her right forearm demonstrated suppurative granulomatous inflammation characterized by scattered loosely formed granulomas showing central suppuration (Figure 3). The overlying epidermis showed reactive epidermal hyperplasia with focal ulceration. The granulomas were surrounded by a mixed inflammatory infiltrate dominated by plasma cells that were seen periadnexally and perivascularly. All special stains for microorganisms (Gram, periodic acid–Schiff, Gomori methenamine silver, acid-fast bacillus and Steiner) were negative on both the liver and skin biopsy. During her two admissions, multiple blood cultures were negative. On presentation, serology and serum PCR for Bartonella henselae were negative, as well as serology for Coccidiodomycosis, Q fever and Brucella and antigen testing for Cryptococcus and Histoplasma.
Figure 2. Liver biopsy (H&E, 40x) showing mixed inflammatory infiltrate composed of lymphocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils and poorly aggregated histiocytes. Multinucleate giant cells were not identified. A few portal areas demonstrate edematous expansion and marked, dense mixed inflammation.
Figure 3. Skin biopsy (H&E, 40x) showing a suppurative granuloma consisting of collections of epithelioid histiocytes with a collection of neutrophils in the center (arrow) and a lymphoplasmacytic inflammatory infiltrate at the periphery.
Treatment and Followup
Despite her initial negative serology, serum PCR and liver biopsy, given a high clinical suspicion, we decided to switch to doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily to treat hepatosplenic cat scratch disease. After switching, she no longer had fevers. Shortly afterwards a rash ensued, presumed to be due to doxycycline. She was switched to azithromycin 500 mg daily. Bartonella henselae serologies at four and six weeks after presentation were positive with titers of 1:128 and 1:256, respectively. After nearly 6 weeks on azithromycin, she felt well, energy level normalized and she returned to her sporting activities. A repeat CT scan showed nearly complete resolution of all hepatic and splenic lesions.
Discussion
Early in the case, given the age of the patient and hepatic lesions, malignancy was a major concern, but liver biopsy was not consistent. In the persistently febrile patient, endocarditis is always a concern with multiple splenic and hepatic lesions being embolic manifestations of infective endocarditis (1). Blood cultures and serologic testing for etiologies of culture negative endocarditis were negative and transthoracic echocardiography was also negative for suspicious valvular lesions. Multiple hepatic lesions with fever raised the suspicion for pyogenic liver abscess but the patient had none of the classic identifiable risk factors including biliary disease, biliary instrumentation, diabetes, Asian ethnicity or injection drug use (2) and cultures (blood and liver biopsy) were negative.
The patient’s hand lesion and ipsilateral adenopathy seen on CT raised concern that the hand was a primary inoculation site with subsequent spread of infection to her regional lymph nodes. Specific infectious etiologic agents to account for our patient’s lymphocutaenous presentation included Bartonella henselae, Francisella tularensis, Nocardia spp, Bacillus anthracis, Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, Rhodococcus equi, Mycobacterium tuberculosis and non-tuberculous mycobacteria, Sporothrix shenckii, endemic mycosis (Coccidioides, Blastomyces and Histoplasma), Cryptococcus, Leishmania and viruses (Parapoxvirus and Orthopoxvirus) (3). Nonetheless, with her cat exposure, primary skin lesion, hepatosplenic disease, non-necrotizing granulomas on biopsy and positive serology with rising titers to Bartonella henselae, cat scratch disease (CSD) complicated by hepatosplenic (HS) manifestations was the most likely diagnosis.
Specificity of serology for CSD has been reported to be above 90% (4,5). A recent review of the literature published on CSD-HS among immunocompetent patients identified 33 cases and added three more (6). Published reports identified a specific bite or scratch by a cat in only 35% of cases. The most frequent symptoms and signs reported were fever (86%), constitutional symptoms (50%), abdominal pain (44%) and adenopathy (33%). Imaging showed multiple hypodense lesions in most patients (81%), with both the spleen and liver affected in 39% of cases, and a similar proportion of cases had abdominal adenopathy. In patients who had liver biopsies, 80% had necrotizing granulomatous hepatitis. Only 33% of biopsies that underwent silver stains (Warthin-Starry, Steiner) were positive for rod shaped bacteria. PCR of biopsy samples or blood had a sensitivity of 81% and serologic assays showed an IgG titer against B. henselae >1:128 in 94%.
Treatment of HS-CSD in immunocompetent hosts is not well defined. CSD is typically a self-limited disease and may be immune mediated. In susceptible hosts, a type IV hypersensitivity reaction through IL-12 production may lead to granuloma formation seen in HS disease (7). Given the possible immune nature of the disease, the role of antibiotics is unclear. Nonetheless, in a small study in children (n=19) with CSD-HS and prolonged fever (mean 22 days, range 7-56 days), patients had favorable responses when treated with rifampin alone or in combination with other antibiotics (n=16, mean time to defervesce 2.6 days, range 1-5 days) with an average duration of antibiotic therapy of 3 weeks (8). As no control group was available for comparison it is unknown if defervescence was due to antibiotic therapy. No trials exist in adults and it is unclear how long to treat these individuals.
Final Diagnosis
Hepatosplenic cat scratch disease
