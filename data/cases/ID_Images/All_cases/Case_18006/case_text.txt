History of Present Illness
A man in his sixties presented with left eye painless vision loss, scotomas, intermittent flashes of light and pressure behind the eye over the past 24 hours, 3 months after allogeneic stem cell transplantation (SCT) for primary myelofibrosis.
He was diagnosed with primary myelofibrosis approximately two years prior to this admission and was treated with hydroxyurea followed by ruxolitinib. He underwent radiation therapy for splenomegaly 3 months prior to his SCT. Approximately 3 months prior to admission, he underwent a fludarabine/melphalan-conditioned unrelated donor allogeneic peripheral blood stem cell transplant. His donor was CMV-positive, while he was negative for both CMV and Toxoplasma gondii.
His post-transplant course was complicated by CMV reactivation without end organ disease and gastrointestinal graft-versus host-diseases (GvHD). CMV viremia was detected on pre-emptive monitoring and treated successfully with valganciclovir which he completed on day 82. He was then transitioned back to acyclovir prophylaxis with continued preemptive monitoring. He received tacrolimus and methotrexate for primary GvHD prophylaxis but developed GvHD on day 54 thus necessitating high dose systemic steroids and beclomethasone. He was on pentamidine for Pneumocystis jirovecii Pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis as his persistent post- transplant pancytopenia precluded the use of trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. He was given posaconazole as antifungal prophylaxis while on high dose steroids for GvHD. On day 100 his steroids were reduced, and he no longer received fungal prophylaxis. He was discharged approximately 1 month after transplant.
He denied fevers, chills, headaches, hearing changes, neck pain, weight loss, weakness, chest pain, shortness of breath, cough, nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea.
Past Medical History
Chronic hepatitis B on lamivudine
Medications
valganciclovir (discontinued D+ 82); acyclovir prophylaxis 800 mg BID; posaconazole (discontinued D+ 100); monthly IV pentamidine for PJP prophylaxis; tacrolimus 2 mg BID for GvHD; prednisone 15 mg daily for GvHD
Epidemiological History
He lives in northwest USA and previously had worked with shipping containers. He had traveled to Asia. He drinks 3-4 beers per day and had a 50-pack year smoking history but quit 3 years ago.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well, and not in acute distress. His vital signs were unremarkable with a temperature of 97°F (36.1°C), HR 97, BP 135/67, RR 12, and oxygen saturations of 100% on room air. His lungs were clear and heart sounds were regular. There was no lymphadenopathy or skin rashes.
The external appearance of both eyes were normal without injected sclera, ptosis, or proptosis. Extraocular muscles were intact bilaterally but there was pain associated with inferior movement of the left eye. Both pupils were reactive to light, but the left eye was noted to have an afferent pupillary defect. He was unable to discern the shape of objects and his visual deficits included upper hemispheric deficiencies. He did not have visual deficits in the right eye. Visual acuity on the left was 20/40 and on the right was 20/20. Cranial nerves I-VII were intact.
The ophthalmologist’s exam revealed a normal intraocular pressure of 18 (normal 12-22 mm Hg). The slit exam revealed diffuse WBC and haziness in vitreous chamber. The fundus exam showed inferior edema continuous with retinal inflammation. There was retinal whitening with punctate hemorrhages noted in the macula (Figures 1 and 2).

Studies
Laboratory findings revealed a white blood cell count of 1500/mm 3 (reference range 3.5-10.8) with a differential of 84% neutrophils and an absolute neutrophil count of 1200. Hemoglobin was 7.1g/dL (reference range 13.5-17.5) and platelets of 24,000//mm 3 (reference range 150,000-400,000). CD4 cell count was 40/mm 3 (reference range 212-1391). Liver function tests were normal.
Chest x-ray was clear without evidence of nodules or consolidations. Computed tomography of the chest revealed numerous bilateral pulmonary nodules located throughout the right lung and the left upper lobe that measured between 3mm and 11mm (Figure 3). MRI of the brain with and without contrast revealed a 5 mm ring enhancing lesion in the right precentral gyrus as well as left periorbital edema and enhancement (Figure 4).
Additional laboratory evaluation included negative serum Toxoplasma gondii IgM and IgG, negative serum aspergillus galactomannan, negative plasma cytomegalovirus PCR, negative serum cryptococccal antigen, negative rapid plasma reagin, and negative serum T. gondii PCR.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Left eye vitreous samples were obtained and sent for bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures, CMV and Toxoplasma gondii PCR, and broad range 16S ribosomal PCR. The T. gondii PCR was positive and the 16S ribosomal PCR was also positive for T. gondii.
The patient was negative for T. gondii antibodies pre-transplant and the donor’s T. gondii serostatus was not known.
Treatment and Followup
Initially the ophthalmology unit performed intravitreal injections of voriconazole, clindamycin, and foscarnet until a diagnosis was made. He was also started prednisolone eye drops. Once the diagnosis of Toxoplasmosis was made, he was treated with pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine, and leucovorin.
He subsequently developed acute kidney injury while on these medications and sulfadiazine was discontinued. Atovoquone was substituted and he was continued on daily pyrimethamine, leucovorin, and prednisone. He received induction therapy dosing for approximately 6 weeks followed by maintenance therapy for another 6 weeks. Repeat MRI of the brain 2 months later showed that there was a slight decrease in the rim-enhancing lesion. Repeat CT chest showed that the pulmonary nodules had decreased in size. His T. gondii IgM and IgG were repeated and remained negative.
Discussion
Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular parasite. It typically remains latent in a normal host but, when there is a T-cell deficiency, fulminant disease can occur. It occurs worldwide and is acquired by ingestion of undercooked or raw meat containing viable cysts or through food and water contaminated with oocytes [1, 2].
Toxoplasmosis in those with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is a rare but potentially life-threatening infection if untreated. Infection typically occurs within the first 6 months post-transplant and the mortality rate is high if unrecognized. Data show that with appropriate treatment 60% of patients show clinical improvement. Infections that occur later after HSCT may have a better prognosis, which could be related to improved immunity over time [3]. Survival is highest in those with ocular and/or isolated cerebral toxoplasmosis but lower in those with disseminated infection [3, 4].
Toxoplasmosis primarily occurs as reactivation of dormant infection and therefore pre-transplant recipient serostatus determination is an important tool in assessing risk for post-transplant disease. Other predisposing factors for developing toxoplasmosis after HSCT include not receiving prophylaxis active against toxoplasmosis such as trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, intensification of immunosuppression for GvHD, and low CD4 cell count (<200/mm 3) [3]. This patient had all these risk factors.
Typically, recipients who develop toxoplasmosis after SCT will be seropositive prior to transplant. Toxoplasmosis is rarely considered in patients post-HSCT if they have had negative T. gondii serology prior to transplant [4]. To our knowledge, the fact that our patient was diagnosed with ocular and cerebral toxoplasmosis in the setting of negative pre-transplant T. gondii serology is unusual. There have been a few reports of patients with isolated cerebral or ocular toxoplasmosis in the setting of negative pre-transplant serology, but it is rare [4, 5]. Possible explanations for a negative serology include his low immunoglobulins or donor-to-recipient transmission [6, 7]. The donor’s serology results were not available. PCR testing of serum, CSF, or vitreous samples can aid in providing a diagnosis. There is a higher sensitivity of PCR on aqueous or vitreous humor in the immunocompromised versus immunocompetent patients [6].
Toxoplasma IgG assays typically have a high negative and positive predictive value. IgM serologic assays have negative predictive values around 98% with a more variable positive predictive value between 45-85% [8]. This is important to recognize as a positive IgM in a setting of low prevalence could lead to an unnecessary invasive work up and treatment.
Final Diagnosis
Ocular and cerebral toxoplasmosis
