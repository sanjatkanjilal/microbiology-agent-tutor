History of Present Illness
2016 July-August Featured Case: A man in his twenties was admitted to the hospital in late summer because of recurrent fevers, foot swelling, dyspnea and bradycardia.
The patient had been well until four weeks before admission when, after walking in a campground in northern New England, he noted a rash (approximately 2cm in diameter) on his right foot with 2 central marks, without known trauma or insect bite. During the next three days, his right foot became erythematous, swollen to double its normal size, and painful. He subsequently developed shaking chills but did not take his temperature. He was seen in the emergency department of another hospital and prescribed cephalexin for one week. During the following week, the foot lesion turned black, with reddish-purple discoloration in the surrounding tissue; the pain worsened and the erythema progressed to halfway up his calf. He was referred to a surgeon and a diagnosis of cellulitis secondary to a spider bite was made; the cephalexin course was extended to a total duration of two weeks.
After completion of antibiotic therapy, the pain, redness and swelling of his right foot initially improved to near-normal appearance. The chills and subjective fevers resolved and he was able to return to work.
Five days before this evaluation, temperatures between 102 and 103°F (38.9°C to 39.4°C) developed at night, and recurred nightly thereafter. The patient took acetaminophen, with transient improvement. There were associated diffuse myalgias; shortness of breath; episodic sharp back pain; right upper quadrant abdominal pain after eating; several episodes of diarrhea, and increased right foot swelling; as well as headache, dizziness, and blurry vision that resolved when afebrile. Pain in the right foot persisted. He did not have nausea, vomiting, photophobia, headache in the absence of fever, focal weakness, numbness, dysarthria, sore throat, cough, new rash, arthralgias, dysuria, or hematuria.
One day before this presentation, the patient returned to the outpatient department of the other hospital because of persistent symptoms; additional testing was ordered and an appointment with an infectious disease specialist was made. On the day of this presentation, he was seen at work, and was referred to the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital.
Past Medical History
He had previously been in good health.
Medications
He took acetaminophen for fevers, and had completed a two-week course of cephalexin.
Allergies
He had no allergies.
Social History
The patient lived in New England and worked in retail.
Epidemiological History
He drank multiple beers approximately twice weekly and smoked cigarettes. He had no other recent travel. He owned a dog.
Physical Examination
He was in no apparent distress, afebrile, with blood pressure 103/60 mm Hg, pulse in the mid-forties per minute, respirations 20 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation 99% while breathing room air. The heart rhythm was regular, bradycardic, with normal S1 and S2 heart sounds and no murmurs. There was left upper quadrant tenderness when palpating the right lower quadrant, and no rebound or guarding. The right foot was diffusely swollen, with discoloration over the dorsal surface. There was no erythema, tenderness, skin breakdown, or fluctuance. The remainder of the examination was normal, including no peripheral stigmata or evidence of embolic disease, and no rash.
Studies
The white cell count was 8400 per cubic millimeter (reference range (ref) 4500-11,000), with 79% neutrophils, 17% lymphocytes, 2% monocytes, and 2% eosinophils; the hematocrit, hemoglobin and platelet count were normal, as were blood levels of electrolytes, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, and renal and liver function tests. The glucose level was 66 mg/dl (ref 70-100), globulin 4.3 g/dl (ref 2.6-4.1), and creatine kinase 56 U/L (ref 60-400). Urinalysis revealed 1+ albumin and trace ketones, with 3 to 5 red cells and few bacteria per high-power field.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Lyme immunoblot IgG testing revealed greater than 5 reactive antigens. Antibody capture enzyme immunoassay for antibodies to Borrelia burgdorferi (the agent of Lyme disease) were positive, with an IgM level greater than 27.8, IgG greater than 17.6 and IgA greater than 16.2 (normal range less than 1), diagnostic of disseminated infection. Test results from the other hospital one day earlier were obtained, with reportedly positive testing for IgM antibodies.
The EKG shown in Figures 1 and 2 revealed complete atrioventricular (AV) dissociation and a ventricular QRS rate between 40 and 49 per minute, normal R-wave progression, and no ST-segment changes or pathological T-wave inversions. The EKG shown in Figures 3 and 4 revealed sinus bradycardia at a rate of 42 beats per minute, with 2nd degree AV block and 2:1 AV conduction.
Blood cultures were sterile. Thick and thin Giemsa smears showed no evidence of intracellular organisms. Magnetic resonance images (MRI) of the foot showed no evidence of osteomyelitis.
Treatment and Followup
Ceftriaxone (2gm daily) was administered intravenously. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a dilated left atrium (AP dimension 42mm (reference 25-38), and SI 56mm) and a mildly dilated left ventricle 56 (reference 37-53), with no segmental wall motion abnormalities, and a 69% ejection fraction. There was a minimal posterior pericardial effusion and no valvular vegetations or evidence of tamponade.
Four days after admission, a treadmill stress test demonstrated excellent exercise capacity, with sinus rhythm ranging between 66 and 160 beats per minute, and first degree AV block, without 2nd or 3rd degree AV block. The patient was discharged on the 5th hospital day, on doxycycline 100 mg twice daily, for a total antibiotic course of three week's duration.
On follow-up one month later, an electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm at a rate of a 56 per minute.
Discussion
Lyme carditis is an uncommon but potentially serious complication of disseminated Borrelia burgdorferi infection. Studies published after the year 2000 have indicated that this manifestation occurs in 1 to 2% of cases of Lyme disease [Forrester and Mead, 2014; Robinson et al. 2015] and is seen more frequently in men than women [Forrester and Mead, 2014]. As compared to the reported frequency of this complication in studies published before 2000, these more recent studies suggest a decline in the rate [Robinson et al., 2014], perhaps related to the greater frequency of antibiotic administration early in the course of Borrelia burgdorferi infection.
Although approximately 60% of patients diagnosed with Lyme carditis may not recall having had a tick bite, many developed a skin lesion consistent with erythema migrans, suggestive of early Lyme disease. Erythema migrans may occur between 5 to 48 days after infection (median 12 days) and last up to several weeks before spontaneous resolution [Robinson et al. 2015]. Dissemination of disease to other tissues, including the heart, occurs weeks to months after initial infection. Skin and organ involvement can occur concurrently. The conduction abnormalities associated with Lyme carditis may be potentially life-threatening [Forrester and Mead, 2014; Robinson et al. 2015] and require temporary pacemaker insertion if associated with asystole, other hemodynamic instability or severe symptoms [Robinson et al. 2015]; however, the abnormal conduction usually resolves within 6 weeks [Forrester and Mead, 2014; Robinson et al. 20154] and the need for permanent pacing is extremely uncommon. When heart block is present, the degree may vacillate rapidly [Forrester and Mead, 2014], as seen in this patient, whose heart block changed from 3rd to 2nd degree within minutes (seen in Figures 2 and 4 above). Associated symptoms in those presenting with complete heart block include syncope, fever, lightheadedness, dyspnea, weakness, palpitations, chest pain, headache, and arthralgia [Forrester and Mead, 2014; Robinson et al. 20154], many of which were present in this patient.
Pathological examination of cardiac specimens obtained from affected patients may reveal spirochetes of Borrelia burgdorferi dispersed diffusely in the tissue, as well as evidence of inflammation [Robinson et al. 2015].
In this patient, the diagnosis was suggested by his residence in a Lyme endemic area and by the clinical picture, which included skin and cardiac findings. The definitive diagnosis was made by serologic testing, with positive IgM and IgG antibodies to Borrelia burgdorferi. The differential diagnosis of infectious etiologies of atrioventricular block with preserved ejection fraction is relatively narrow and includes Lyme disease, aortic root abscess in setting of bacterial endocarditis, and Chagas disease [Bern 2011]. Any infection that causes myocarditis [Fung et al. 2016] may result in atrioventricular block; thus the infectious differential diagnosis expands in the setting of accompanying myocardial dysfunction. Other diseases transmitted by ticks such as babesiosis and anaplasmosis were briefly considered, but these were much less likely given the clinical picture and laboratory test results, including the absence of intracellular organisms on Giemsa staining and normal white cell, hematocrit and platelet counts. Bacteremia with endocarditis was also considered, but disproved with negative blood cultures and echocardiography. Influenza was also less likely given the onset in summer, the associated leg findings and the absence of significant upper respiratory symptoms.
Final Diagnosis
Lyme disease, caused by Borrelia burgdorferi infection, complicated by carditis and complete heart block.
