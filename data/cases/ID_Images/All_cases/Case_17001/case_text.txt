History of Present Illness
A previously well man in his 40s presented to our Emergency Department (ED) with 2 weeks of progressive left eye swelling and redness (Figure 1), which he presumed was due to an insect bite. He had difficulty opening his left eye. A week prior to the ED presentation, a general practitioner commenced him on oral flucloxacillin (similar to cloxacillin) 500mg qid for 7 days, however the swelling and redness continued to progress. The patient denied any pain, visual changes, fevers and nausea.
Figure 1. Left eye swelling at 2 weeks of illness
Epidemiological History
He was born and raised in Melbourne, Australia, has an office-job and spends time in the Mornington Peninsula (coastal Victoria). There was no significant animal exposure. He had no recent overseas travel. He does not smoke, drink alcohol or use illicit drugs and did not have any recent trauma.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. He was afebrile (36.1°C = 97°F), his blood pressure was 117/80 with a pulse rate of 75 beats and respiratory rate of 16 per minute, and oxygen saturation of 99% on room air. He had periorbital swelling around the left eye involving his upper and lower eyelid (Figure 1). This was erythematous, mildly tender and minimally fluctuant. There was no visible punctum or discharge. His visual acuity was 6/6 m bilaterally and his range of eye movements were normal. There was no pain with eye movements. The remainder of the cranial nerve examination and general examination was normal.
Studies
Full/Complete blood count and differential, renal and liver function tests were normal. The C-reactive protein was 9 mg/L (reference range 0-5 mg/L). Computer tomography (CT) scan of the orbits showed left periorbital soft tissue swelling and subcutaneous fat standing with a small curvilinear region of low attenuation measuring 4 x 27 x 16 mm suggestive of a subcutaneous collection (Figure 2). The post-septal tissues and globe were normal with no cortical destruction. Ophthalmic veins and cavernous sinuses were normal and paranasal sinuses were well-aerated.
Figure 2. Figure 2: CT of orbits. Red arrow indicating periorbital soft tissue swelling.
Figure 2: CT of orbits. Red arrow indicating periorbital soft tissue swelling.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Cefazolin 1g 8-hourly was administered intravenously. He had a washout of the left peri-orbital collection where a small abscess close to the lateral margin of the left eye was noted. Gram stain showed 1+ polymorphs, no bacteria were seen and routine bacterial cultures were negative. He was discharged the day after the operation on amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (875/125 mg) twice daily and topical chloramphenicol ointment for his left eye. Despite this, he presented again two weeks later with persistent erythema and swelling and a small ulcer over the lateral left eyebrow (Figure 3). He was taken to the operating room for further biopsy.
Figure 3. Figure 3: Persistent left eye swelling (post-initial surgery) at 4 weeks of illness. Red arrow indicating the ulcer.
Figure 3: Persistent left eye swelling (post-initial surgery) at 4 weeks of illness. Red arrow indicating the ulcer.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Mycobacterial stain of surgical biopsy of the lesion revealed acid fast bacilli (Figure 4) and Mycobacterium ulcerans PCR was positive.
Figure 4. Figure 4: Fite’s acid fast stain demonstrating acid fast bacilli (AFB). Red arrow indicating the AFB.
Figure 4: Fite’s acid fast stain demonstrating acid fast bacilli (AFB). Red arrow indicating the AFB.
Treatment and Followup
The patient was treated with clarithromycin 500 mg twice-daily and rifampicin 300mg twice-daily, aiming for a duration of 4 months. At three months of follow-up the patient was significantly improved (Figure 5) with mild residual swelling around the left eye.
Figure 5. Figure 5: Photo at 3 months of follow-up demonstrating significant improvement.
Figure 5: Photo at 3 months of follow-up demonstrating significant improvement.
Discussion
Discussion of Differential diagnoses:
• The lack of fevers, pain and systemic symptoms make a pyogenic bacterial infection unlikely. Pus is usually seen.
• Orbital mucormycosis could explain a swollen protruding eye, but the area is not usually erythematous. He had no limitations in eye movement, he is a normal host and his CT did not show involvement of the globe.
• Cutaneous T-cell lymphomas can occur anywhere, but most commonly presents as patchy trunk and limb rashes. This is a very rare diagnosis. Histology was negative for malignancy.
• There was no history of contact to account for an allergy.
• Despite popular belief, snakes and scorpions are not commonly found in Melbourne homes! Such a localised and slowly progressive swelling without pain, purpura or systemic toxic symptoms would be unusual.
Mycobacterium ulcerans infection, or Buruli ulcer, is an emerging health problem in parts of the developing world and, increasingly, Australia. First described in sub-Saharan Africa, the disease has now been identified in Japan, Mexico, Central America, and Papua New Guinea (reviewed in (1)). In Australia, the disease occurs in specific endemic areas, particularly the coastal and peninsular areas of temperate climate Victoria (south-eastern state), where it is known as Bairnsdale ulcer after a local town (2). The median incubation period is 4.5 months but can range from 34 to 264 days (3). Recent epidemiological research in Victoria suggests a possible role for mosquitos, other biting arthropods and possums in its transmission (4, 5).
Lesions usually begin as a painless nodule under 5mm in diameter, however they can also present as papules, plaques or edematous lesions (6). Frequently there are no systemic features or regional lymphadenopathy as M. ulcerans produces mycolactone, a toxin that inhibits the immune response (7). Infection commonly occurs on the exposed limbs, although it can present on any part of the body. The initial lesion breaks down over days to weeks forming the classic undermined Buruli ulcer which progresses slowly and painlessly (6).
The diagnosis of M. ulcerans can be difficult due to its fastidious nature and specific growth requirements. The diagnosis is based on the clinical picture, the detection of acid-fast staining (42% sensitive) (8) and PCR (86-98% sensitive) (8, 9). Yield of culture is low. We did not send for AFB culture as Mycobacterium ulcerans culture in Lowenstein-Jensen or liquid media is slow (typically requires 6-12 weeks) and the sensitivity approaches only 50% (8). For ulcerated lesions, a swab under the rim of the undermined ulcer for PCR is recommended; however for non-ulcerative lesions (plaques, edematous lesions, nodules), swab specimens often provide false-negative results and biopsy or fine-needle aspirate are often necessary.
While older reviews and guidelines advocated for surgery with wide excision of margins as the mainstay of M ulcerans management (10, 11), recent randomised and observational studies (12-15) have demonstrated antibiotics to be effective. At the time of this writing in 2017, the recommended antibiotic therapy according to Australian guidelines (2) is rifampicin combined with either clarithromycin (preferred) or a fluoroquinolone (moxifloxacin or ciprofloxacin). Antibiotics duration is 8 weeks for simple cutaneous lesions, and 12 weeks for lesions involving deeper structures (ie. joints, bones). Paradoxical reactions occur in up to 20% of patients, with worsening of the lesion in a process known as an immune reconstitution inflammatory reaction (16). This does not represent treatment failure, and severe reactions can be managed with adjunctive oral corticosteroids. As clinical and microbiological cure is achievable with antibiotics, surgery is limited to debridement of necrotic areas, repair of large defects or rare instances where antibiotics is declined or contraindicated and direct closure of a small lesion is preferred. Risk of relapse with surgery alone is high (17). The World Health Organization website has a number of useful resources in the epidemiology, diagnosis and treatment of Buruli ulcers (18).
Final Diagnosis
Mycobacterium ulcerans infection causing left eye pre-septal cellulitis.
