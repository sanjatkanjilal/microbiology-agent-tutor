History of Present Illness
A teenaged female presented to our hospital with complaints of vision changes in the left eye. She started having pressure like sensation in the left eye 3 days prior to presentation. She had pain with movement of the eye to the right. She initially had developed dark spots in the vision that progressed to blurring and decreased vision. She also had decrease in the color vision in the central fields. She denied having any change in the vision in the right eye. She was seen at an outpatient optometry office and was found to have macular edema. She was referred to the hospital where she was seen by the ophthalmologist and was confirmed to have optic neuritis.
Past Medical History
Polycystic Ovarian Disease and Hypertension both diagnosed when she was a teenager
Medications
None
Epidemiological History
She was born in the East Coast of the United States and had lived in various East Coast states. She denied having any sick contacts. At the time of history, she worked part time and her job mostly involved desk work. She is unmarried and sexually active with 1 male partner. She has cats at home. She vapes occasionally and denies any illicit drug use.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. Her vitals were stable with temperature 98.6 °F (37 °C), pulse 98 beats per minute, blood pressure of 138/86 mm Hg and respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute. She had pain in the left eye on rightward gaze with relative afferent pupillary defect in left eye. There was a loss of visual fields in the left eye left superior and lower fields. Rest of the exam including neurological exam was negative.
Studies
The hematocrit was 39.2% (reference range 37.7 – 47.9%), white blood cell count was 10,770 cells/µL (reference range 4600-10,200/µL), C-Reactive Protein was 1.34 mg/dl (reference range 0.01-0.5 mg/dl), Erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 48 mm/hour (reference range 0-20 mm/hour). Other laboratory test results including electrolytes, renal function, liver function, vitamin B12, folate level, Human Immunodeficiency Virus Antigen/ Antibody test, Treponemal Antibody, Bartonella Antibodies, Anti-nuclear antibody screen, Toxoplasma antibody were negative. MRI Brain and MRI Orbit were performed and revealed no acute findings.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Ophthalmology service was consulted, and dilated fundus exam was performed that showed massive 360° optic nerve head elevation with vessel obscuration, hemorrhages, and blurring of the disc margins with edema spanning into macula. There were no macular exudates or macular star. Findings were consistent with optic neuritis (Figure 1). Since the patient had macular edema associated with disc elevation suspicion was high for neuroretinitis caused by entities such as Cat Scratch. While awaiting results of Bartonella serologies, the patient was empirically started on Doxycycline orally 100 mg twice a day as per the recommendations from Infectious Disease service.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Three days later, ophthalmology service repeated dilated fundus exam and the patient was found to have macular edema with exudates adjacent to nerve nasally in half macula star configuration (Figure 2) reinforcing clinical suspicion for Bartonella neuroretinitis. However, Bartonella antibodies test returned negative at this point.

Treatment and Followup
Patient requested to be discharged from the hospital. Infectious disease service recommended a course of oral Doxycycline 100 mg twice a day and oral Rifampin 300 mg twice daily for a total of 4 weeks. She was then seen after 3 weeks in the ophthalmology clinic in her hometown and a thorough work up was performed, and Bartonella serologies were repeated. The Macular Ganglion Cell Complex (GCC) available in OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography) showed mild thinning nasally and temporally in the left eye (Figure 3). OCT Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL) showed swelling of the layer in the left eye which was 125 µm average thickness compared to the right eye which was 91 µm average thickness (Figure 4). She followed up for routine visit 3 weeks later. By this time, she had completed the antibiotics and was off the medications. Her repeat Bartonella henselae IgG returned as 1:2048 (reference range <1:128 titer) and Bartonella henselae IgM >1:20 (reference range <1:20 titer). Bartonella quintana antibodies were negative. Patient’s vision continued to improve off the medications. Her dilated fundus exam of the left eye revealed normal disc, few macular star exudates, normal vessels and normal periphery (Figure 5).

Discussion
Ocular manifestations of Cat Scratch Disease (CSD) include Parinaud oculoglandular syndrome, neuroretinitis, papillitis, optic neuritis, and focal retinochoroiditis [1]. Cats are usually asymptomatic carriers. Transmission to humans occurs through a cat’s scratch or bite, and less commonly, through mucous membrane contact [2]. Among all patients with CSD, about 1 to 2 percent develop neuroretinitis, which is a syndrome of acute visual loss from optic nerve edema associated with macular exudates. Bartonella PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) on the blood is usually negative. Direct identification of Bartonella henselae using microbiological cultures is difficult owing to the bacterium’s slow growth rate. There is no gold standard for conclusive CSD diagnosis and as such serological test and rising titers of IgG and elevated IgM is considered gold standard for diagnosis [3,4,5]. Patients with neuroretinitis typically present with fever, malaise, and unilateral blurred vision. On examination patients usually have unilateral involvement, with decreased visual acuity, often associated with an afferent pupillary defect as seen with our case. Retinal findings may include hemorrhages, cotton wool spots, and multiple discrete lesions in the deep retina. Some patients develop a stellate macular exudate (known as a "macular star") [3]. Macular stars are due to vascular leakage from the optic-disc edema, not by a retinal process, so the optic-nerve edema precedes the macular star. At first glance, the macroscopic star could be missing. It typically resolves 1–12 weeks following the beginning of optic-disc edema. The key role of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) in retinal cells’ metabolism might influence the recovery potential of the retina, as already revealed by research regarding degenerative retinal conditions [6]. The macular star disappears after four weeks but could remain for almost a year and impede the recovery of visual acuity [7]. The extreme sensitivity of macular cells must be considered as an important factor in the recovery of the macula. In our patient, we noticed improvement of the macular star, but it persisted at the 6 weeks follow up in the clinic post discharge from the hospital. Fundus imaging, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and fluorescence angiography (FA) can aid in the diagnosis and treatment of CSD [8]. Late leakage from the optic disc is the most frequently observed finding on FA. Peripapillary angiomatosis and peripapillary serous retinal detachment are additional FA observations [9,10]. Although neuroretinitis is a well-described complication of B. henselae infection, angiomatous lesions remain uncommon. In previous studies, neovessels were discovered during an OCT angiography of a Bartonella focal chorioretinitis. It may assist in differentiating Bartonella lesions from other noninfectious or infectious fundus lesions because Bartonella spp. have a unique vascular proliferative characteristic [11]. In neuroretinitis, optic-disc edema causes serous retinal detachment, resulting in immediate unilateral vision loss, followed by macular exudates organized in a partial or full star pattern around the fovea [12,13]. OCT may show subretinal fluid in all eyes with Bartonella neuroretinitis, while ophthalmoscopy or fluorescein angiography may not. According to Parikh et al. [14], OCT may be helpful in detecting intraretinal edema associated with optic-disc inflammation preceding serous retinal detachment and subsequent macular-star formation. B. henselae -induced ocular inflammation is often benign and self-limiting, with good visual outcomes. Most patients with neuroretinitis appear to have a good long-term prognosis, although patients with macular exudates may have residual defects that include optic disk pallor, diminished contrast sensitivity, and altered color vision. Lee et al. state that antimicrobial treatment reduces the duration of the disease symptoms and that, therefore, patients should be treated with antibiotics until the laboratory testing is completed [15]. On the other hand, Bhatti asserts that therapy has no effect on the cure rate [16]. Given the rarity of Bartonella neuroretinitis, recruiting participants for a randomized clinical study would be very challenging and would require multicenter research [17]. Reed et al. [18] found that antibiotics resulted in a faster visual recovery and a shorter duration of illness. Chiu et al. [19] found no difference in visual results between treated and untreated patients (antibiotics, corticosteroids, or both). There is insufficient evidence to support the advantages of specific antibiotic treatment in immunocompetent individuals with uncommon CSD manifestations [20]. Numerous antibiotic treatments, particularly gentamicin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, azithromycin, ciprofloxacin, erythromycin, doxycycline, and rifampin, were utilized in atypical CSD, alone or in various combinations. Due to their deep penetration of the central nervous system, the majority of researchers and doctors advise a protocol of doxycycline and rifampin for 10–14 days [21], with a treatment course of 2–4 weeks in immunocompetent individuals and four months in immunosuppressed patients
Final Diagnosis
Bartonella henselae Neuroretinitis
