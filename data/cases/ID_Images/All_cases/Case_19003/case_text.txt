History of Present Illness
A young male returning traveler in his twenties presented to the emergency department (ED) with a three-week history of a pruritic migrating serpiginous rash on his right foot (Figure 1, when he first noticed the lesion). The rash appeared while he was in Thailand, about ten days into his trip. The patient also noted having a nonproductive cough for two weeks with occasional wheezing. He denied other skin lesions, dyspnea, hemoptysis, chest and abdominal pain, or changes in bowel habits. Past Medical History
The patient is not known to have any chronic medical problems.
Medications
The patient was not using any medications. He recently used atovaquone/proguanil (250 mg/100 mg PO daily for 3 weeks) for malaria prophylaxis while on a trip to Southeast Asia, but discontinued it upon return, a few days before presenting to the ED.
Epidemiological History
The patient had recently returned from a one-month vacation where he travelled to Laos, Thailand, Malaysia, and Singapore. Travel activities included hiking, swimming in freshwater, and sunbathing. He admitted to contact with animals including elephants, monkeys, and water buffaloes, but denied eating any raw meat or fish or drinking tap water. He is a non-smoker, does not use recreational drugs, and denied sexual activities while travelling.
Physical Examination
Patient appeared well, with no signs of systemic toxicity or respiratory distress. Vital signs were the following: Temperature 36.4°C (97.5°F), Blood Pressure 135/80, Heart Rate 97, RR 18, Oxygen saturation 98% on room air.
The cutaneous lesion (Figure 2) was located over the right foot fifth metatarsal. It was a raised vesiculobullous serpiginous lesion measuring 3 cm in length and about 2 mm in width. Significant swelling and redness surrounded the lesion. According to earlier pictures taken by the patient, the lesion has been migrating proximally leaving burrows behind its leading edge. The lesion moved about 4 cm in the last 3 weeks.
Chest expansion was symmetrical and without accessory muscle use. There was no dullness to percussion over either lung field. Expiratory rhonchi were auscultated in both hemithorax. The examination was otherwise normal.

Studies
Chest X-ray (Figure 3) showed increased patchy central opacities in the right and left lower lung zone. There was no evidence of pulmonary edema or pleural effusion.
His white blood count was raised at 15.3 x10^9/L (reference range: 4.5-11) with 31% eosinophils (absolute eosinophil count, 4.75 x10^9/L (ref 0-0.45)). Hemoglobin and platelets were normal. IgE was high at 917 ug/L (ref 0-240). Renal and liver function tests were normal.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Based on the CXR findings, cutaneous eruption and the recent travel history, he was referred to a tropical medicine specialist.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Filaria serology: negative
Strongyloides serology: negative
Strongyloides stool culture: negative
Stool ova/parasites: negative
Treatment and Followup
The patient was prescribed ivermectin 15 mg PO QD for 3 days. After taking the medication, the cutaneous lesion gradually disappeared and the respiratory symptoms resolved. He completed a repeat blood test and microscopy a few weeks later. The results showed a normal eosinophil count and no ova/parasite in his stool. The repeat Chest X-ray was normal (view Figure 4). Discussion
Cutaneous larva migrans (CLM) results from the migration of a non-human animal hookworm in human skin. Ancylostoma braziliense, Ancylostoma caninum, or other nematodes of the hookworm family are the common causative organism. This usually occurs in the context of a person in an endemic area (such as Southeast Asia) walking barefoot or sitting on a beach contaminated with an infected animal’s stool. Given that the human is an accidental host, the hookworm lacks the proteases necessary to penetrate below the skin’s basement membrane. As a result, unlike Ancylostoma duodenale or other human hookworm infections, the larva cannot enter the lymphatics or bloodstream. Thus, it migrates through the epidermis for weeks to month until it is eventually disintegrated by immune defenses. Clinically, this manifests as a creeping eruption, a highly pruritic migrating serpiginous rash that may have a vesiculobullous quality (1).
Cellular and humoral immune defense against multicellular parasites is largely mediated by eosinophils and IgE antibodies. The resultant eosinophilia, if persistent, can have a number of consequences on various organ systems, collectively termed secondary hypereosinophilic syndromes (2). The patient presented in this case was diagnosed with Loeffler’s syndrome, an eosinophilic pneumonia secondary to a parasitic infection (3).
The pathogenesis by which CLM leads to Loeffler’s syndrome is not fully elucidated. Some postulate that it occurs as a hypersensitivity reaction to the parasitic antigens, others believe that on rare occasions, CLM can lead to visceral larval migration through lung parenchyma. This has been described with A. caninum (1, 4). Alternatively, the patient may have been infected with two separate hookworm larvae, one cutaneous (non-human) and one visceral (human). The normal route of a human larval hookworm infection consists of skin penetration, lymphatic spread, hematogenous dissemination, lung infiltration and migration up the airways to the oropharynx. The larva is then swallowed and implants itself in the intestines (5). The transpulmonary phase of its migration, and resultant pulmonary eosinophilia, can present with a cough. However, the primary clinical manifestations of a visceral hookworm infections are gastrointestinal, namely, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting (6). The lack of gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms and negative stool ova/parasite analysis in the patient can either be seen as evidence for the absence of a visceral hookworm, or as evidence that the visceral parasite was eradicated during the pulmonary phase of the disease (prior to GI implantation).
The major diagnostic dilemma involved in the case was differentiating the patient’s presentation from strongyloidiasis. Strongyloides stercoralis parasitic infections have a high incidence in Southeast Asia and can be acquired from stepping in contaminated, usually humid, soil. The condition can present with a serpiginous pruritic rash called larva currens. It is distinguished from CLM by its rapid rate of migration (five or more cm per hour) and its tendency to form urticarial bands (7). Strongyloides is uncommonly associated with Loeffler’s syndrome. Its pulmonary symptoms usually arise in the context of chronic infection or acute hyperinfection in an immunocompromised host (8). This diagnostic possibility was further excluded when the serology result was negative.
Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia (TPE), caused by filarial nematodes such as Wuchereria bancrofti, was also included as a differential diagnosis. The mosquito-borne illness is endemic to Southeast Asia, can present with striking eosinophilia, and has notable pulmonary symptoms. However, filariasis typically presents with pronounced adenolymphangitis, skin abscesses, and elephantiasis. Contrary to a transitory Loeffler’s syndrome, TPE is a long-lasting mixed obstructive-restrictive lung disease with possible progression to pulmonary fibrosis. Occasionally, it can present with constitutional symptoms such as fever, malaise, and weight loss (9). Filarial serology was also shown to be negative.
Paragonimiasis, or lung flukes, were lower on the differential diagnosis. Although they are endemic to Southeast Asia, they are typically acquired through the ingestion of raw meat or fish, an exposure that the patient denied. Once ingested, the flukes migrate from the duodenum through the peritoneum and diaphragm into the lungs. Thus, symptoms usually consist of pleuritic pain preceded by GI disturbance. Their migration is sometimes associated with pneumothorax and pleural effusions (8).
A community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) was also considered in the diagnostic evaluation. However, the recent travel history, comorbid CLM, and peripheral eosinophilia made CAP unlikely. The fact that the respiratory and skin symptoms both resolved after taking antihelminthic medications (ivermectin) further cemented the likelihood of a parasitic etiology.
Final Diagnosis
Loeffler’s syndrome secondary to helminthiasis.
