History of Present Illness
2013 September-October Featured Case: A man in his forties was seen in the emergency department of the hospital because of fever and pain and swelling of the right knee several days after nephrolithotripsy.
The patient was well until six weeks earlier, in early summer, when low back pain developed and persisted despite treatment with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications. Approximately one month before this presentation, pain in the back and severe right flank pain that radiated down his right leg occurred, associated with hematuria. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis with the administration of contrast revealed nephrolithiasis, including stones in the left renal pelvis (13mm by 7 mm), left lower pole (a cluster measuring together 10 mm by 5 mm), and in the right proximal ureter (4-to-6mm), with ureteral thickening surrounding the proximal right ureter and mild prominence of the collecting systems bilaterally. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbosacral spine showed disk extrusion on the right side at the 3rd and 4th lumbar level with moderate central canal stenosis and impingement on the right descending L4 nerve roots, central disk protrusion with an associated annular disk tear at the 4th and 5th lumbar level with impingement on the traversing right L5 nerve roots, and left paracentral disk extrusion at the 5th lumbar and first sacral level which contacted the descending left S1 nerve roots without evidence of impingement. Muscle relaxants and pain medications were administered.
Five days before this evaluation, retrograde cystoscopy and ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy and stone retrieval on the right side and placement of a right ureteral stent were performed, with the administration of cefazolin (2 grams) preoperatively.
Three days after the surgical procedure, his temperature reportedly rose to 103.0°F (39.4°C). During the next two days, increasing pain and swelling of the right knee occurred. He contacted his physician and was referred to the emergency department for evaluation that day.
Past Medical History
He had a history of bilateral nephrolithiasis, recent flank pain and lithotripsy. Stone analysis revealed calcium oxalate (70%) and calcium phosphate (carbonate-apatite) (30%). Urine cultures at the time of the procedure had grown mixed flora. A tuberculosis skin test had been negative the previous year.
Allergies
He had no allergies.
Social History
He lived alone and worked in a service industry.
Epidemiological History
He had not been sexually active in recent months. He smoked cigarettes, and did not drink alcohol or use illicit drugs. He had visited a rural area in New England two weeks before admission, but had not gone camping or hiking, and did not recall any insect bites. He had not traveled internationally in several years.
Physical Examination
On examination, the temperature was normal. The right knee was moderately swollen, without erythema, and there was tenderness with any range of motion. Knee flexion and extension were decreased. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 6400 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4500-11,000) (68% neutrophils, 19% lymphocytes, 10% monocytes, 2% eosinophils, and 1% basophils), erythrocyte sedimentation rate 21 mm per hour (ref. range 0-11), blood level of C-reactive protein was 87.7 MG/L (reference range < or = to 8, for inflammation), and the level of uric acid was normal.
A radiograph of the right knee demonstrated mild degenerative changes and a moderate effusion (Figure 1).
Figure 1. Radiograph of the right knee with effusion.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Arthrocentesis was performed in the emergency department, and 50cc of serous fluid was aspirated.
Analysis of the joint fluid revealed 16,250 white cells per cubic millimeter, with 80% neutrophils, 2% lymphocytes, 16% monocytes, and 2% macrophages or lining cells; no crystals were seen. Gram stain of the joint fluid revealed moderate polymorphonuclear leukocytes, few mononuclear cells and no organisms. Cultures of the joint fluid aspirate were obtained, and on the fourth day, one of two anaerobic bottles was positive. Culture of the organism is shown (Figures 2 and 3).
The patient was contacted and instructed to return to the emergency department. He reported persistent pain and swelling of his right knee.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The anaerobic culture of the joint fluid grew Mycoplasma species (Figure 3).
Figure 4. Colonies of Mycoplasma species (10x magnification) on ureaplasma differential agar (A7).
Treatment and Followup
The patient returned to the emergency department after culture of the initial joint fluid was positive and the organism identified as Mycoplasma species (thought to be Mycoplasma hominis). Arthrocentesis was repeated, and revealed brown fluid, with 66,250 white cells (98% neutrophils). The white blood cell count was 12,300 per cubic millimeter (71% neutrophils), erythrocyte sedimentation rate 69 mm per hour, and C-reactive protein 159.7 mg/L. Broad-spectrum antibiotics including vancomycin, ceftriaxone and azithromycin were administered.
The next day, arthroscopy for pyoarthrosis was performed in the operating room, with irrigation (18 liters of solution) and debridement of pre-existing articular cartilage damage in the right knee.
An infectious diseases consultation was obtained. Azithromycin was stopped and doxycycline begun, and vancomycin and ceftriaxone were continued until cultures were confirmed to be negative for other organisms. Testing for Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection of the throat and urethra and Chlamydia infection of the urethra was negative.
Discussion
This patient developed an acute monoarticular septic arthritis - a swollen painful knee with an effusion and a positive culture - shortly after a genitoureteral procedure, and despite the administration of cefazolin preoperatively. Analysis of the initial arthrocentesis aspirate revealed a leukocytosis, with no organisms on gram staining and no crystals seen, and without establishing either an infectious (bacterial, fungal or viral) or non-infectious (crystal-related or other reactive or inflammatory) etiology. On the fourth day, one of two anaerobic bottles grew Mycoplasma species, whose colonies had a characteristic “fried egg” appearance on agar [Meloni et al. 1980], as seen in Figures 3 and 4.
Mycoplasma, and specifically Mycoplasma hominis, in the family Mycoplasmataceae, is an uncommon cause of septic arthritis, but when present is more likely to occur in patients who are immunocompromised [Luttrell et al. 1994; Phuah et al. 2007]. Of cases of Mycoplasma septic arthritis, a review of the literature revealed 50% of cases (8 of 16) occurred after a structural abnormality or manipulation of the urogenital tract [Luttrell et al. 1994]. Delays in diagnosis were thought to be caused in part by the lack of staining by gram stain aniline dyes (which stain cell wall structures), the organism’s slow growth, and difficulties in detecting the organisms [Luttrell et al. 1994; Phuah et al. 2007] in culture bottles or the pinpoint and translucent colonies on agar (as seen in Figure 2 in this case). Mycoplasma’s lack of a rigid cell wall also contributes to its insensitivity to antibiotics that impair cell wall synthesis [Luttrell et al. 1994], a category that includes cefazolin, the antibiotic administered prophylactically before his urologic procedure.
Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Staphylococcus aureus are more common causes of acute septic arthritis and are organisms that have cell walls that stain with analine dyes; if present, either might have been more apparent in the gram stain of the knee aspirate. The prophylactic administration of cefazolin at the time of his procedure might have inhibited culture growth at the time of the first arthrocentesis; for that reason broad spectrum antibiotic coverage was administered on admission until repeat cultures were negative at 48 hours.
Haemophilus influenzae septic arthritis was historically seen more commonly in children and associated with H. influenzae type B in the pre-vaccination era, but its occurrence has diminished since the widespread use of vaccination. Though relatively uncommon in recent years, H. influenzae invasive disease is increasingly associated with noncapsulated or non-typeable isolates that are more antibiotic-resistant [Le Quellec et al. 2013; Dworkin et al. 2007].
Lastly, Chlamydia trachomatis, an obligate intracellular gram-negative organism, is the most common cause of reactive arthritis [Carter and Inman 2011] and of sexually transmitted bacterial infections (with more than 91 million new cases of the annually) [http://www.who.int/docstore/hiv/GRSTI/003.htm], and can present in a triad associated with non-gonococcal urethritis and conjunctivitis (also known as Reiter's syndrome, among other eponyms) [Carter and Inman 2011]. Reactive arthritis related to Chlamydia infections is thought to be under-recognized, and the syndrome and mechanism of disease are poorly understood [Carter and Inman 2011].
Final Diagnosis
Septic arthritis of the right knee caused by Mycoplasma species, after ureteral lithotripsy.
