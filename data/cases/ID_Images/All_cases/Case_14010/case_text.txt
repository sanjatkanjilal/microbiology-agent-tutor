History of Present Illness
A man in his fifties was admitted because of progressive rectal pain and urinary retention of one-month's duration. He also reported diarrhea, an eight-pound weight loss and intermittent night sweats, with no history of fevers, chills, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, melena or hematochezia.
Past Medical History
He had non-insulin dependent diabetes mellitus and diverticulosis that had been diagnosed by colonoscopic examination 18 month earlier.
Social History
The patient lived with his wife and children in an urban area and worked in education. He did not drink alcohol or smoke tobacco.
Epidemiological History
He owned dogs, a bird and snakes. He lived in the southwest United States and had traveled extensively to the Middle East, northern Africa and the western horn of Africa. The most recent trip was 2.5 years earlier to Tunisia, after which he had intermittent diarrhea for approximately 18 months, when his symptoms completely resolved.
Physical Examination
The patient did not look acutely ill or toxic. The blood pressure was 121/82 mm Hg, pulse 115 beats per minute, temperature 98.6°F (37.0°C) and respirations 18 breaths per minute, with oxygen saturation 94% while breathing room air. There was borderline tachycardia with regular rhythm and no murmurs, and tenderness to deep palpation in the lower mid-abdomen and pelvic area, with no appreciable masses. The remainder of the examination was normal. A urinary catheter was in place due to urinary retention.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 21,300 per cubic millimeter (reference range 3400-10,400), eosinophils 14% (reference range 0-5), hematocrit 36.0% (reference range 40.0-51.0 in men), and platelets 694,000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 150,000-425,000). A complete metabolic panel was normal. The blood level of C-reactive protein was 21.44 mg/dL (reference range <0.6), erythrocyte sedimentation rate 95 mm/hr (reference range 0-20), carcinoembryonic antigen <0.5 ng/ml (reference range in non-smokers 0.0-3.0), and cancer antigen 19-9 8 U/ml (reference range 0-37). HIV serology was non-reactive.
Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a large mass involving the proximal rectum and distal sigmoid colon with surrounding inflammatory changes and anterior displacement of the bladder.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was admitted to a general medicine floor. The gastrointestinal service was consulted and flexible sigmoidoscopy was performed. A large atypical circumferential mass was seen approximately 25 to 27 cm from the anal verge. The area was described as congested, friable and granular (Figures 1 and 2). Pathological examination of the surgical biopsies revealed marked mucosal eosinophilic infiltration with occasional lymphoid aggregates and no evidence of fungal elements, other microorganisms, lymphoma, or other malignancy; normal mast cells were present.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis (Figure 3) revealed a large soft tissue mass (Arrow A) involving a long segment of the rectosigmoid colon with extension into the anterior soft tissues to the seminal vesicles and prostate and posteriorly into the presacral space. A circumferential mass in the cecum below the ileocecal valve suggestive of cecal carcinoma was noted (Arrow B). In addition, multiple areas of enlarged bilateral external iliac, common iliac, retroperitoneal and mesenteric lymphadenopathy were seen.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Serological testing for coccidioidomycosis, strongyloidiasis and schistosomiasis by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay was negative, as were stool specimens tested for acid fast bacilli. Interferon-gamma release assay and chest radiograph were negative.
CT-guided biopsy of the rectal mass was performed. Pathological examination of the biopsy specimen showed extensive inflammation and fibrosis. Fungal organisms were present, confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining (Figure 4) and Grocott’s methenanine silver stain (GMS) ((Figure 5). The morphology of the fungal organisms was consistent with basidiobolomycosis. Several weeks later, a serological test result for basidiolomycosis returned and was positive.

Treatment and Followup
Itraconazole, 200 mg one tablet twice daily, was administered. He tolerated antifungal medication without any undesirable side effects. He was discharged from hospital, with gradual improvement in his symptoms. No surgical intervention was undertaken. He continued to follow up in the colorectal surgery clinic, as well as in infectious disease clinic.
At follow-up almost one year later, the patient had done well and remained symptom-free. An interval MRI of the abdomen and pelvis showed complete resolution of rectosigmoid colonic mass with no evidence of active inflammation. Therapy was discontinued after 12 months and symptoms recurred 2 months later. Repeat MRI of the abdomen and pelvis showed worsening inflammation. Itraconazole was resumed, with resolution of symptoms.
Discussion
The clinical presentation (gastrointestinal mass and eosinophilia), epidemiological history (residing in southwest US) and pathological and microbiological data are very suggestive of gastrointestinal basidiobolomycosis. The patient did not have pulmonary symptoms suggestive of disseminated tuberculosis or coccidiodomycosis. Furthermore, there was no evidence of intestinal ulceration, adhesion, fistula or strictures to suggest tuberculous enteritis. Intestinal schistosomiasis usually present with intestinal polyps and ulceration rather than infiltrative masses. Pathological/microbiological data were not consistent with lymphoma.
Basidiobolomycosis is a rare disease caused by the fungus Basidiobolus ranarum. B. ranarum is a microscopic fungus in the order of Entomophtorales of the class of Zygomycetes. This mycosis is found in tropical and temperate areas of South America, Africa and the Arabian Peninsula [Vikram et al. 2012]. It is found in soil, plant, decaying vegetables, feces of reptiles (frogs, toads, lizards) and bats. In the US, most of the cases have been found in Arizona [Lyon et al, 2001; CDC 1999]. In humans, B. ranarum is known to cause chronic subcutaneous nodules of the arm, trunk and shoulder. It also has been reported to cause granulomatous intestinal mass in the gastrointestinal tract of immunocompetent hosts. While traumatic inoculation is the most the common way of acquiring B. ranarum, in this patient acquisition was thought to be through ingestion, leading to gastrointestinal manifestation.
Clinical manifestations of gastrointestinal basidiobolomycosis include fever, weight loss, abdominal pain, abdominal mass, abdominal distension and constipation [Alhuraiji et al., 2014]. The fungus has a predilection to involve the colon and rectum, but it can also involve the small bowel, liver, gallbladder and the stomach. Complications of the disease include bowel obstruction, urinary retention, perforation, esophageal varices, duodenobiliary fistula and death [Vikram et al. 2012]. The differential diagnosis includes cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulitis, pyogenic abscess, tuberculosis, lymphatic filariasis or mycetoma [Alhuraiji et al., 2014.]
Risk factors for acquisition of gastrointestinal basidiobolomycosis include high risk activities (such as gardening or landscaping), ingestion of contaminated food product, gastric acid suppression, diabetes mellitus, gastrectomy, and residing in an endemic area [Vikram et al. 2012].
The diagnosis of basidiobolomycosis is often based on tissue culture. However, a probable diagnosis can be made based on histopathological examination of a biopsy specimen. A specific serologic immunodiffusion assay is available commercially, but its sensitivity is unknown. Marked peripheral eosinophilia occurs in the majority of patients, although the pathogenesis is poorly understood. The microscopic appearance of B. ranarum in tissue shows sparingly septate, broad, thin-walled hyphae with peaked zygospores. The histopathology of surrounding tissue shows a sheath of eosinophilic infiltration, referred to as the Splendore-Hoeppli phenomenon [CDC 1999]. Macroscopically on Sabouraud agar, colonies have a waxy, yellowish-to-gray appearance with many radial folds [Lyon et al. 2001].
Treatment of basidiobolomycosis is usually with ketoconazole, itraconazole or voriconazole. In vitro susceptibilities are variable and isolate dependent. In one study, Basidiobolus spp. displayed low minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) to all azoles. Another study showed high level resistance to fluconazole, amphotericin B, flucytosine, and capsofungin. Isolates were found to be susceptible to itraconazole, voriconazole and posaconazole. In some cases, surgical intervention is required. The mortality is estimated at 20% [Vikram et al. 2012, Guarro et al. 1999].
Final Diagnosis
Gastrointestinal basidiobolomycosis caused by Basidiobolus ranarum.
