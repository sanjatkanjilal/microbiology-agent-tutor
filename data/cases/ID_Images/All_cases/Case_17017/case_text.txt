History of Present Illness
A man in his forties with HIV who was virologically suppressed on antiretroviral therapy (ART) with a recent CD4 of 736 cells/uL (37%) presented with 7 days of diffuse, constant headaches, poor sleep and generalized irritability. He denied fevers, chills, night sweats, neck stiffness, photophobia or phonophobia, skin or genital lesions. He also complained of a "lump" in his rectum associated with painful defecation.
He was diagnosed with HIV about 10 years ago, and is currently on emtricitabine, tenofovir and ritonavir-boosted atazanavir. He had had several unprotected encounters with male partners recently and had a history of primary syphilis treated with a single dose of intramuscular benzathine penicillin G three years ago.
Allergies
Sulfamethoxazole (rash).
Epidemiological History
He had a 15 pack-year cigarette smoking history and also smokes crystal methamphetamine and marijuana. He has not travelled recently.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. Blood pressure was 115/82 mm Hg, pulse 65 beats per minute, temperature 97.5°F (36.3°C), and respirations 18 breaths per minute. Head, eyes, ear, nose, and throat exam (including fundoscopic exam) was unremarkable. Cardiac and pulmonary exam was normal. There was no neck stiffness. Abdominal examination revealed mild right upper quadrant (RUQ) tenderness not associated with rebound, guarding, nor a positive Murphy’s sign. Genitourinary and rectal examinations were unremarkable. There was no rash.
Studies
Liver enzymes were elevated, with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 56 IU/L (reference range 12-38 IU/L), alanine aminotransferase 132 IU/L (10-45), alkaline phosphatase 496 IU/L (25-100). Total bilirubin was elevated at 2.7 mg/dl (0.2-1.3). Rectal PCR was negative for chlamydia and gonorrhea.
His white blood cell count was 7,800 per mm3 with normal differential. Four sets of blood cultures were negative. Hepatitis B DNA and Hepatitis C viral RNA were not detectable by PCR method. Tuberculin skin test and serum interferon gamma release assay (Quantiferon TB gold test) were negative. Beta-d-glucan, serum histoplasma antigen and antibody screen, serum cryptococcal antigen and aspergillus galactomannan tests were negative. MRI of the brain with and without contrast revealed minimal parenchymal volume loss without evidence of focal lesions.
An ultrasound liver revealed an ill-defined hypoechoic mass (Figure 1). MRI of the abdomen showed an ill-defined 2.8-cm rim enhancing lesion with central necrosis in the left lateral hepatic segment (Figures 2a and 2b), corresponding to the ultrasound lesion and several smaller lesions were seen scattered throughout the liver and spleen; there was mild retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy.
CT-guided biopsy of the left hepatic lobe lesion was performed.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Two weeks after the liver biopsy was performed the patient returned with a new complaint of a non-pruritic, maculopapular rash, spread throughout his trunk, abdomen, upper and lower extremities (Figure 3). A single lesion was also seen on his left palm (Figure 4). He also complained of intermittent night sweats, feeling “feverish”, chills, myalgia and RUQ pain exacerbated by taking a deep breath. Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Serum Rapid Plasma Reagin (RPR) titer was newly elevated at 1:256. Core biopsy of the liver lesion revealed acute inflammation, consistent with necrotic abscess. Spiral shaped organisms were noted on Warthin-Starry silver staining (Figure 5). Aerobic, anaerobic, fungal, and AFB cultures were negative as was the flow cytometry and cytology.
Given his ongoing headache, lumbar puncture was performed. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed 20 white blood cells (92% lymphocytes), normal glucose and normal protein level. CSF bacterial culture was negative. The fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption test (FTA-ABS) in the CSF was positive but CSF VDRL (Venereal Disease Research Laboratory) was negative. He had a colonoscopy performed to investigate the ‘rectal lump’ which revealed a hyperplastic cecal polyp, colonic tubular adenoma and diverticulosis.
Figure 6. Warthin-Starry silver stain of liver biopsy (60x) demonstrating a spiral-shaped organism (arrow).
Treatment and Followup
The patient was diagnosed with syphilis associated with CNS involvement, hepatic abscess and splenic nodules. He was treated with 14 days of intravenous penicillin G at a dose of 24 million units per day. Liver function tests normalized within three weeks of completion of therapy. RPR, repeated four months after treatment, decreased to a titer of 1:4. There was complete resolution of his headache, proctitis and skin rash following treatment. Repeat liver ultrasound four months after therapy showed a decrease in the size of the left hepatic lobe lesion from 2.8 cm to 1.0 cm.
Discussion
Syphilis is caused by the spirochete, Treponema pallidum. The primary stage of syphilis presents as a single or multiple ulcerative lesion(s) at the point of entry, usually as genital ulcerative disease. If left untreated, syphilis progresses to the secondary disseminated stage and one third of these patients may silently develop latent stage syphilis, characterized by cardiovascular and/or CNS complications or gummatous destructive lesions over the ensuing 5-30 years. However, it is interesting to note that idea of distinct phases of primary, secondary and tertiary syphilis is being challenged, particularly in the HIV-infected population.
The incidence of syphilis in the US is increasing. Between 2015 and 2016, the number of reported primary and secondary cases in the United States increased by 18%, and there were 27,814 primary and secondary syphilis cases reported in 2016. Most (58%) of these cases were among men-who-have-sex-with-men (MSM). It is recommended MSM should be screened for STI at least annually and every 3-6 months for those at high-risk (eg. those with multiple or anonymous sexual partners). This is particularly true since many STIs, including primary syphilis, may be asymptomatic and may go unrecognized [1-3].
Syphilis infection is an independent risk factor for future HIV acquisition [8]. Rectal gonococcal or chlamydia infection can increase the risk of HIV seroconversion by up to eight times [9]. Therefore, all patients with syphilis should also be offered HIV screening [10].
The role of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) in the rise of the syphilis epidemic is currently debated [11]. Kojima et al. have reported that MSM populations taking PrEP for HIV prevention are up to 44.6 times more likely to contract syphilis infection than MSM population not on PrEP with other studies pending [12]. The use of doxycycline chemoprophylaxis against syphilis has been proposed as a prevention tool in high risk MSM [13], but this remains controversial.
The spirochetemia seen in secondary syphilis results in diverse clinical manifestations. This is illustrated in our patient who was found to have liver, spleen and CNS involvement. Syphilitic hepatitis often occurs with syphilitic ulcerative proctitis perhaps because spirochetes from the rectal area are thought to directly invade the portal circulation [2- 3, 4- 5, 6].
To our knowledge, hepatic abscess with concomitant extrahepatic visceral involvement has not been previously reported in patients with secondary syphilis, although syphilitic hepatitis has been well described. Unexplained cholestasis should alert the clinician to screen for syphilis in at-risk patients because it is a reversible cause of liver dysfunction with appropriate antimicrobial therapy [5]. The most prominent laboratory abnormality at the time of presentation is unexplained anicteric or icteric cholestasis with a marked elevation of alkaline phosphatase [3,5,7], mild elevations in liver transaminase levels and modest hyperbilirubinemia. Gamma glutamyl transferase and lactate dehydrogenase may be elevated as well.
The diagnosis of syphilitic hepatitis is made by the combination of relevant symptoms with cholestasis or other liver function test abnormalities and serological evidence for syphilis such as a positive RPR titer and a reactive FTA-Abs result or microhemagglutination assay result positive for T. pallidum (MHA-TP) [3, 7]. Spirochetes are often not readily visualized in tissue samples. In a case series of 7 HIV- infected patients with syphilitic hepatitis, a relationship between the RPR titer and the absolute CD4+ T lymphocyte count was noted [3]. The inference is that patients with higher CD4+ T-cell counts may be more likely than others to manifest clinical hepatitis as a result of a more robust inflammatory response in the periportal region [3]. Key histological findings in cases of syphilitic hepatitis include non-specific periportal hepatocyte necrosis, peri-cholangiolar inflammation and occasional visualization of spirochetes [3]. The relatively high CD4 cell count in our patient might have led to more intense inflammatory reaction to spirochetes with the resulting hepatic abscess.
The current recommended treatment for secondary syphilis is a single 2.4 MU of intramuscular benzathine penicillin [14]. Almost all patients with syphilitic hepatitis reported in the literature were treated successfully with penicillin. The reported duration of the therapy varied from 1 dose of intramuscular penicillin to 14 days of aqueous penicillin depending on the accompanying symptoms. Liver enzyme levels, in general, quickly normalized in syphilitic hepatitis even after one dose or a week of penicillin [5]. Diagnosis of neurosyphilis is rather challenging; it should be based on clinical and serologic findings. Typically, a positive CSF VDRL or positive FTA-ABS along with an elevated CSF protein and mononuclear pleocytosis are seen [15]. Lumbar puncture is necessary to establish the diagnosis especially in the context of neurologic, ocular and otic signs. Our patient received a total of 14 days of treatment because of the meningeal involvement.
To conclude, syphilis rates are rising across the US, especially among MSM. High risk MSM should be screened more frequently as signs and symptoms of syphilis can often be subtle and go unrecognized. Hepatic involvement due to spirochetemia is not uncommon, although liver abscess due to syphilis is rare. Physicians should have a low threshold to obtain a lumbar puncture to rule out neurosyphilis if clinical suspicion is high. MSM with symptoms of proctitis should be tested for STIs, including chlamydia, gonorrhea, HSV and syphilis.
Final Diagnosis
Syphilis with CNS involvement, hepatic abscess, and splenic nodules in a HIV-infected man
