History of Present Illness
2009 IDSA Fellows' Day Case Submission Summary: A man with fevers, sweats, decreased appetite, sore throat, aches and weakness for 2 weeks.
A man in his twenties was well until two weeks before presentation when fevers developed, and occurred intermittently thereafter, associated with diaphoresis, decreased appetite, mild sore throat, generalized weakness and diffuse body aches. One week before admission, he went to an urgent care clinic. A rapid test for streptococcal pharyngitis was negative, and he was sent home without antibiotic therapy. He took over-the-counter medications, without improvement. The following week, increasing weakness developed, associated with the sensation of loss of balance when walking. He presented for evaluation.
Past Medical History
He reported a history of a heart murmur since childhood. He reported no history of sexually transmitted diseases, and his human immunodeficiency (HIV) status was not known.
Medications
Medications included acetaminophen, diphenhydramine and phenylephrine.
Social History
He was single and worked in an office.
Epidemiological History
He was sexually active with men, without condom use, and reported 5 total lifetime partners. He did not smoke, drink alcohol or use illicit drugs. There were no sick contacts, pets or recent travel.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. The blood pressure was 121/68 mm Hg, pulse 102 beats per minute, temperature 38.7°C (101.7°F), and respirations 18 breaths per minute. Dentition was poor. A grade 3/6 systolic ejection crescendo-decrescendo murmur was heard loudest at the upper right sternal border and radiated to the carotid arteries bilaterally. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 20,000 cells/mm³ (reference range 4,400 -11,300), with polymorphonuclear leukocytes 85%, lymphocytes 9%, monocytes 6%, and basophils 0.1%). Hemoglobin was 9.4 g/dL, hematocrit 28.2 % and platelet count was 404,000/mm³. The levels of electrolytes and tests of renal and liver function were normal. A chest radiograph, including heart size, was normal, without evidence of pulmonary infiltrates.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was admitted to the hospital and remained febrile for 11 days with a maximal temperature of 39.1°C (102.4°F). A transthoracic echocardiogram showed thickening of the aortic valve, consistent with a vegetation (Figure 1). Blood cultures were obtained; gram stain of the blood culture is shown in Figure 2.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Blood cultures grew Neisseria gonorrhoeae, which was sensitive to ceftriaxone [0.12 S] and meropenem [0.03 S], showed intermediate response to penicillin [0.50I], and was resistant to ciprofloxacin [>=32 R]. Testing for antibodies to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) was negative and HIV nucleic acid was not detected.
A transesophageal echocardiogram also demonstrated a vegetation on the aortic valve. An aortic valve replacement was performed, and an extensive aortic root abscess was noted. Pathological examination of the resected valve showed a perforation of the aortic valve specimen and an acquired fusion of the valve leaflets, with rough edges due to vegetations (Figure 3). Treatment and Followup
The post-operative course was uncomplicated; the administration of intravenous ceftriaxone was begun 9 days before surgery and continued for 4 weeks following surgery.
Discussion
Gonorrhea was described by Hippocrates as early as the 4th to 5th B.C., as “strangury”, meaning obtained from the “pleasures of Venus”. In 130 A.D., the term “gonorrhea” (or “flow of seeds”) was coined by Galen. The association between carditis and gonorrhea was described in 1838, and the causative agent,Neisseria gonorrhoeae, was discovered in 1879. The incidence has decreased with the advent of antibiotics in the 1930s [Edwards and Apicella 2004]. In 2004, the incidence of gonorrhea was estimated to be 113.5 per 100,000 persons [CDC]. In the preantibiotic era, an estimated 11 to 20% of bacterial endocarditis was caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. There was a predilection for males, and the organism affected primarily the left-sided heart valves [Jackman and Glamann 1991]. Clinical manifestations of gonococcal endocarditis include fever (described as double quotidian) in 74%, arthralgia (44%), and rash (12%); affected patients may have had no antecedent history of urethral discharge. The organism has a tendency to be aggressive, which may lead to valvular destruction [Jurica and Bomzer 1987]. Studies in the post-antibiotic era report mortality of up to 19% despite medical and surgical treatment. Surgical intervention may be necessary in more than half of cases [Jackman and Glamann 1991]. In this case, persistent pyrexia despite antibiotic therapy and evidence of a valvular abscess prompted surgical intervention. Due to increasing antimicrobial resistance, fluoroquinolones are no longer recommended for the treatment of gonorrhea in the United States. Third generation cephalosporins are considered first-line therapy. In summary, endocarditis was once a common and often fatal complication of N. gonorrhoeae infection. Now, with the advent of antimicrobial treatment of gonococcal infection, it is far more rare, but may still occur, with potentially life-threatening consequences.
Final Diagnosis
Infective endocarditis and aortic valvular abscess caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
