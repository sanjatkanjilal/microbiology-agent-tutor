History of Present Illness
A female in her twenties presented with sore throat and fever in the summer. COVID-19 and rapid strep tests were negative, herpes simplex virus (HSV) throat swab returned positive at urgent care. A few days later she developed abdominal pain and presented to hospital, where she was still febrile and labs revealed transaminitis (ALT 1208, AST 1929) and leukopenia (White blood cell count 1.1). Her urine toxicology screen was negative and acetaminophen level was undetectable.

No significant past medical history

Oral contraceptive pill

Occupation: Works with young children in day care Substance use: No tobacco or recreational drug use; drinks alcohol socially Travel: Born and bred in Northeast United States, no recent travel; Central America a few years prior. Sexual: Had protected intercourse with a new male partner two weeks prior Living: Lives near a city in the Northeast US Vaccinations: Completed childhood vaccinations
Physical Examination
The patient was ill-appearing and flushed, but not jaundiced. Temperature was 102.7 F, BP: 121/81, HR: 100, RR: 22, O2 saturation: 97% on room air. She was alert and oriented to time, place and self, but was taciturn. Abdominal exam revealed no tenderness or hepatosplenomegaly. There was no palpable cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy and there were no oral or external genital ulcers. The remainder of the exam was normal.

Treatment and Followup
The patient was started on acyclovir 10 mg/kg intravenously every 8 hours with improvement in transaminases. However, on hospital day 3 she developed altered mental status. MRI brain with contrast was negative for acute changes and lumbar puncture was done, with cerebrospinal fluid containing 2 total nucleated cells/uL, lymphocytes 95 %, protein 33 mg/dL, HSV1 PCR 28624 copies/ml. Thus, HSV encephalitis was thought to be the cause of her change in mental status. Given the fevers, splenomegaly, cytopenias, hypertriglyceridaemia, hypofibrogenaemia, hemophagocytes on bone marrow biopsy, low NK cell activity, elevated ferritin and elevated IL2 receptor levels, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was suspected. Due to her clinical deterioration, dexamethasone was started and a dose of etoposide, followed by rituximab, was given, as part of HLH treatment protocol. The patient improved rapidly thereafter, with return of normal mental status and rapid decline in liver enzymes, improvement in cell counts, and decline in ferritin, as well as a decrease in serum HSV levels. Ultimately, she received two weeks of IV acyclovir, alongside one dose of etoposide, four doses of rituximab (spaced one week apart), and a two-month-long dexamethasone taper. Full HLH protocol was deferred due to the rapid improvement and risks of etoposide therapy. It was thought that she had a syndrome on the HLH spectrum, but perhaps not true HLH, due to the rapid recovery after a single etoposide dose. The two-week parenteral acyclovir course was followed by four months of high dose valacyclovir (1 gram orally three times per day), then four months of valacyclovir 1 gram oral daily. On discharge (hospital day 11) the patient was ambulating well and was asymptomatic. By three months post discharge the patient had undetectable serum HSV levels, normal liver enzymes and normal ferritin. EBV levels also became undetectable. She was back to her usual activity.
Discussion
Hepatitis is a rare manifestation of herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection, accounting for <1 % of causes of liver failure and <2 % of viral causes of liver failure overall [1]. Risk factors for this disease include immunosuppression, in the form of HIV, bone marrow or solid organ transplantation, malignancy, steroid use, and pregnancy [1,2,3]. However, it can also occur in immunocompetent individuals, in which scenario it is usually caused by a primary HSV infection [1]. Frequently, patients with HSV hepatitis do not have mucocutaneous lesions, and so the diagnosis is often delayed or missed [1]. The rate of progression to acute liver failure, and death, is high - up to 90 % mortality according to some sources [3]. Timely initiation of high-dose acyclovir therapy dramatically reduces mortality [1]. Therefore, the clinician should have a high suspicion for HSV hepatitis in a patient presenting with fever and transaminase elevation, even without typical herpetic skin lesions, and should have a low threshold to start acyclovir. In a case series by Norvell et al, factors associated with mortality were advanced age, immunocompromise, ALT level >5000, platelets <75,000, encephalopathy, coagulopathy and failure to start antiviral therapy [1]. The optimal duration of high dose acyclovir therapy is unclear, but should be for at least 7 days, and guided by clinical response [2]. Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is an even rarer complication of HSV infection and few case reports of this phenomenon exist in literature [4,5,6,7,8]. Again, it is associated with immunocompromise, or sometimes an underlying genetic mutation, particularly affecting interferon signalling and NK cell/ T cell function [4,9]. This entity also carries a high mortality. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is similarly known to trigger HLH, but in this case was thought not to be playing a key role, with the modestly elevated serum levels attributed to reactivation in the setting of acute illness [10]. In our patient, a basic outpatient immunologic work-up and testing for GATA2 deficiency, and other mutations associated with HLH, was negative. More detailed immunologic and genetic work-up is pending.
Final Diagnosis
HSV hepatitis with HLH-like syndrome secondary to primary HSV-1 infection; co-existent EBV reactivation
