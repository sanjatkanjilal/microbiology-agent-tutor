History of Present Illness
Summary: An elderly man with fatigue and fever.
An elderly man with stage IV non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) had recently received chemotherapy. He complained of fatigue for two days and a fever to 102°F (38.9°C) and chills on the day of admission. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest 2 weeks earlier had revealed pulmonary nodules for which he was started on voriconazole while awaiting tissue diagnosis. He reported having had temporary peripheral intravenous lines for chemotherapy, but denied having had any central lines.
Past Medical History
He had stage IV large cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) and diabetes mellitus.
Medications
Medications included chemotherapy (most recent course 2 weeks earlier) with cyclophosphamide, etoposide, vincristine, prednisone and rituximab (anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody). He was also taking voriconazole.
Physical Examination
He appeared chronically ill with a temperature of 102.5°F (39.2°C), pulse 130/minute, and blood pressure 78/52 mm/Hg. Examination of the lungs revealed decreased breath sounds at the right base. The rest of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count (WBC) was 5,300 cells/mm³ (neutrophils 63%, bands 22%, lymphocytes 0%, monocytes 15%).
Computed tomography (CT) of the chest showed two nodules in the right lower lobe: 1.7 centimeters (cm) by 1.9cm and 1.4cm by 2 cm. There was also a small right-sided pleural effusion (Figure 1).
Figure 1. Chest CT scan.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A CT-guided fine needle aspirate was done on a pulmonary nodule, but an insufficient amount of tissue was obtained. A gram stain of the positive blood culture revealed the findings seen in Figure 2.
Figure 2. Gram stain of blood culture.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The gram stain of the positive blood culture revealed branching gram-positive rods (Figure 2). A modified acid-fast stain performed on the blood culture also showed branching rods (Figure 3) that were identified as Nocardia asteroides.
Figure 3. Modified acid-fast stain.
Treatment and Followup
Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) was started, and the patient became afebrile within 24 hours. He was sent home on oral TMP-SMX, and did well clinically.
Discussion
This case illustrates the importance of obtaining a definitive diagnosis in an immunocompromised host with pulmonary nodules, since the differential diagnosis is very broad. For example, it is not possible to differentiate fungal disease from nocardiosis on the basis of imaging. An accurate diagnosis is essential in providing appropriate therapy.
A broad range of immunocompromised patients is at increased risk of infection with Nocardia. Risk factors include hematological malignancies, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, steroids and chemotherapy.
Nocardia bacteremia is unusual. In a recent study from the Massachusetts General Hospital, positive blood cultures accounted for 8.1% of the total Nocardia isolates seen between 1990-1997. Pulmonary involvement occurs in about 2/3 of patients with Nocardia bacteremia. The incubation times for the positive blood cultures ranged from 2-14 days, with a median time to growth of 4 days. Of note, 30% of patients with Nocardia bacteremia had concomitant bacteremia with other organisms, usually gram negative bacilli. This was probably due to the fact that many of the patients were predisposed to such infections by virtue of their immunocompromised state. Finally, patients with bacteremic nocardiosis appear to have similar presentation, risk factors, clinical course and therapeutic outcome as non-bacteremic patients with nocardiosis.
Final Diagnosis
Pulmonary nocardiosis (Nocardia asteroides) with bacteremia.
