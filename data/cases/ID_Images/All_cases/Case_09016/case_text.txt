History of Present Illness
2009 IDSA Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: An elderly woman with fevers, chest pain and dyspnea.
A woman in her seventies was admitted in the spring to a hospital because of fevers, chest pain and shortness of breath. She had been well until 6 days before admission, when subjective fevers, severe myalgias, pleuritic chest pain, shortness of breath, dry cough and frontal headache developed. She self-administered ibuprofen, without relief. She presented to an emergency department.
Past Medical History
There was a history of coronary artery disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hypothyroidism.
Medications
Medications included atenolol, hydrochlorothiazide, simvastatin, acetylsalicylic acid and levothyroxine.
Epidemiological History
She lived in a city in Minnesota, and spent most weekends in a cabin in a rural area where she gardened, but did not hike or camp. She owned a dog, and had no sick contacts, or recent tick bites, and had not traveled recently. She did not smoke or drink alcohol.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared ill and lethargic. The temperature was 101°F (38.3°C), blood pressure 80/50 mm Hg, pulse 110 beats per minute, respirations 26 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation 87% while breathing ambient air. There were diffuse crackles and decreased breath sounds at the bases of the lungs bilaterally and tachycardia; the remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 21,000/mm³ (reference range 4,000-11,000) with polymorphonuclear leukocytes 96%, lymphocytes 3%, and monocytes 1%, hemoglobin 11.9 g/dL (ref 13.3-17.7) and platelets 205,000/mm³ (ref 150,000-450,000). The level of creatinine was 2.6 mg/dL (ref 0.66-1.25 mg/dL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 343 IU/L (ref 0-55 IU/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 288 IU/L (ref 0-70 IU/L), alkaline phosphatase 151 IU/L (ref 40-150 IU/L), and total bilirubin 3.6 mg/dL (ref 0.2-1.3 mg/dL). INR and partial thromboplastin time (PTT) were 2.87 and 129 seconds respectively, (ref 0.86-1.14 and 22-37 seconds, respectively), fibrinogen 129 mg/L (ref 200-420 mg/dL), D-dimer was greater than 20 µg/mL (ref <0.5 µg/mL) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 9150 IU/L (ref 325-750 IU/L).
A radiograph of the chest demonstrated a pleural effusion on the left and bilateral perihilar infiltrates (Figure 1).

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Specimens of blood, sputum and urine were cultured. Imipenem, vancomycin and intravenous fluids were administered for presumed bacterial sepsis. Bi-level positive airway pressure (BiPAP) was initiated, however, the patient's hemodynamic and respiratory status deteriorated. The trachea was intubated and pressors administered. She was transferred by helicopter to a tertiary care center. A Wright stain of a peripheral blood smear is shown (Figures 2 and 3).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Peripheral blood smears (Figures 2 and 3) demonstrated numerous neutrophils with morulae consistent with human granulocytic anaplasmosis.
Treatment and Followup
Pulseless electrical activity developed. In consultation with the family, comfort measures only were administered, and the patient died shortly thereafter.
Results of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated numerous neutrophils with morulae consistent with human granulocytic anaplasmosis. Cultures of the blood, urine, and sputum remained sterile.
Discussion
Human granulocytic anaplasmosis is caused by Anaplasma phagocytophilum, an obligate intracellular bacterium that grows within membrane bound vacuoles in neutrophils. Ixodes ticks are the primary vectors, and white footed mice and deer serve as animal reservoirs. Incidence has been increasing in the United States over the past decade; the highest rates are seen in Minnesota, Connecticut, Wisconsin, New Jersey and New York (CDC).
Clinical presentation is variable, ranging from asymptomatic infection to fatal disease. The most common symptoms are fever, headache, myalgia, and malaise (Bakken et al. 1996; Bakken et al. 2006). Severe complications may develop including a sepsis-like picture, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and acute renal failure. Laboratory studies often reveal thrombocytopenia, elevated liver function tests, and leukopenia, although roughly 50% of patients will not have depressed white blood cell count (Dumler et al. 2005). Diagnosis can be made by detection of intracellular morulae on Wright or Giemsa-stained peripheral blood smear during the early stage of infection, although sensitivity ranges from only 20% to 80% (Bakken et al. 2006; Dumler et al. 2007). Demonstration of a four-fold increase in antibody titers from acute-to-convalescent serum or documentation of seroconversion is highly sensitive, but less useful in the acute setting (Aguero-Rosenfeld 2002; Bakken et al. 2002). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing is increasingly being used.
Treatment is with doxycycline, 100 milligrams twice daily, generally for 7 to 10 days. Rifampin can be used in patients with tetracycline allergy. Response to therapy is usually very rapid, with resolution of fever occurring within 24 to 48 hours.
Final Diagnosis
Human granulocytic anaplasmosis caused by Anaplasma phagocytophilum.
