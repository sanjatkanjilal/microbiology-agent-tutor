History of Present Illness
2011 IDSA Fellows’ Day Case Presentation Summary: A man with pain in the lumbar back and legs.
A man in his seventies developed progressive pain in the lumbar region extending to the both legs. He was in good health until approximately two years earlier, when intermittent fevers, chills, and malaise developed. Unintentional weight loss of 50 pounds (22.7 kilograms) occurred; the weight was regained over the next four months as his symptoms spontaneously resolved.
During the year before admission, pain developed throughout his body, particularly in the lower back, the buttocks, the joints, and both legs; the pain was eventually refractory to analgesics. During that period, he was evaluated by his primary care physician, and consultants in rheumatology, neurology, and infectious diseases; however, no definitive diagnosis or etiology for his symptoms was made.
His back pain became intolerable, and he was admitted to the hospital for further evaluation. There was no history of trauma to his back or bowel or urinary incontinence.
Past Medical History
He had a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus. Transitional cell bladder cancer (stage 0) was diagnosed 7 years before and treated with partial tumor resection and Bacille Calmette Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy; a recurrence occurred the following year, with subsequent resection and immunotherapy. He had also had bilateral knee replacements because of osteoarthritis.
Medications
Medications included gabapentin, cyclobenzaprine, methylprednisolone, amlodipine, lisinopril, omeprazole, fenofibrate and acetylsalicylic acid. He had also received three injections of epidural steroids.
Allergies
He had no allergies to medications.
Epidemiological History
The patient was born in the Midwest, and as a young adult lived in Europe for several years. He had worked as a firefighter and moved to the Southeast upon retirement. His hobbies included gardening and fishing. He had smoked cigarettes in the remote past, after smoking one pack of cigarettes per day for 20 years. He drank alcohol in moderation, and did not use illicit drugs or own pets.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared clinically stable. The blood pressure was 133/62 mm Hg, pulse 72 beats per minute, temperature was 96.9°F (36.1°C), and respirations 16 breaths per minute. Examination of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine revealed no visible abnormalities or tenderness to palpation. He did not have pain with straight leg raise in the sitting and supine position. Mild decrease in range of motion was noted at the hips and bilateral knees. Mild sacroiliac joint tenderness was noted on palpation. The remainder of the physical examination was normal.
Studies
The level of C-reactive protein was 2.4 mg/dl (reference range 0.0-1.0). Other initial laboratory results, including serum electrolytes, glucose, and tests of liver function, were normal.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the spine (Figures 1 and 2) revealed spondylosis and abnormal signal intensity in the lumbar disk between the 4th and 5th lumbar vertebrae (L4 and L5), the adjacent endplates, bilateral facet joints, and the left sacroiliac (SI) joint. A new large saccular infrarenal aortic aneurysm (52 mm in diameter) was also noted (Figures 3 and 4). Whole body bone scanning revealed focal increased uptake in the left inferior sacroiliac region, which localized to the SI joint. There was also focal increased uptake in the bilateral L4-L5 regions, right greater than left, which localized to the body and facet joints at L4-L5 bilaterally.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Aspiration of the left SI joint under computed tomography (CT)-guidance was performed. Vancomycin and ceftriaxone were administered empirically for the treatment of spondylodiscitis.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Mantoux tuberculin skin test was positive at 20 mm induration. The result of a tuberculosis interferon gamma release assay was negative. Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex PCR testing of periaortic tissue was negative; however, Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex DNA was detected in tissue specimens from the aortic node. Cultures from the left SI joint aspirate were positive for Mycobacterium bovis.
Aortic resection with infrarenal periaortic tissue debridement and replacement with a rifampin-soaked tube graft was performed. Pathological examination of the tissue revealed necrotizing granulomas (Figure 5) by hematoxylin and eosin and Movat staining.
Figure 5. Pathology of resected aorta.
Treatment and Followup
After initial identification of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex DNA in the aortic node, vancomycin and ceftriaxone were discontinued, and isoniazid, rifampin, and ethambutol with pyridoxine were administered. The back pain improved significantly with medical therapy.
Follow up MRI of the lumbar region revealed interval improvement of the osteomyelitis. However, his bladder cancer recurred. BCG instillations were no longer part of his future immunotherapy plans.
Discussion
The Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex is composed of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis), Mycobacterium microti, Mycobacterium africanum, and Mycobacterium bovis (M. bovis) [Abu-Nader and Terrell, 2002]. Whereas M. tuberculosis remains the cause of most human tuberculosis, M. bovis is the causative agent of tuberculosis in certain animal species, affecting only a small percentage of humans. In the 1920s, Calmette and Guérin developed the Bacille Calmette Guérin (BCG) vaccine to prevent tuberculosis, using a live attenuated derivative of a virulent strain of M. bovis. BCG is genetically and phenotypically similar to other species of M. tuberculosis complex as well as to other strains of M. bovis. Intravesical form of BCG was first introduced in 1976 for treatment of superficial transitional cell bladder cancer [Vegt et al. 1997]. In the 1980’s studies reported complete remission in more than 70% of patients with superficial and stage 1 bladder cancer. In 1990, the US Food and Drug Administration approved intravesicular BCG for the treatment of superficial bladder carcinoma.
The exact mechanism of therapeutic benefit of BCG in bladder cancer remains unknown; however, BCG has been postulated to stimulate an immune response that leads to granulomatous inflammation and tumor cell destruction. Intravesicular BCG has generally been well-tolerated. The incidence of significant adverse effects has been minimal. Reports of associated complications have included high fever (>39°C (102.2°F)), hematuria, granulomatous prostatitis, pneumonitis, or hepatitis, arthralgias, epididymo-orchitis, BCG sepsis, urethral obstruction, bladder contracture, renal abscess, and cytopenia. Complications with disseminated M. bovis-BCG infection, such as the mycotic aneurysms and musculoskeletal lesions in this case, are rare [Witjes et al. 2003].
Risk factors for metastatic spread of M. bovis -BCG include immune deficiencies or direct trauma to the bladder epithelium, which may occur during catheterization or tumor resection, or urethral injury during cystoscopy or instillation [Abu-Nader and Terrell, 2002]. Patients who undergo procedures such as transurethral resection of the prostate or biopsy within a few weeks of BCG instillation may also facilitate hematogenous spread. The discovery of viable BCG bacilli in biopsy specimens and morning urine cultures up to a year following intravesicular therapy might explain disseminated infection years after BCG instillation [Siatelis et al. 2011; Bowyer et a. 1995; Lamm et al. 1992].
In this case, the detection of M. bovis in culture of aspirate taken from the SI joint was considered proof of BCG-induced infection [Seelig et al. 1999]. A presumptive diagnosis of BCG dissemination was justified by the presence of concurrent vertebral osteomyelitis and aneurysm, until subsequent aortic tissue samples could be surgically obtained. The relationship between infected aneurysm and vertebral osteomyelitis is known to occur with tuberculosis infection via local extension from an adjacent infected focus or hematogenous spread [Rozenblit et al. 1996; Seelig et al. 1999]. Presumably, BCG infection follows a similar pattern. Multiplex polymerase chain reaction can be used for rapid and specific detection of BCG, including representatives of M.bovis -BCG, non-BCG M. bovis, and other species of the M. tuberculosis complex [Talbot et al. 1997].
All strains of M. bovis are intrinsically resistant to pyrazinamide [Abu-Nader and Terrell, 2002]; combination antimycobacterial therapy with at least isoniazid and rifampin (with inclusion of additional drugs such as ethambutol and other agents including quinolones with activity) are the cornerstone of therapy for this infection. If complications arise with BCG immunotherapy, BCG should be stopped immediately and subsequent BCG instillations should be cancelled. Triple anti-mycobacterial therapy is warranted in cases involving disseminated infection or organ involvement. Corticosteroids may be added as adjunctive treatment. Surgical intervention may be necessary, as in this case. Prophylaxis with isoniazid has not been proven to reduce the frequency of adverse effects associated with BCG instillation.
Final Diagnosis
Sacroiliitis, vertebral osteomyelitis and mycotic aneurysm caused by Mycobacterium bovis secondary to Bacille Calmette Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy for bladder cancer.
