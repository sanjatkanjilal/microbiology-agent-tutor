History of Present Illness
A female in her fifties with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) was admitted to the hospital for an acute lymphoid blast crisis. She was treated with hyper-CVAD (cyclophosphamide/ vincristine sulfate/ doxorubicin hydrochloride/ dexamethasone). Eighteen days after initiation of chemotherapy, she began to feel generally unwell with abdominal discomfort and diarrhea coinciding with her first day of count recovery. Over the next three days, she developed a diffuse macular rash and fever.
Past Medical History
Type II diabetes
Medications
Vancomycin 1g IV q12h (day 1 of therapy); Piperacillin-tazobactam 3.375g IV q6h (day 9 of therapy); Acyclovir 400mg PO q12h (prophylaxis); Fluconazole 400mg PO daily (prophylaxis)
Epidemiological History
She lived with her husband in a community outside the city, they have no pets at home. She had no history of travel outside of North America. She had many family members who came to visit her throughout her admission. She denied any smoking, alcohol use or illicit drugs.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared generally frail, with a low mood but no acute distress. Her blood pressure was 144/78, pulse 96 beats per minute, respirations 16 per minute with normal oxygen saturation. Temperature was 38.1°C (100.5°F), with maximal temperature of 39.1°C (102.2°F) two days prior to assessment. Cardiac and respiratory exam were within normal limits. The site of her indwelling peripheral vascular access had some erythema without fluctuance or purulent drainage. She had a diffuse papular erythematous rash on her posterior scalp which was non-tender and blanchable (Fig 1). This rash was also present on her abdomen, chest and thighs, though more petechial in appearance (Fig 2).

Studies
The total white blood cell count was less than 0.1 x 10^9/L (reference range (RR) 3.5-10.5), hemoglobin was 82 g/L (RR 115-155). Her platelets were less than 10 x10^9/L (RR 130-380). Electrolytes were normal, creatinine 89 umol/L (RR 22-75) or 1.01 mg/dL (RR 0.5-1.0). Her aspartate aminotransferase was 101 U/L (RR 15-37), alanine transaminase was 88 U/L (RR 17-62), alkaline phosphatase was 328 U/L (RR 50-136), gamma-glutamyl transferase was 352 U/L (RR 5-55) and total bilirubin 8 umol/L (RR 3-17) or 0.47 mg/dL (RR 0.2-1.2).
A chest radiograph revealed no acute intrathoracic pathology. Computed tomography of the chest showed mild interstitial pulmonary edema, but no infective focus in the lungs and no pulmonary embolism. Computed tomography of the abdomen showed fluid-filled distended loops of large bowel, with normal caliber small bowel loops. Ultrasound of the abdomen showed normal echogenicity of the liver and a normal biliary tree, with a moderate amount of ascites in the pelvis.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
After development of the rash, piperacillin-tazobactam was discontinued as it was suspected she had a drug eruption. She progressed to develop anemia (hemoglobin below 80 g/L) requiring two units of packed red blood cells, as well as progression of her diffuse papular facial rash. She underwent routine bone marrow aspiration and biopsy on day 3 as part of her chemotherapy reinduction protocol for treatment refractory ALL. Repeat induction chemotherapy (idarubicin and fludarabine) was initiated on illness day 6, and within the next week she developed severe fatigue, oligoarthritis (right ankle), progressing to polyarthritis (including the joints of her hands), as well as a purpuric rash on her legs (Fig 3). As her neutropenia worsened post-reinduction, she underwent septic work-up multiple times for fevers without an identifiable source. She required six more transfusions of packed red blood cells for anemia, as well as intermittent platelet transfusions for thrombocytopenia.

Treatment and Followup
On further questioning, the patient’s daughter had been diagnosed with “slapped cheeks” around the time of a recent visit, ten days preceding the development of symptoms, while the patient was neutropenic. This daughter had multiple young children, and according to the patient, at least half of them had a rash in the preceding week.
Intravenous immunoglobin was initiated on illness day 4 and repeated on illness day 11 and 18. Repeat parvovirus B19 PCR post-intravenous immunoglobulin remained positive (day 23), and the patient had ongoing symptoms including arthralgias, papular rash, severe fatigue, and ongoing neutropenia post-reinduction chemotherapy. This improved somewhat when her counts recovered on illness day 35.
Repeat bone marrow aspiration after count recovery did not show presence of inclusion bodies but did have non-specific staining of some trabecular cells for parvovirus B19. She was discharged home in morphological clinical remission.
She was assessed in outpatient Infectious Diseases clinic one month after discharge from hospital (day 67 of illness), with ongoing symptoms of significant fatigue but resolution of her arthralgias, fever and rash. Her parvovirus B19 PCR remained positive at this time.
Discussion
Humans are the only known host of parvovirus B19, which is an erythroid virus transmitted by respiratory route through close contacts (1). The initial phase of symptoms is generally non-specific (fever, malaise, diarrhea, rash) and coincides with viral replication in upper respiratory tract and concomitant viremia. The second phase of symptoms is immune-complex mediated, correlating with entry of virus into erythroid progenitor cells of the bone marrow (2, 3). In immunocompetent hosts, infection is often asymptomatic but can also include erythema infectiosum (“Fifth disease”) and transient arthropathy. Infection in immunocompromised hosts can present with transient aplastic crisis and pure red cell aplasia with persistent viremia (4, 5). Some patients may have mild elevation in markers of end-organ function, though severe end-organ damage is rare (6). Children most commonly exhibit cutaneous manifestations of disease, whereas arthralgias and arthropathy are more predominant in adults (7).
Chronic infection is often seen in patients with dysfunctional adaptive immunity, most commonly reported in patients with HIV not receiving antiretroviral therapy, patients with congenital immunodeficiency, and patients with underlying hematologic malignancies (3, 8-10). Ongoing symptoms correlate with lack of host immunoglobulin and inability to clear immune complexes.
The diagnosis of parvovirus B19 is often clinical. Serology may not always be positive in patients with immunodeficiency or may be confounded by administration of intravenous immunoglobulin (1). Serum PCR for viral DNA may be performed, and special staining can be performed on tissue biopsy (such as bone marrow aspirate and biopsy) to detect viral inclusion bodies (3).
Treatment of parvovirus B19 infection is supportive, and in cases of chronic infection there is some evidence supporting the value of pooled population intravenous immunoglobulin to assist with clearance of immune complexes, especially in cases of pure red cell aplasia (6, 11). It is recommended that patients with persistent viremia and associated immunodeficiency be maintained on respiratory droplet precautions (12). Generally, the rash of parvovirus B19 disappears within a few weeks in immunocompetent hosts, and immune recovery is pivotal for symptom improvement. There are case reports of recurrent erythema and viremia many months after the initial illness (13). Many patients will develop fatigue after infection, characterized by ongoing arthralgias despite optimal therapy.
Final Diagnosis
Chronic parvovirus B19 infection in immunocompromised host
