History of Present Illness
2009 IDSA Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A woman in her forties with nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and abdominal pain.
A woman in her forties with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) was admitted to a hospital because of nausea, vomiting, watery and non-bloody diarrhea and diffuse abdominal pain of two weeks’ duration. The abdominal pain increased with eating and was relieved by vomiting. There was no history of fever, chills or dyspnea.
Past Medical History
The patient had been diagnosed with HIV infection more than a decade earlier, and had AIDS. She was not taking antiretroviral medications because of gastrointestinal symptoms caused by the medicines, and was on no prophylactic medications. An ovarian cystectomy had been performed several months earlier.
Medications
She was not taking any medications at the time of admission.
Allergies
She had no known allergies.
Epidemiological History
There was no history of travel, exposure to sick contacts or pets.
Physical Examination
She appeared comfortable, in no acute distress. The temperature was 97.7°F (36.5°C), blood pressure 109/78 mm Hg, pulse 94 beats per minute, respirations 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 99% while breathing ambient air. The abdomen was soft, with bowel sounds. There was mild tenderness around the cystectomy surgical site, without guarding or rigidity. The remainder of the physical exam was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 3,400 cells per cubic millimeter (reference range 4,500-11,000), with neutrophils 84.9%, lymphocytes 8%, and monocytes 7.1%; the hemoglobin and hematocrit were normal. The serum level of sodium was 134 mmol/l (ref 135-141), potassium 2.4 mmol/l (ref 3.3-4.6), chloride 96 mmol/l (ref 101-110), and bicarbonate 27 mmol/l (ref 21-29), urea nitrogen 10 mg/dl (ref 6-22), creatinine 0.6 mg/dl (ref 0.5-1.1). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 142 U/L (ref 14-33), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 56 U/L (ref 10-42), alkaline phosphatase 59 U/L (ref 35-104), and total bilirubin 0.2 U/L (ref 0.2-1.0). The levels of amylase and lipase were normal. The level of CD4 T-cell lymphocytes was 27 cells per cubic millimeter and the level of HIV ribonucleic acid was 10,000 copies per milliliter by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Cultures of the blood for bacteria, including acid fast bacilli, were sterile.
Testing of stool samples for Clostridium difficile toxin, fecal leukocytes and acid fast bacilli (AFB) stain were negative, and stool cultures did not grow any bacterial pathogens.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The abdominal pain increased in severity and localized to the right upper quadrant. An ultrasound of the abdomen demonstrated diffuse increased echogenicity and a distended gallbladder, without a thickened gall bladder wall or pericholecystic fluid, which was suggestive of acalculous cholecystitis. A hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan did not visualize the gallbladder; the findings were thought to be suggestive of acute cholecystitis. A cholecystectomy was performed laparoscopically. Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A cholecystectomy was performed laparoscopically. Pathological examination of the specimen with hematoxylin and eosin stain revealed numerous organisms consistent with Cryptosporidium parvum (Figures 3 and 4, arrows). Examination of a stool specimen for ova and parasites was also positive for Cryptosporidium parvum.

Treatment and Followup
Following surgery, her abdominal pain and tests of liver function improved. Nitazoxanide was administered, with minimal improvement in diarrhea. She declined initiation of antiretroviral therapy, and was discharged to a skilled nursing facility for intravenous fluids and total parenteral nutrition.
Discussion
Cryptosporidiosis is a defining illness of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), if accompanied by diarrhea of more than one month’s duration [Fahey 2003, Schneider et al 2008]. It can lead to a severe, debilitating illness in severely immunocompromised patients with HIV/AIDS and CD4 T-cell lymphocyte counts of less than 100 cells/mm³. Biliary tract involvement is more common in advanced HIV disease with CD4 T-cell lymphocytes of less than or equal to 50 cells/mm³ [Vakil et al 1996]. Symptoms of cholangiopathy may include constant or intermittent right upper quadrant pain, nausea and vomiting. Abnormalities in the values of liver chemistries are commonly found. Infections commonly occur in warm moist months, transmitted by ingestion of contaminated drinking water or swimming in public swimming pools. Oocysts are resistant to killing by chlorination, but killed by heat.
Common radiologic findings of cryptosporidial biliary tract disease include acalculous cholecystitis and intrahepatic sclerosing cholangitis with papillary stenosis [Chen and LaRusso, 1999]. The increased mortality observed in patients with AIDS who have biliary disease has not been attributed to the disease itself, but to the degree of the underlying immunosuppression [Vakil et al 1996].
In the older literature, cryptosporidial biliary disease has only been described in immunosuppressed patients and occurred in 20-65% of HIV patients with AIDS cholangiopathy [Chen et al 1999].
The differential diagnosis includes disseminated Mycobacterium avium intercellulare complex, disseminated Cytomegalovirus infection, disseminated Mycobacterium tuberculosis, bacterial acalculous cholecystitis, Candidal cholecystitis and other causes of diarrheal diseases in HIV/AIDS such as Isospora, Cyclospora, and Microsporidium. Therapeutic options are limited. Drugs such as nitazoxanide may achieve symptomatic relief and parasitological cure in some immunocompetent individuals.
In patients with HIV who have CD4 counts greater than 50 cells/mm³, rates of parasitological cure are 63% as compared to 25% in the patients treated with placebo [Fox et al 2005]. In patients with CD4 T-lymphocytes levels less than or equal to 50 cells/mm³, no difference has been noted between treatment and placebo groups. Other therapeutic options such as paromomycin or spiramycin have had disappointing treatment results [Abubakar et al 2007]. Sphincterotomy has not been associated with relief of pain, but cholecystectomy is indicated in patients with cholecystitis [Vakil et al 1996]. Highly active antiretroviral treatment (HAART), with restoration of the CD4 count, is the most effective treatment. HIV-infected patients should avoid drinking tap water and contact with persons with diarrhea, farm animals and domestic pets [CDC 2009].
Final Diagnosis
Disseminated cryptosporidiosis caused by Cryptosporidium parvum, presenting as cryptosporidial acalculous cholecystitis, in a patient with with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).
