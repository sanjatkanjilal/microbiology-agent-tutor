History of Present Illness
A previously well man in his fifties presented with slowly progressive, bilateral lower extremity skin lesions over four weeks. He had sustained minor superficial skin abrasions while swimming in a hot spring and hiking in the Southwest US six days prior to first noticing the skin lesions. These were initially “purplish marks”: one seen on his left medial calf, two on his distal right medial calf and one more proximal on his right calf. He was initially treated with doxycycline, then amoxicillin-clavunate for presumed cellulitis but the leg lesions continued to enlarge and ulcerate. He also developed a burning pain in his right calf and he was sent to the emergency department. He had no other symptoms associated with the progressive skin lesions, denying fevers, chills, night sweats, weight loss and swollen lymph nodes.
Medications
Doxycycline and Amoxicillin/clavulanate
Epidemiological History
He is an office-worker, an ex-smoker and occasionally drinks alcohol. He has multiple pets including a dog, multiple reptiles, birds and fish. He denies any unusual dietary habits such as raw meat or unpasteurized dairy.
Physical Examination
The patient was a well-appearing adult male in no acute distress. The blood pressure was 131/87, pulse 77 beats per minute, temperature 97°F (36.1°C), and respirations 20 breaths per minute. The examination was significant for the presence of two well circumscribed indurated, erythematous plaques approximately 2x2 cm and 10x6 cm in diameter on the right lower extremity, the larger plaque was medial and proximal to the ankle and the smaller plaque anterior and distal to the knee. The inferior lesion had central ulceration and necrosis [Fig.1]. An approximately 3x3 cm extremely indurated, mildly erythematous, mildly scaly plaque was present on the medial left lower extremity proximal to the ankle [Fig. 2].

Studies
The white blood cell count was 10,800 cells/microliter (reference range 3.8-10.6 x10 3 cells/microliter) with mild eosinophilia at 7.2% (reference range 0-7%) and an absolute eosinophil count of 780 x10 3 cells/microliter. The platelet count was elevated at 463,000 cells/microliter (reference range 150-440 x10 3 cells/microliter). The serum creatinine was 1.0mg/dL (reference range 0.7-1.2 mg/dL). The alkaline phosphatase was elevated at 102 U/L (reference range 32-91 U/L) and gamma-glutamyl transferase was elevated at 201 IU/L (reference range 7-50 IU/L). Total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase levels were within normal range. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was elevated at 27 mm/hour (reference range 0-20 mm/hr). Other routine laboratory test results were normal.
A venous Doppler ultrasound of the right lower extremity revealed no evidence of deep vein thrombosis but did reveal severe subcutaneous edema of the distal right leg worse along the medial aspect of the ankle.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Skin biopsies revealed ribbon-like hyphae with rare septae on Grocott’s methenamine silver (GMS) stain [Fig. 3]. Tissue samples were plated directly onto Sabouraud-dextrose agar and on day 3 of culture, fine, white hyphae were noted to be growing on agar multiple plates [Fig. 4]. Pythium insidiosum was identified via 18S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing at the reference laboratory of University of Texas, San Antonio.

Treatment and Followup
Treatment with amphotericin B and posaconazole was initiated and multiple surgical debridements were performed. Necrotic fascia and muscle deep to the distal right and left lower extremity skin lesions was identified and debrided. Prior to the identification of Pythium insidiosum, he was discharged on posaconazole.
At his outpatient follow-up, a week post-discharge, the previously debrided lesions looked worse. He was readmitted and underwent additional exploration of his wounds. Extensive tissue necrosis was noted surrounding the previously-debrided right lower extremity wound which necessitated a below-the-knee amputation. Tissue samples sent for histopathology were notable for abundant ribbon-like hyphae with rare septae on GMS stain [Fig. 5].
An immunotherapeutic veterinary Pythium antigen vaccine was administered along with transition to itraconazole, terbinafine, minocycline, and caspofungin. Six months post his right below-knee amputation, he has no ongoing evidence of pythiosis.

Discussion
Pythium insidiosum is a fungus-like parasitic oomycete found in standing water which mainly infects non-human mammals (1). Human pythiosis has been associated with hemoglobinopathies and has only rarely been reported in North America (2, 3). Human pythiosis occurs predominantly in Thailand (2, 4). Human cases have also been reported in South America, India, Australia and Europe (5, 6, 7, 8). Four clinical syndromes have been described: cutaneous/ subcutaneous, vascular, ocular and disseminated (2). According to a retrospective analysis of 102 pythiosis cases in Thailand the mortality rates of cutaneous/subcutaneous and ocular pythiosis may be low although treatment often involves aggressive surgical debridement and enucleation (2). In contrast, the vascular and disseminated forms carried a 40% and 100% mortality rate, respectively (2).
Of the reported cases, 75% had careers in agriculture and 80% had an underlying hematologic disease (2). Testing for underlying predisposing conditions for pythiosis including hemoglobinopathies, acquired and primary cell-mediated and humoral immunodeficiency, and phagocytic dysfunction were unremarkable in our patient. Most cases have been linked to exposures to natural bodies of water and we suspect our patient was exposed when he waded in a hot spring six days prior to noticing his skin lesions. The state department of health was notified of the case and investigated the hot spring where the exposure was thought to have occurred. No environmental samples were taken.
This case highlights several important features of human pythiosis. It is frequently mistaken for mucormycosis due to similar histopathologic appearance (3). Little is known about managing this disease due to its rarity in human hosts. Treatments are largely based upon limited case reports and series. Pythium insidiosum is resistant to traditional antifungals as it lacks ergosterol in its plasma membrane (1). However, combination therapies may provide synergistic activity against this organism (9, 10). An experimental immunotherapeutic Pythium insidiosum vaccine developed for equine and canine pythiosis can be utilized in human cases (2). Most importantly, early and aggressive surgical debridement with confirmed negative margins is necessary for effective treatment (2).
Final Diagnosis
Pythiosis
