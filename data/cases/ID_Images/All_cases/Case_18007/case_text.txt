History of Present Illness
A Colombian man in his seventies presented with a history of weight loss, fever, decreased appetite, night sweats, and new cutaneous ulcerations involving his arms, legs, and abdominal wall over a period of 4 weeks on a background of multibacillary leprosy 30 years prior, which had been treated with dapsone and rifampin for 1 year. He also reported numbness and paresthesia in the upper and lower extremities and 3-4 weeks of cough productive of yellow sputum. He denied hemoptysis.
He has insulin-requiring diabetes.
Epidemiological History
He worked in healthcare in Columbia and is now retired. He has no pets and no travel history outside of Columbia.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. The blood pressure was 160/80 mm Hg, pulse 88 beats per minute, respirations 22 breaths per minute, and temperature 102.2°F (39°C) during initial hospitalization. Lungs revealed bibasilar crackles and coarse breath sounds anteriorly.
Skin exam revealed multiple ulcerated lesions on the elbows, abdomen, and chest wall with surrounding erythema (Figures 1 and 2). Both lower limbs had multiple hypopigmented confluent macules and he had deformed ankle and feet joints consistent with Charcot joints (Figure 3). Neurological exam revealed decreased sensation in the bilateral upper and lower extremities (including temperature, light touch, vibratory, and proprioception) and decreased reflexes in bilateral upper and lower extremities. He also had thickened palpable nerves in the epitrochlear fossae bilaterally and in the neck. The exam was negative for hepatosplenomegaly and cervical, axillary, or submandibular lymphadenopathy. His exam was otherwise normal.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
His sputum was smear positive with 3+ acid-fast bacilli (AFB). Molecular PCR and culture from sputum was positive for M. tuberculosis (susceptible to rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol).
Histopathological examination of skin biopsies demonstrated the occurrence of a Type 2 reaction in a patient with previous history of treated multibacillary disease. Skin biopsy showed a high bacillary index, prominent neutrophilic infiltrate and AFBs were present in the perineurium (Figures 5 and 6). A PCR from the skin biopsy (sent to National Hansen's Disease Program, Carville, Louisiana) was positive for M. leprae.

Treatment and Followup
The patient was initially treated with rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol plus clofazimine via directly observed therapy in addition to dapsone 100 mg daily. Sputum cultures remained positive for tuberculosis at 2 months. Pyrazinamide and ethambutol were later discontinued after his AFB smear and culture converted to negative. Isoniazid is currently being continued for a total of 7 months (with rifampin) to complete tuberculosis treatment. Rifampin, dapsone, and clofazimine are being continued for a minimum of 2 years for treatment of leprosy, with plans to obtain repeat skin biopsy to assess for decreasing bacillary load. He also received a prednisone taper beginning at 80 mg daily (with close glucose monitoring) later decreased to 20 mg daily for treatment of a type 2 leprosy reaction (erythema nodosum leprosum).
Discussion
Our patient was diagnosed with acute pulmonary tuberculosis and a Type 2 leprosy reaction, also known as erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL). Leprosy reactions are immunological reactions that occur frequently among patients with Hansen’s disease (leprosy) at any point, including prior to the initiation of multidrug therapy, during therapy, or even years after completion of therapy, often producing significant neurologic sequelae. A type 1 reaction, also known as a reversal reaction, occurs due to an intensification of cell-mediated immunity (1). This presents as increased acute erythema, warmth, edema, and occasional ulceration of pre-existing cutaneous plaques and nodules, without systemic involvement and is usually paucibaciliary (1). Type 2 reactions (ENL) result from antigen-antibody complex deposition, leading to new subcutaneous nodules which are painful, and tender and are accompanied commonly by systemic symptoms of fever, malaise and lymphadenitis (1). This is characterized by a neutrophilic infiltrate (as seen here) accompanied by vasculitis and/ or inflammation of the subcutaneous layer of fatty tissue (panniculitis) (1).
Leprosy remains an important neglected tropical disease in Colombia. Leprosy can lead to long-term complications which most commonly involve the peripheral nerves and skin. It can also affect the eyes, bones, testes, and mucous membranes (2). As this case illustrates, co-infection of M. leprae with other mycobacterial infections, such as tuberculosis, may facilitate the occurrence of late leprosy reactions or possibly clinical relapses of leprosy (3,4). Recent evidence suggests that the occurrence of severe leprosy reactions or severe forms of leprosy including Lucio’s leprosy may be due to co-infection between M. leprae and other mycobacteria including Mycobacterium lepromatosis (5-7). Lucio’s leprosy is uncommon and is characterized by hemorrhagic plaques due to mycobacterial invasion of endothelia (1), and this type of leprosy did not occur in our case. Co-infected patients require long-term clinical monitoring for severe neurologic sequelae, late leprosy reactions, and adverse effects of long-term anti-inflammatory use.
According to WHO guidelines, no additional laboratory tests are recommended if a patient meets one of three common signs: (i) definite loss of sensation in a pale (hypopigmented) or reddish skin patch; (ii) thickened or enlarged peripheral nerve with loss of sensation and/or weakness of the muscles supplied by that nerve; or (iii) presence of acid-fast bacilli in a slit-skin smear (8). Steroids were utilized to prevent further sensory loss in our case and are often initiated to prevent further nerve injury (1). Clofazamine was included in the current recommended regimen but is commonly added as a steroid-sparing agent (1). In Colombia, the rate of drug-resistant tuberculosis is 1.2% in 2016 (9). Prospective monitoring of patients with Hansen’s disease may help define the impact of mycobacterial co-infections on the severity and spectrum of disease of leprosy.
Final Diagnosis
Mycobacterium leprae and Mycobacterium tuberculosis co-infection
