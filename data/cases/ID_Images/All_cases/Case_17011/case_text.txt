History of Present Illness
A man in his forties with HIV/AIDS diagnosed one year ago (nadir CD4 of 25 cells/uL and a current CD4 count of 55 cells/uL) was admitted to the hospital with a new tonic-clonic seizure and altered mental state. He was on tenofovir, emtricitabine and efavirenz as well as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and azithromycin as prophylaxis but had been non-adherent for the last 6 months and had been lost to follow-up.
Past Medical History
He had no other past medical history. No drug allergies.
Epidemiological History
The patient was born, bred and was living in Paraguay. He reported no domestic or international travel. He denied injection drug use or history of blood transfusions. He is a heterosexual but has not been sexually active in the last year. He had no animal exposures.
Physical Examination
On examination, the patient was cachectic, responsive, but oriented to person only. His blood pressure was 105/70 mm Hg, pulse 120 beats per minute, temperature 96.8ºF (36.0 º C), and respirations 22 breaths per minute. He was able to follow simple commands and exhibited mild dysarthria. The reminder of the physical examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 2410/uL (reference range 4500–11000), lymphocyte count was 650/uL (1000-4800), CD4 was 55 cells/uL (5%), and HIV viral load 95,350 copies/mL. The remainder of the blood work was unremarkable.
A non-contrast CT scan of the head showed a lesion in the medial frontoparietal region which was centrally hypodense, peripherally hyperdense, and with surrounding edema (Figure 1).
Figure 1. CT Brain (non-contrast) at admission demonstrating a large hypodense lesion in medial frontoparietal area with peripheral enhancement and surrounding edema (red arrow).
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A lumbar puncture was performed which showed a clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with a normal opening pressure of 100 millimetre of water (reference range 60-200), white blood count of 13 cells/uL (100% lymphocytes), elevated protein level of 87 mg/dL (15-45), and low glucose level of 37 mg/dL (50-80); serum glucose was 106 mg/dL (<100).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Direct examination of CSF showed numerous flagellar organisms, consistent with trypomastigote form of Trypanosoma cruzi (Figures 2a and 2b, Video 1). India ink staining was negative for Cryptococcus. Result of serum Trypanosoma cruzi enzyme linked immunoassay (ELISA) was positive and anti-Toxoplasma IgG was negative.

Treatment and Followup
The patient was started on benznidazole 5mg/kg/day (divided in 2 doses). Antiretroviral therapy with tenofovir, emtricitabine and efavirenz and prophylactic trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole were also re-started. The patient's mental status improved within 72 hours, and he was discharged home to complete 60 days of benznidazole. Five months after diagnosis, the patient had steady neurological improvement with decreased frequency of convulsive episodes; repeat CD4 count was 248 cells/uL and HIV viral load was undetectable. A follow-up CT scan done 10 months after the diagnosis of Chagas meningoencephalitis revealed calcification of the lesion in the medial frontoparietal region with an area of encephalomalacia and a right calcified frontal lesion that was not apparent in his initial imaging study (Figure 3).

Discussion
Chagas disease, or American trypanosomiasis, is a parasitic infection caused by the protozoan Trypanosoma cruzi (T. cruzi).[1] It is primarily transmitted by hematophagous “kissing bugs” insects of the family Reduviidae and subfamily T riatominae, also known as vinchuca (Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, and Paraguay), barbeiro (Brazil), chipo (Venezuela), and chinche (Central America).[2] The vectors live in cracks and holes of rustic homes and acquire the trypomastigote forms of the parasite after feeding on blood from infected humans or animals (armadillos, marsupials, domestic animals).[2]
The ingested trypomastigotes transform into epimastigotes in the triatominae’s midgut where they multiply and differentiate into metacyclic tripomastigotes. Humans become infected when the vector defecates during or immediately after its blood meal and fecal material containing metacyclic trypomastigote is inoculated through mucosal membranes or skin breaks.[3] The metacyclic trypomastigotes invade cells from tissues and inside the cells they can transform in amastigotes which can continue intracellular replication or be released into the circulation as bloodstream trypomastigotes.[4] Chagas disease transmission can also occur via consumption of contaminated food or drink, transfusion of blood products, organ transplantation, intravenous drug use or by the transplacental route.[1] Current estimates suggest that 5-18 million people are infected. Previously confined to rural areas of Central and South America where the vector is endemic, the present distribution includes areas of the United States and Europe due to migration.[5]
The clinical manifestations of Chagas disease vary according to whether the infection is acute, chronic, or a reactivation of chronic infection. The acute phase of Chagas disease infection can be subclinical or associated with nonspecific systemic symptoms (fever, malaise, anorexia) and inflammatory reaction at the inoculation site (known as chagoma).[6] Trypomastigotes may be visualized in the blood during 8-12 weeks of the acute phase. Approximately 70% of patients will progress to a chronic “indeterminate” phase, during which individuals are asymptomatic and anti- T. cruzi antibodies can be detected in the absence of parasitemia.[6] Thirty percent of patients will develop clinical manifestations of chronic infection such as gastric motility disorder and cardiomyopathy.[1]
Reactivation of chronic T. cruzi infection is rare, but may occur in immunosuppressed individuals.[7] In HIV-infected patients with chronic T. cruzi, reactivation can lead to high morbidity and mortality. In patients with a CD4 count < 200 cells/uL, meningoencephalitis or focal cerebral abscess (chagoma) is the most common reported manifestation of reactivation.[8,9] Clinical and radiographic CNS manifestations during reactivation are nonspecific and can mimic other opportunistic infections, such as toxoplasmosis, tuberculous meningitis, cryptococcosis, or neoplastic processes. Because it shares significant epidemiological, clinical, and radiographic similarities with Toxoplasma gondii, cerebral T. cruzi should be considered in the differential diagnosis of meningoencephalitis syndromes and CNS lesions in HIV-infected patients with substantial exposure to endemic areas or pertinent risk factors in whom cerebral toxoplasmosis is suspected.
Diagnosis of Chagas reactivation is achieved by direct visualization of the parasite by microscopy of fresh anticoagulated blood or buffy coat.[1] In cases of meningoencephalitis, trypomastigotes may be seen in direct examination of the cerebrospinal fluid and amastigotes may be found on histological examination of brain tissue.[10,11] Because T. cruzi meningoencephalitis is relatively uncommon, most cases are initially misdiagnosed as toxoplasma encephalitis. In the appropriate epidemiological setting, parasitological or histopathological studies for diagnosis of T. cruzi reactivation should be considered at the time of patient’s initial presentation and among those with inadequate response to toxoplasma therapy to avoid delay or erroneous treatment.
The optimal treatment in immunocompromised individuals with Chagas disease reactivation remains uncertain.[1] Treatment with benznidazole or nifurtimox are currently used; both drugs that have proven to be efficacious in non-immunocompromised patients.[12] Because of the better side effect profile and the reportedly better clinical outcomes in a small series of treated HIV-infected patients with T. cruzi infection, standard dose of benznidazole 5 mg/kg daily divided into two doses for 60 days is commonly used as first line treatment.[6,8] Reported adverse effects include dermatitis, peripheral neuropathy, and bone marrow suppression. There are no well documented cases of Chagas-associated immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) in patients infected with HIV following initiation of antiretroviral therapy; therefore, the current recommendation is to start antiretroviral therapy as soon as the patient is clinically stable.[14]
The appropriate follow up evaluation for monitoring response to treatment in patients with CNS Chagas reactivation has not been formally evaluated. Clinical evaluation and direct examination of blood or buffy coat can be used to monitor treatment failure.[1] The role of radiologic monitoring after initiation of treatment is unclear. A small number of case reports have described partial regression of brain lesions, calcification, and encephalomalacia in neuroimaging studies obtained after initiation of therapy.[3,15]
In conclusion, T. cruzi should be considered as a differential diagnosis in HIV-infected patients presenting with meningoencephalitis or space-occupying CNS lesions who are from endemic areas or have relevant risk factors. Clinicians should be familiar with clinical manifestations and diagnostics of T. cruzi CNS reactivation because it may mimic opportunistic infections commonly found in immunosuppressed persons. Delay in diagnosis can lead to neurologic sequelae or mortality.[3]
Final Diagnosis
Meningoencephalitis and chagoma, caused by Trypanosoma cruzi
