History of Present Illness
An African American heterosexual male in his 40s with a history of HIV presented to a local emergency room with complaints of a worsening skin rash. His rash had reportedly been progressing over the past few weeks with involvement of his face, scalp, torso, and legs. Review of systems was also positive for a mild cough, nasal congestion, and a recent nosebleed but was otherwise unremarkable.
Past Medical History
The patient’s medical history was significant for HIV, diagnosed about 5 years prior to presentation. He was initially on coformulated efavirenz/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine, but it was discontinued due to nightmares. He was not taking ART until about two months prior to admission when an outside provider had re-initiated therapy with darunavir/cobicistat and tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine. Pre-ART laboratory data demonstrated an HIV RNA of 2.13 million copies/mL and an absolute CD4 count of 4 cells/cubic mm.
Medications
Darunavir/cobicistat, tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine, and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim
Epidemiological History
The patient had previously been incarcerated until release a few months prior to admission. No reported travel outside the United States. He is a current smoker but denies alcohol or illicit drug use.
Physical Examination
The patient was thin and chronically ill-appearing. Temperature 100.6 degrees Fahrenheit, BP 105/63, HR 117, respiratory rate of 29. Pertinent findings on physical examination included a diffuse rash with hyperkeratotic plaques on the forehead, bilateral cheeks, nose, and upper lip with erosions and hemorrhagic crusting. (Figures 1-3). Erosions were also noted on the hard palate, tongue, and buccal mucosa bilaterally. No organomegaly or lymphadenopathy was appreciated on examination. Figure 1. Studies
The patient had a white blood cell count of 4.9 TH/cmm (normal value: 4.0-10.00 TH/cmm) with an absolute lymphocyte count of 0.18 TH/cmm (normal value: 1.0-3.0 Th/cmm). Hemoglobin was 6.8 g/dL (normal value: 13.0- 17.0 g/dL). Platelet count was 3 TH/cmm (normal value: 150-400 TH/cmm). ESR was elevated to 119 mm/hr (normal value 0.0-10.0 mm/hr), and CRP was 11.5 mg/dL (normal value: 0.0-0.5 mg/dL). Liver function tests, aside from albumin (2.6 g/dL; normal value: 3.5-5.5 g/dL) were unremarkable.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
On admission, the patient was started empirically on broad spectrum antibiotics, acyclovir, and fluconazole with minimal improvement of skin lesions. The infectious diseases team recommended the addition of oral ivermectin while awaiting workup results that included HIV RNA, RPR, serum Cryptococcus antigen, urine Histoplasma and Blastomyces antigens. Dermatology and otolaryngology were consulted and performed multiple skin biopsies. ART was continued with bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
An updated HIV RNA was 202 copies/mL, and the patient’s absolute CD4 count was 20 cells/cmm (14%). A urine Histoplasma antigen was positive at >23 ng/mL. A Histoplasma antibody test was positive with a titer of 1:128. A urine Blastomyces antigen was also positive at 12.45 ng/mL, which we suspect was the result of cross-reactivity between the Histoplasma and Blastomyces antigen tests. The Blastomyces antibody was negative along with negative serum Cryptococcal antigen, RPR, and HTLV-1/2 antibody testing. Pathologic evaluation of all skin biopsies (right earlobe, right forearm, and lip) showed evidence of Histoplasma infection (Figure 4 and 5). The organism also grew on fungal culture several weeks after collection. The isolate was sent to a fungal reference laboratory and confirmed to be Histoplasma capsulatum.

Treatment and Followup
Urine Histoplasma antigen returned positive and the pathology report described yeast-like organisms consistent with disseminated Histoplasmosis (figure 4 and 5). All other antimicrobials were discontinued, and the patient was started on intravenous amphotericin B. Due to steady improvement over a 2-week period, he was transitioned to oral itraconazole for continued management. By the time of discharge, the patient had experienced modest improvement in his skin lesions, and outpatient follow up was arranged.
Two weeks after discharge, the patient was re-admitted with nausea and intractable abdominal pain. CT imaging demonstrated a 5-cm mass in the right lobe of the liver. Biopsy revealed hepatocellular proliferation, sinusoidal dilation, and congestion. No microorganisms were identified by staining and cultures from the liver mass were negative. The patient was discharged home after significant symptomatic improvement to continue ART and antifungal therapy.
Less than two weeks after the second admission, the patient returned with complaints of shortness of breath at rest and on exertion, orthopnea, and lower extremity edema. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed an ejection fraction of 15%. An EKG was significant for a QTc of 551. The patient subsequently improved after intravenous diuresis and was discharged home on guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure with plans to transition from itraconazole to posaconazole after his QTc improved with outpatient follow up with Infectious Disease and Cardiology. During this admission it was noted that the findings on prior liver biopsy were secondary to hepatic congestion from heart failure.
Discussion
In people with HIV, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) is characterized by an exaggerated or atypical inflammatory response following initiation of ART manifested as either paradoxical worsening of a previously known infection or unmasking of a previously unrecognized one. IRIS has been observed both in ART-naïve patients initiating therapy for the first time as well as treatment-experienced patients who are re-initiating ART after a period of being off therapy. Criteria that support the diagnosis of IRIS include: (1) a temporal relationship between ART initiation and clinical deterioration with symptoms of an infectious or inflammatory condition, (2) symptoms not explained by a newly acquired infection, predicted course of a previously diagnosed infection, or adverse effects of drug therapy, and (3) evidence of virological response to ART with >1-log decrease in HIV RNA often with an associated increase in CD4 cell count[1]. In our patient, the temporal relationship between ART initiation and development of symptoms after previously being asymptomatic along with his rapid virological response evidenced by a 4-log decrease in viral load support the diagnosis of an unmasking IRIS.
In patients with IRIS, memory T cells are redistributed from lymphoid tissue to organs, and the number of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells increases faster than that of regulatory T cells, resulting in an imbalance in the immune system [2,3]. This disproportionate inflammatory response has been linked to a number of infectious pathogens [1,4]. Histoplasmosis -associated IRIS appears to be rarely reported in the literature with an estimated incidence rate of 0.74 cases per 1000 HIV-infected person-years and may present months after initiation of ART [4-7]. Asymptomatic dissemination beyond the lungs is fairly common but usually only becomes clinically apparent in patients with impaired cellular immunity in conditions like HIV as seen in our patient [6]. Reactivation of latent infections may occur long after the primary infection has taken place [7,8]. Some evidence suggests increased incidence in endemic regions like the Ohio and Mississippi River Valleys after ART initiation [4-7].
Immune reconstitution in the setting of disseminated Histoplasmosis is classically characterized by fever, fatigue, and a combination of respiratory and/or gastrointestinal symptoms. Central nervous system and cutaneous involvement appear to be far less commonly reported [6]. In the United States, for instance, less than 10% of all cases of disseminated Histoplasmosis present with cutaneous manifestations, and reports of cutaneous manifestations in the absence of involvement of other organ systems are exceedingly rare [9-12]. Cutaneous manifestations of Histoplasma are most often described as diffuse papuloulcerative lesions with crusting, followed by pustular or nodular lesions as witnessed in our patient’s case and can easily be mistaken for other infectious etiologies highlighting the importance of culture and pathology in making a diagnosis [9,10].
Once diagnosed, management of Histoplasmosis -associated IRIS focuses on treatment of the underlying infection since rates of mortality are high [6,7]. The natural disease course is progressive and fatal over a few months time if untreated, and due to the significant morbidity and mortality associated with untreated disease, some literature even propose a role for implementation of screening for latent and subclinical Histoplasmosis prior to initiation of ART therapy for patients in endemic regions [5]. Antifungal agents such as amphotericin and itraconazole (for consolidation and long-term suppression) are the mainstays of therapy [6,7]. For patients intolerant of itraconazole, oral posaconazole and voriconazole have been demonstrated to be effective against Histoplasma and are alternatives for patients who are only moderately ill [7].
While our patient was able to continue ART and remain virologically suppressed throughout his treatment course, the potential adverse effects of antifungal therapy must always be considered. Triazoles may have drug-drug interactions, and gastrointestinal symptoms are the most commonly reported side effects seen in patients receiving itraconazole. However, evidence suggests an association between congestive heart failure and itraconazole use, likely due to the drug’s direct negative inotropic effect [13, 14]. Therefore, itraconazole has a warning advising against its use in individuals with a history of congestive heart failure or in those who develop signs or symptoms of heart failure during its administration [14]. We suspect that our patient’s newly-diagnosed heart failure was either caused by, or at least exacerbated by, the itraconazole he was receiving, so we opted to change his treatment regimen. This black box warning has not been issued with other triazoles although concerns about drug absorption and need for QTc monitoring remain relevant for the majority of the drug class.
Here we present a case of disseminated Histoplasmosis after re-introduction of ART in a treatment-experienced HIV-positive patient. As the efficacy and tolerability of ART continue to improve, IRIS has become an increasingly more commonly recognized early complication observed after ART initiation. This patient’s presentation, characterized primarily by cutaneous lesions, highlights the need for clinicians to: 1) keep a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating immunocompromised hosts that may have atypical manifestations, 2) involve additional consulting services when needed to assist in obtaining a timely diagnosis to guide antimicrobial therapy that may reduce morbidity and mortality, and 3) monitor for potential adverse effects of antimicrobial therapy that may require use of alternative treatment regimens.
Final Diagnosis
Histoplasmosis -Associated Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome
