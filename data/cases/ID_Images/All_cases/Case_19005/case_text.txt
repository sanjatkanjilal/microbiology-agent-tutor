History of Present Illness
A previously well man in his sixties presented with 3 months of exertional dyspnea associated with occasional chest pain. His exercise tolerance decreased from one mile to 100 feet. He denied orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, or lower extremity edema. He also reported anorexia, 50-pound weight loss, and frequent night sweats but denied fevers and chills. He was hospitalized and found to have diffuse lymphadenopathy and hypercalcemia and was diagnosed with Adult T-Cell Leukemia/Lymphoma (ATLL) secondary to human T-lymphotropic virus (HTLV)-1 infection.
Epidemiological History
He was born in the Caribbean and moved to the mid-Atlantic United States as a young adult. He had over a 100-pack-year smoking history. He drank about 36 ounces of beer and a pint of liquor a day. He smoked marijuana several times a week.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared fatigued and chronically ill. His temperature was 98.6°F (37.0°C), blood pressure was 106/64 mmHg, pulse 101 beats per minute, respiration was 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation was 100% on room air. He had no lymphadenopathy. His lungs were clear to auscultation bilaterally. He had normal active bowel sounds. His abdomen was soft, non-tender, non-distended, with negative Murphy’s sign, no rebound tenderness, and no hepatosplenomegaly. The exam was otherwise normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 13,000/microliter (reference range (RR) 4.5-11), with 21% neutrophils, 62% lymphocytes, 8% atypical lymphocytes, 9% monocytes, 0% eosinophils, hemoglobin 10.3 g/dL (RR 13.9-16.3) and platelets 208,000/mm 3 (RR 150-350). His blood urea was 72 mg/dL (RR 7-22), creatinine 2.4 mg/dL (RR 0.6-1.3), calcium 16 mg/dL (RR 8.4-10.5), and ionized calcium 1.68 mmol/L (RR 1.13-1.32). His albumin was 4.5 g/dL (RR 3.5-5.3), aspartate aminotransferase 69 U/L (RR 0-37), alanine aminotransferase 104 U/L (RR 0-40), and alkaline phosphatase 130 U/L (RR 30-120). His parathyroid hormone was 14 mg/mL (RR 10-65), parathyroid hormone-related peptide 36 pg/mL (RR 14-27), 25-hydroxy vitamin D 29 ng/mL (RR 30-100), 1-25 vitamin D < 8 pg/mL (RR 18-72), and lactate dehydrogenase 607 U/L (RR 118-273).
He tested negative for HIV and Hepatitis A, B and C. Other routine laboratory test results were normal.
A chest x-ray on presentation showed clear lung fields (Fig. 1). CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast demonstrated a 5mm right upper lobe nodule, homogenous liver, and normal-sized spleen without lymphadenopathy.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
On the first hospital day, the patient was started on cyclophosphamide, vincristine, etoposide, and high dose prednisone. On day 2, he developed new right upper quadrant pain and rebound tenderness with an abdominal plain film showing dilated loops of small bowel consistent with small bowel obstruction. On day 3, the patient had increased oxygen requirement with a chest x-ray showing diffuse pulmonary opacities (Fig. 2). His hemoglobin decreased from 10 to 5 g/dL with no evidence of gastrointestinal bleeding.
In this setting, he was transitioned to comfort care, and he passed away shortly thereafter.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
An autopsy demonstrated ATLL involving the bone marrow, lymph node, spleen, kidneys and liver. In addition, he was found to have Strongyloides hyperinfection syndrome with dissemination involving the larynx, trachea, heart, bilateral lungs, esophagus, stomach, small bowel, large bowel, liver, pancreas, spleen, testes, scrotum, thyroid gland, lymph nodes, skeletal muscle, nervous tissue, and skin. There was extensive hemorrhage and congestion in the lungs, along with a dusky and dilated small bowel. Treatment and Followup
Discussion
Human T-Lymphotrophic Virus-1 (HTLV-1) was the first human onco-retrovirus discovered (1). It is found in sub-Saharan Africa, South America, Caribbean basin, northern Iran, Japan, central Australia (1). Roughly 10 million people are infected with HTLV-1 (1). The virus is transmitted by breast feeding, by sexual contact, or by contaminated blood supply or needles (2). Although the majority of HTLV-1 infections are asymptomatic, up to 5% are associated with Adult T-Cell Leukemia/Lymphoma (ATLL), which usually presents in the fifth decade after being infected in childhood (1,2). HTLV-1 primarily infects CD4 T-lymphocytes and is different than other retroviruses in that it creates new cell clones by cell-to-cell contact via “virologic synapses.”. Even with early initiation of chemotherapy, ATLL carries a poor prognosis (1).
S. stercoralis is an intestinal nematode that infects between 30-100 million people worldwide mostly in the tropical regions, including areas in southeastern United States and southern Europe (3,4,5,6). The life cycle can be divided into the free-living cycle and the parasitic cycle. The filariform larvae from the environment enter through the human skin, move into the circulation and to the lungs where they penetrate alveoli, ascend the bronchial tree, are swallowed, and arrive at the duodenum where they become adult female worms and lay eggs in the intestinal mucosa. The rhabditiform larvae are then released into the intestinal lumen. At this point, the larvae are either passed in the feces and develop into free-living adult worms, or they mature in the large intestine into filariform larvae and penetrate the intestinal mucosa or perianal skin, repeating the parasitic cycle in the same host, known as autoinfection (7). S. stercoralis is the only helminth that can complete its whole life cycle in a single host (6). Risk factors for acquiring S. stercoralis include visiting endemic regions, gardening, and working in agriculture (4). S. stercoralis may lay dormant and continue its life cycle in the human host until there is a disruption in homeostasis, leading to hyperinfection and dissemination of larvae. Risk factors for hyperinfection include patients receiving immunosuppression (such as steroids), solid organ transplant recipients and patients with hematologic malignancies, diabetes, alcoholism, and HTLV-1 coinfection (4,8).
There is a wide spectrum of S. stercoralis clinical presentations. Most commonly, patients are asymptomatic. Cutaneous manifestations (eg, larvae currens) are found early in the disease course. Chronic infections are usually asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic with vague gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal bloating. Rarely, patients may have hyperinfection with dissemination (2,4). With hyperinfection, symptoms occur at sites of highest larvae burden, typically the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts. Symptoms can be severe and include vomiting, obstruction, and pulmonary hemorrhage (4), all of which occurred with our patient. Another presentation to be aware of is gram-negative bacteremia and meningitis, where the filariform larvae penetrate the intestinal wall and carry with them gram-negative organisms into the blood stream and, at times, the central nervous system (4).
Diagnosis of S. stercoralis can be made via several modalities. Definitive non-invasive diagnosis is made by stool examination for ova and parasites. Due to the intermittent shedding of larvae, at least 3 stool samples are required to increase the sensitivity of the test (7). Another common method is serum enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which is 90% sensitive but can cross-react with other parasites (7). In cases of hyperinfection, diagnosis is much less difficult given the severe presentation and large number of larvae that can be found in stool, CSF, or BAL fluid (4,7). However, a clinician needs to have a strong clinical suspicion and consider epidemiologic factors in order to arrive at the correct diagnosis.
Ivermectin is the treatment of choice for strongyloidiasis, with duration depending on severity of infection (4,6,7). For uncomplicated acute and chronic strongyloidiasis, ivermectin 200 ug/kg (single dose orally for 1-2 days) is recommended (6,7). Alternatively, albendazole 400mg orally twice daily for 3-7 days can be used (6,7). For Strongyloides hyperinfection and dissemination, recommendations are to use ivermectin 200 ug/kg per day orally for at least 2 weeks after stool parasitic exams are negative. Equally important is to stop or reduce immunosuppression if possible (6,7). Patients from sub-Saharan Africa with suspected concomitant Loa loa infection should not receive ivermectin due to the risk of fatal encephalopathy caused by rapid microfilaricidal activity (7). Unfortunately, decreased treatment efficacy has been noted with co-infected HTLV-1/ S. stercoralis patients (2). Follow-up stool examination for Strongyloides should be performed several weeks after treatment to document infection clearance. If parasites are still seen, retreatment is warranted (7).
S. stercoralis and HTLV-1 share some endemic regions such as Japan, Jamaica, and parts of South America (8,9,10). HTLV-1 infected patients have a greater risk of acquiring strongyloidiasis and developing severe infections with Strongyloides (2). Patients that are co-infected have a modified immune response that allows for increased autoinfection. Separately, HTLV-1 creates a Th1-response with high production of interferon-gamma (IFN-g), whereas patients infected with S. stercoralis have a Th-2 immune response with elevated levels of IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IgE, and increased mast cells and eosinophils, all of which help in the defense against helminths (2). However, co-infection leads to a unique imbalance of immune responses. Decreased IL-4 and IL-13, the absence of IgE antibodies against Strongyloides, and decreased IL-5 lead to reduced elimination of larvae in the stool, inhibition of mast cell degranulation, and decreased number and function of eosinophils, respectively. As a result, this immunologic switch from Th-2 to Th-1 response impairs parasite killing and predisposes to hyperinfection and dissemination (2,11). In turn, there are reports that S. stercoralis can influence the development of ATLL (12,13). This case highlights the importance of screening for S. stercoralis i n patients with ATLL secondary to HTLV-1 who are candidates for immunosuppression, especially those with epidemiologic risk factors.
This case also illustrates how patients can be asymptomatic from their parasitic infection with normal eosinophil counts due to the HTLV-1 downregulation of the Th-2 response, and that the clinician should have a high index of suspicion in order to diagnose Strongyloides hyperinfection. Soon after immunosuppression, our patient developed abdominal pain and ileus and had pulmonary hemorrhage. He had risk factors for hyperinfection such as having co-infection with HTLV-1, receiving high dose steroids, and extensive alcohol use. Patients should be screened for S. stercoralis if they have intermediate or high-risk exposure to S. stercoralis (from endemic area, long trips to endemic area), known HTLV-1 infection, receiving immunosuppression or are candidates for immunosuppression (steroids, pre-transplant evaluation in certain situations), or with unexplained eosinophilia (7,14).
Final Diagnosis
Adult T-Cell Leukemia/Lymphoma secondary to Human T-Lymphotrophic Virus-1 with Strongyloides hyperinfection
