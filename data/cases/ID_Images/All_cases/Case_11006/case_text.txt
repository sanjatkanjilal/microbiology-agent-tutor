History of Present Illness
2011 IDSA Case Presentation Summary: A woman with hyperesthesia, weakness and unsteady gait.
A woman in her forties with ulcerative colitis developed hyperesthesia over her left posterior forearm, which spread to her entire left arm and shoulder, chest, and back over a two-week period, associated with weakness of her left arm and unsteady gait.
She saw her primary care physician, who sent her to the emergency department; she was admitted for further evaluation. She reported no fever, chills, headache, neck pain, and denied any respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms before the onset of her neurologic symptoms.
Past Medical History
She had a history of ulcerative colitis.
Medications
Medications included infliximab and prednisone (20 mg daily), which she had been taking for the previous 7 months.
Allergies
She had no allergies.
Epidemiological History
She had not traveled recently, and denied intake of unpasteurized dairy or deli meats.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well, alert and fully oriented. The blood pressure was 125/73 mm Hg, pulse 54 beats per minute, temperature 98.6°F (37.0°C), respirations 12 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation 95% while breathing ambient air. The neurological examination revealed poor coordination during fast, alternating movements; left upper extremity motor weakness and hyperesthesia of the left arm, shoulder, chest and back. There were no meningeal signs, and the remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 9100 cells per cubic millimeter (reference range 4000-12,000); other routine laboratory test results were normal. Blood cultures were sterile. An interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis was negative.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and cervical spine, performed with the administration of gadolinium, showed an abnormal signal in the medulla predominant on T2 and FLAIR images, with small amount of gadolinium enhancement that extended to the entire cervical cord down to the level of the first thoracic vertebra (T1) (see Figures 1 and 2).

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient initially declined lumbar puncture. Because of a concern for a rapid demyelinating condition, the patient was immediately started on methylprednisolone (60 mg every 6 hrs, intravenously), with slight improvement in her weakness.
On the fifth hospital day, double vision and a peripheral facial palsy on the left side developed. The next day, difficulty in swallowing and speaking occurred, without shortness of breath. Her mental status remained intact. Dysphagia progressed and the trachea was subsequently intubated for airway protection.
A repeat MRI of the brain showed increased T2/FLAIR signal with enhancement in the brainstem and a new region of increased uptake in the posterior left pons (Figures 3 and 4).
Figure 3. Follow-up MRI of the brain and brainstem, with gadolinium.
Figure 4. Follow-up MRI, with gadolinium.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A lumbar puncture was performed and analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed 269 leukocytes per cubic millimeter (55% neutrophils and 45% monocytes), protein 92 mg/dL (reference range 14-45 mg/dL)), and glucose 54 mg/dL (ref 40-80 mg/dL).
Gram stain of the CSF showed no organisms, but culture of the CSF grew gram positive rods with tumbling motility characteristic of Listeria monocytogenes (Figure 5).
Figure 5. Gram stain of CSF culture growth.
Treatment and Followup
The patient was started on high dose ampicillin and gentamicin intravenously and steroids were tapered. Within forty-eight hours of starting antibiotics, the patient noted improvement of the hyperesthesia. By day 6 of antibiotics, she noted 50% improvement of the hyperesthesia and by day 7, she was extubated. A repeat brain and cervical MRI after 2 weeks of antibiotics still showed abnormal signal in the pons and medulla which was stable but the enhancement seen previously on cervical cord had decreased.
Ampicillin was administered intravenously throughout her admission and, at discharge, penicillin G was administered to complete an eight weeks' course of therapy.
Her overall function improved, but her neuropathy, diplopia and left-sided facial palsy persisted.
Discussion
Listeria monocytogenes is a food-borne facultative anaerobic gram-positive bacterium which, when ingested by susceptible individuals, typically causes infection in the blood and central nervous system (CNS). It grows at refrigeration temperatures bwtween 4°C to 10°C. Several food-borne Listeria outbreaks have occurred in the past 20 years. Foods most classically associated with this infection are Mexican-style cheeses, turkey deli meats and frankfurters.
Recently, Listeria has been linked to cantaloupe consumption. This latest outbreak started in summer of 2011 with all illnesses starting on or after July 31, 2011. The initial 7 cases were reported from Colorado and all seven patients had eaten cantaloupes within a month prior to onset of their symptoms. These contaminated cantaloupes were traced back to Jensen Farms in Colorado. Four strains with widely differing PFGE pattern combinations and 2 serotypes (1/2a and 1/2b) were identified (CDC, 2011). All the 4 outbreak strains were isolated from whole and cut cantaloupe samples from patients’ homes, grocery stores and the farm. Jensen Farms recalled all their cantaloupes on September 14, 2011. As of November 1, 2011, there were 139 cases in 28 states with 29 deaths including one pregnant woman who miscarried at the time of illness (CDC Listeria outbreak website). More cases are still being reported at the time this case was written due to the lag time between diagnosis and laboratory confirmation as well as the incubation period which may take up to 2 months.
Immunocompromised patients and pregnant women are at particularly high risk for Listeria infection. Infection with Listeria has also been reported as an infectious complication of anti-tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) treatment, but the majority of reported cases are septicemia and meningitis [Slifman et al. 2003, Wallis et al. 2004].
By contrast, Listeria brainstem encephalitis, one of its rare CNS manifestations, has been reported predominantly in healthy adults with only about 8% seen in immunocompromised patients [Armstrong and Fung 1993]. It is typically a biphasic illness, usually beginning with headache, fever, nausea and vomiting, followed by cranial nerve palsies, ataxia, weakness, decreased consciousness and occasionally, seizures. Myelitis and spinal cord abscesses are even rarer manifestations of neurolisteriosis and have been reported in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients [Bartt 2000, Josephson et al. 2000].
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination is the most useful diagnostic test for both Listeria brainstem encephalitis and cervical myelitis. In a review of CSF findings in reported cases of brainstem encephalitis, CSF leukocytes ranged from 100 to 499 cells, which may either be neutrophilic or lymphocytic predominant cells [Armstrong and Fung 1993]. CSF protein is high and CSF glucose is often normal. Blood cultures are positive in half of the cases. In general, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is more useful than CT scan in visualizing the posterior fossa which often shows increased intensity of the lesions on T2-weighted images which may or may not enhance with contrast [Alper et al. 1995].
Demyelinating conditions, with intense lesions on MRI T2-weighted images, as seen in our patient, have been reported in a case series of 20 patients during and following treatment with TNF-alpha antagonists [Mohan et al. 2001]. However, patients typically improved with discontinuation of the offending drug and the start of alternative therapies such as glucocorticoids, interferon (IFN)-beta or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Our patient’s symptoms worsened on steroids, which raised suspicion for an infectious etiology.
Mycoplasma pneumoniae can also cause an acute transverse myelitis or disseminated encephalomyelitis condition [Daxboeck 2006]. It is more common in children and is usually preceded by respiratory symptoms. Mycoplasma pneumoniae IgM can also persist for an extended period of time, sometimes up to 12 months after an initial infection, in some patients. In this case, Mycoplasma infection was less likely because of the absence of preceding respiratory symptoms and the patient’s improvement on ampicillin, a drug that does not target Mycoplasma.
There is an increased risk of reactivation of tuberculosis (TB) in patients on anti-TNF-alpha treatment with the majority presenting as extrapulmonary disease [Keane et al. 2001]. Tuberculosis may potentially have atypical presentations as well. However, the majority of TB CNS infections presents with basilar arachnoiditis or meningeal enhancement associated with some degree of hydrocephalus on neuroimaging. In addition to the lack of compatible clinical and radiologic findings, our patient had negative interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis.
Listeria monocytogenes infection should be considered in an immunocompromised patient presenting with acute craniopathies, hemiparesis or any focal neurological deficits, even with lack of systemic signs and symptoms of infection. Examination of the CSF is a crucial tool in the evaluation of acute neurologic symptoms prior to giving empiric high-dose corticosteroids or other treatment modalities which would potentially worsen an underlying infection.
Final Diagnosis
Brainstem encephalitis and cervical myelitis caused by Listeria monocytogenes in an immunocompromised woman with ulcerative colitis.
