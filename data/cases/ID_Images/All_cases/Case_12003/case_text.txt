History of Present Illness
2012 July-August Featured Case: A man with HIV infection and new onset ankle pain, leg edema and erythema.
A man in his thirties, on antiretroviral therapy for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection and with a history of previous treatment for tuberculous pleurisy and syphilis, was well until approximately 6 weeks before presentation when he had unprotected intercourse with another HIV-positive man. Shortly thereafter, sore throat, chills and subjective fevers developed, followed by a diffuse whole body erythematous rash. He was seen in a medical walk-in clinic. On examination, a morbilliform maculopapular, erythematous rash that blanched was present on his anterior torso, back, arms and thighs. A rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test was positive at 1:2 dilution, which was stable compared to routine testing performed one month before. A viral exanthem was diagnosed, and he was advised to return in 2 weeks for another RPR titer.
The symptoms and physical findings resolved in several days. Four weeks later, he contacted his medical providers because of intermittent bone pain of 2 weeks' duration, and skin irritation over the Achilles tendon region of the left ankle. He reported his skin was slightly red and warm, with a blister. He denied fever, chills, gastrointestinal or genitourinary symptoms. He was advised to return for examination and follow-up RPR testing.
Past Medical History
HIV infection had been diagnosed 15 years before in the setting of weight loss, fevers, and fatigue. CD4 T-lymphocytes cell count at diagnosis was 330 cells per cubic millimeter, and the level of HIV nucleic acid was 1600 copies per milliliter. The patient began antiretroviral (ARV) therapy shortly after diagnosis, then stopped after 4 years because he felt well. The CD4 T-lymphocyte count decreased to less than 100 per cubic millimeter. He resumed ARV therapy four years before this evaluation. Two months before this evaluation, the CD4 count was 599 per cubic millimeter, and the level of HIV RNA was undetectable.
Eleven years before presentation, anal human papilloma virus had been diagnosed and local surgical excision performed.
Five years before presentation, pleurisy and pneumonia were diagnosed. A presumptive diagnosis of tuberculosis was made, and a 6-month course of antituberculous medications (4 drugs for 2-3 months, then 2 drugs for 4 months) was administered. Four years before presentation, a diagnosis of pneumonia was made, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX) was administered for probable Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia and, as noted above, the patient resumed ARV therapy.
Approximately 22 months before this presentation, he developed a rash on his arms, chest, palms, and soles; a diagnosis of syphilis was made. Benzyl penicillin was reportedly administered intramuscularly once weekly for 3 weeks. Eight months before this evaluation, he was noted to have a persistently positive RPR titer at 1:128 dilution. A CSF examination did not show evidence for neurosyphilis. He received another course of treatment for syphilis with benzathine penicillin intramuscularly once weekly for 3 weeks. After re-treatment, the RPR titer declined to 1:2 dilution when measured two months before this encounter.
Medications
His medications included abacavir (300mg every 12 hours), lopinavir and ritonavir in combination (200mg/50mg, 2 tablets every 12 hours) and maraviroc (150mg twice daily. He also took ibuprofen (200mg 1-2 tablets every 8 hours as needed for pain).
Family History
There was no family history of cancer, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease.
Epidemiological History
He was born Mexico, had moved to Costa Rica 6 years before and to New England 18 months before this presentation. He was sexually active with men. He exercised regularly.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. The blood pressure was 120/80 mmHg, pulse 61 beats per minute, weight 194lb (88.2kg) and temperature 98.1°F (36.7C). There was mild retropharyngeal erythema, without exudates. Inguinal lymph nodes (sub-centimeter) were palpable and non-tender. There was erythema on both anterior shins (Figures 1 and 2), which was partially blanching; purpuric lesions were also present. The area of erythema was warm. There was 1+ anterior tibial edema bilaterally and trace to no pedal edema. The knees and ankles were tender to palpation and painful with active and passive range of motion, without joint effusion, warmth or erythema. The remainder of the examination was normal.

Studies
The complete blood count was normal (including the white blood cell count at 8100 cells per cubic millimeter, with 76% neutrophils, 18% lymphocytes, 5% monocytes and 1% eosinophils), as were blood levels of electrolytes, glucose, total protein, albumin, globulin, calcium, tests of renal and liver function and the urinalysis. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 47 mm per hour (reference range 0-13).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
An anal culture for Neisseria gonorrhoeae (also known as gonococcus or GC) was positive. Figure 3 shows growth on a JEMBEC agar plate and Figure 4 subculture growth on Thayer-Martin agar. Figure 5 demonstrates a gram stain of the cultured organism. Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that this gonococcus was resistant to penicillin G and tetracycline, intermediate to ciprofloxacin, and susceptible to ceftriaxone and spectinomycin. Pharyngeal culture for the organism was negative, as was urinary PCR testing for GC and Chlamydia trachomatis nucleic acid. Blood cultures were sterile. A rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test was unchanged and reactive at 1:2 dilution. Testing for Lyme disease (antibodies to Borrelia burgdorferi) and hepatitis B virus infection was negative.

Treatment and Followup
Azithromycin (250 mg 4 tablets (1gram) orally, once) and ceftriaxone injection (20 mg/ml solution, 1 gram injection every 24 hours for 48 hours) was administered, followed by oral cefixime 400mg orally, twice a day for an additional 6 days. At follow-up one month later, he reported some residual joint aches and fatigue but was otherwise improved, and a follow-up rectal culture on a JEMBEC plate was negative.
Discussion
Of the possible diagnoses listed in the differential, recurrent secondary syphilis was a consideration based on the clinical history, but the absence of a rise in the RPR titer made this diagnosis less likely. Although the patient lived in an endemic area for Lyme disease, the initial rash was not typical for erythema migrans. Furthermore, Lyme disease more frequently causes a monoarthritis than the diffuse arthralgias and enthesopathy that this patient described. The clinical syndrome in this patient could correspond to an uncommon serum sickness-like presentation of acute hepatitis B virus (seen in fewer than 10% of cases). The unprotected sexual encounter is a plausible route of transmission, but the antecedent rash seen in this patient would be atypical. The serum sickness-like phase of hepatitis B virus infection can precede overt jaundice; however, this patient had no symptoms of nausea or vomiting and no elevation in transaminases that typify acute hepatitis B. Reactive arthritis was a reasonable consideration for his arthralgias; however, this patient did not have conjunctivitis, urethritis, or sacroiliitis, which are often seen with this condition. The exposure history, timing, and symptom complex all suggested the possibility of disseminated gonococcal infection; the positive rectal culture and clinical response to antibiotics confirmed this diagnosis [Rice PA, 2005].
Neisseria gonorrhoeae is a common sexually transmitted pathogen that can cause asymptomatic infection (more common in women) as well as a multitude of clinical syndromes. The most common clinical presentation is urethritis; however, N. gonorrhoeae can also cause inflammation of other mucosal surfaces, including the cervix, the conjunctivae, the oropharynx, and the rectum. More serious complications include pelvic inflammatory disease in women, purulent arthritis, and a constellation of symptoms called disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI). DGI is characterized by tenosynovitis, dermatitis, arthralgias (typically of appendicular joints), and skin lesions [Rice PA, 2005]. Rare complications include perihepatitis (Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome), and less commonly endocarditis or meningitis.
The type of clinical syndrome exhibited by a patient infected with gonococcus (asymptomatic, mucosal inflammation, septic arthritis, DGI) is thought to depend upon both microbial and host factors. For instance, certain strains of N. gonorrhoeae are thought more likely to cause DGI and minimal genital inflammation [Marrazzo JM et al., 2010; Workowski KA and Berman S, 2010; Tapsall JW et al., 1992]. Considering host factors, women are far more likely than men to exhibit asymptomatic infection and are three times as likely to exhibit DGI. Other host factors such as pregnancy, recent menses, and complement deficiency predispose to DGI [Marrazzo JM et al. 2010; Holmes KK et al., 1971; Wise CM et al., 1994; Ellison RT 3rd et al. 1987].
The diagnostic approach for gonorrhea depends on the clinical syndrome suspected. Symptomatic urethritis should be assessed by Gram stain of purulent exudate, which has excellent sensitivity and specificity in this context; however sensitivity is much reduced in asymptomatic infection [Workowski KA and Berman S, 2010]. Because of its improved performance characteristics in asymptomatic patients, nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) has replaced Gram stain and culture for first-line diagnostic testing of urine or vaginal swabs from asymptomatic individuals in much of the US [Marrazzo JM et al., 2010; Workowski KA and Berman S, 2010]. NAATs are not yet FDA approved for pharyngeal or rectal specimens, though preliminary data suggest that they may ultimately be useful [Marrazzo JM et al. 2010; Schachter J et al., 2008]. Culture is still important as well; in many cases (such as this one) it may be the only clue to disseminated infection. Additionally, it allows for susceptibility testing, which is of increasing importance given the rapid emergence of antibiotic resistance (see below). Cultures from non-sterile sites (e.g., oropharynx, rectum) should be inoculated shortly after collection onto appropriate pre-warmed media (usually selective Thayer-Martin media), then placed rapidly into a carbon dioxide containing environment. This can be accomplished either via a candle jar in a 35-to-37 degree celsius incubator, or (as for this case) by using a John E Martin Biological Environment Chamber (JEMBEC) plate, with a carbon dioxide generating tablet added at the time of inoculation, prior to incubation [Levi MH, 1997]. In all cases, patients with suspected gonococcal infection should also be screened for Chlamydia, HIV, and syphilis, given shared risk factors and rates of coinfection.
Treatment of DGI should include parenteral therapy. While few data are available to guide choice or duration of therapy, current recommendations include initial treatment with ceftriaxone (1g once daily). If a patient looks clinically well, or refuses admission, a single dose can be given in clinic via an intramuscular or intravenous route before switching to oral therapy. However, consideration should be given to either admitting the patient, or bringing him or her back to clinic the following day for a second dose of parenteral antibiotics and to confirm response to therapy (both for diagnostic clarity and in view of increasing antibiotic resistance). When clearly clinically improving, patients can be transitioned to oral therapy (as of this writing, typically cefixime 400mg twice daily), to complete 7 to 10 days of total therapy. [Editor's Note: As of August 10, 2012, and after publication of this case, CDC Guidelines for treatment of gonorrhea changed [reference CDC, MMWR 2012]. Before treatment, clinicians are advised to check for updated treatment guidelines.] Antibiotic resistance has emerged as a real threat in gonococcal infections. As such, antimicrobial susceptibility testing should be strongly considered in serious cases and should be pursued in all cases with poor initial response to empiric therapy [Marrazzo JM et al. 2010; Workowski KA and Berman S, 2010]. Fluoroquinolone resistance is sufficiently common that this class is no longer recommended for treatment in the US unless a patient’s isolate is cultured and shown to be susceptible [Marrazzo JM et al. 2010; CDC, 2007; Tapsall JW, 2008]. Even third generation cephalosporins, long the standard of care in the US, have begun to show resistance worldwide [Duncan S and Duncan CJ, 2012; Bolan GA et al.,2012; Martin I et al., 2012; Lo JY et al., 2012; Ross JD and Lewis DA, 2012].
Antibiotic resistance in the US is monitored by the CDC through state and local public health authorities, and the CDC’s Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project (GISP) routinely tests available samples to monitor trends. Gonorrhea is a reportable illness, allowing contact tracing and screening of sexual partners. All recent partners should be evaluated, and even those without symptoms of active infection should be considered for antibiotics for presumed asymptomatic gonorrhea and Chlamydia infection [Workowski KA and Berman S, 2010]. All involved parties should be told to abstain from sexual activity until treatment is completed and symptoms have fully resolved [Workowski KA and Berman S, 2010].
Final Diagnosis
Disseminated Neisseria gonorrhoeae (gonorrhea) infection with enthesopathy in a patient with HIV infection and prior syphilis.
