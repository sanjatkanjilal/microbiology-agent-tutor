History of Present Illness
A previously well man in his sixties was urgently transferred to our hospital for consideration of ventriculoperitoneal shunting after initially seeking medical care 21 weeks prior.
He first developed fevers, sweats, myalgias and sinus congestion shortly after returning from Arizona and was treated with amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. His symptoms then progressed to include fevers and a new cough. Two weeks after symptom onset, he was diagnosed with a bibasilar pneumonia and was treated with levofloxacin. However, he then developed unrelenting headaches followed by diplopia and was admitted to an outside hospital 12 weeks after symptom onset.
There, he underwent a lumbar puncture (LP) which revealed cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) white blood cell count 402 (reference range 0-5) of which 71% were lymphocytes and 4% were neutrophils. His CSF glucose was undetectable (reference range 40 - 70 mg/dL) and total protein was 460 mg/dL (reference range 12 - 60 mg/dL). Bacterial and fungal CSF culture showed no growth. Serum Coccidioides IgG was negative and Cryptococcal antigen was negative. He was discharged with a peripherally inserted central catheter for receipt of intravenous ceftriaxone for treatment of presumed Lyme meningitis despite testing negative for Lyme disease both in the serum and in CSF. He was readmitted to the same hospital with deep venous thrombosis and relentless headaches and underwent another LP which revealed 581 white blood cells in the CSF of which 73% were lymphocytes and 10% were neutrophils. CSF glucose was 23 mg/dL (reference range 40 - 70 mg/dL) and total protein was 509 mg/dL (reference range 12 - 60 mg/dL). Quantiferon Gold assay was negative and CSF Lyme antibody was again negative.
Magnetic resonance imaging of his brain revealed abnormal enhancement of the perimesencephalic cisterns. Positron emission tomography (PET) revealed mild 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose avid paratracheal, axillary and hilar nodes. A lymph node biopsy was performed showing non-necrotizing granulomas and stains for acid fast, fungal and bacterial organisms (AFB, GMS, and gram stains) were negative. Flow cytometry of the lymph node biopsy specimen was normal. He was diagnosed with multisystem sarcoidosis and prescribed 60 mg of prednisone daily as an outpatient. Shortly thereafter, he developed altered mental status and was readmitted to the outside hospital where he was found to have acute hydrocephalus and was transferred to our hospital for urgent ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. The infectious diseases service was consulted.
Past Medical History
Hypertension and benign prostatic hypertrophy
Epidemiological History
He was a life-long non-smoker, does not use illicit drugs nor consume alcohol regularly. He lived in the north east USA. He visited India for about 2 weeks a few years ago, Arizona a few months prior to admission and Louisiana 1 year prior to admission. He owned several cats and dogs but no birds.
Physical Examination
Our initial physical exam was notable for a temperature of 101.1ºF (38.3ºC). His blood pressure was 108/67 mm Hg, pulse 88 beats per minute, respirations 20 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation was 91% on 4 liters per minute by nasal cannula. He had meningismus on neck exam. Scattered crackles were appreciated on pulmonary exam. He was sleepy but rousable and oriented only to self. He was not oriented to year. He could not convey his history accurately but otherwise had a normal neurological exam. Right frontal ventriculoperitoneal shunt was present, and the rest of the examination was otherwise unremarkable.
Studies
His initial complete blood count was notable for a white blood cell count of 10,500/uL (reference range 4.0 - 10.0 K/uL) with 90.9 % neutrophils (reference range 34 - 71%). Complete blood count was otherwise unremarkable. His 4th Generation HIV Antigen/Antibody Assay was negative.
Interventional radiology guided lumbar puncture was performed: CSF tube 1 had 73 total nucleated cells (reference range 0-5), 539 red blood cells (reference range 0-5), 37% neutrophils, 55% lymphocytes, 7% plasma cells. Tube 4 had 272 total nucleated cells (reference range 0-5), 104 red blood cells (reference range 0-5), 40% neutrophils, 51% lymphocytes, 8% plasma cells. Total protein was 1260 mg/dL (reference range 15-45). Glucose was 4 mg/dL.
Chest computed tomography (Figure 1) revealed extensive reticulonodular opacities which were seen diffusely throughout his lungs measuring 2 mm. No consolidations were seen. Prominent mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymph nodes were seen.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Bronchoscopy with bronchioalveolar lavage revealed spherules that were consistent with Coccidioides. (Figure 2)
Coccidioides antibody in the CSF was elevated at 1:8 (reference range <1:1), Coccidioides antibody to F antigen (IgG) was positive.
Serum and CSF cryptococcal antigen were negative. Fungal blood culture ultimately grew Coccidioides immitis/posadasii.
While the CSF collected from the first lumbar puncture at our hospital had no growth, a repeat lumbar puncture was performed and CSF culture grew Coccidioides immitis/posadasii.

Treatment and Followup
He was started on fluconazole 800 mg daily and IV liposomal amphotericin 400 mg daily. He was intubated for airway protection and hypoxemia but his mental status continued to worsen with loss of brainstem reflexes. He ultimately passed away. Autopsy showed diffuse nodularity of the lung pleural surfaces (Figure 3). Axial cross section of both lungs revealed widespread micronodular discoloration of the parenchymal surfaces (Figure 4). Microscopy revealed pathognomonic spherules involving the lung parenchyma (Figure 5). Further autopsy findings included prominent para-aortic, peritracheal, and peribronchial lymph nodes and markedly enlarged axillary lymph nodes (1.8 - 6.5 cm) with necrotizing granulomatous inflammation and confluent necrosis (Figure 6). The thyroid, adrenals, liver and spleen also showed evidence of disseminated Coccidioides infection.
Brain autopsy findings were notable for disseminated Coccidioides infection diffusely involving the leptomeninges with severe lymphoplasmocytic inflammation (Figure 7, 8). Multifocal parenchymal involvement with microabscesses measuring up to 0.2 cm were seen in the frontal, insular, parietal and temporal cortexes, hippocampus, and cerebellum. The lateral ventricle, choroid plexus, and third ventricle were also involved. There was a fungal abscess at the cerebellopontine angle measuring up to 1.0 cm. Multifocal septic emboli involving the frontal cortex, parietal cortex, basal ganglia, medulla, and cerebellum, measuring from 0.1-0.5 cm were also noted.

Discussion
Coccidioidomycosis is colloquially referred to as Valley fever, which alludes to the San Joaquin valley (located in central California) where Coccidioides is highly endemic. Neighboring areas of Arizona, Nevada and New Mexico are also endemic for Coccidioides along with parts of South America. There are 2 species of Coccidioides - immitis and posadasii. They cause similar disease and cannot be differentiated by serology [1-3]. All 3 major endemic fungi in the United States -- Coccidioides, Blastomyces, and Histoplasma -- were actually initially misidentified as protozoa [4]. Coccidioides exists in the environment in the saprobic life cycle as a mycelium or mold composed of septated hyphae which can form spores called arthroconidia [5]. This is the infectious particle, which is easily aerosolized and inhaled. Each arthroconidia is capable of either forming more mycelia or infecting mammals to form spherules. Coccidioides is unique among the pathogenic thermally dimorphic fungi since it does not form single yeast cells but rather conglomerates of endospores within a spherule. Each endospore can form another spherule and spherules are refractory to phagocytosis [6].
Diagnosis of coccidioidomycosis is often difficult. A serum immunodiffusion antibody test against Coccidioides sent at the outside hospital during this patient’s initial hospitalization for meningitis was negative. There are 3 antibody tests for Coccidioides; the immunodiffusion and complement fixation assays are very specific for this disease but less sensitive than the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [7]. The 2016 IDSA guidelines estimate that the ELISA for Coccidioides is about twice as sensitive as the immunodiffusion or complement fixation tests, especially early in the course of disease and is the recommended initial test [8]. Immunodiffusion helps detect infection in the early phases of disease correlating with the IgM response and complement fixation is better in later phases and therefore correlates with IgG [7]. However, negative serological testing does not rule out disease, but a positive test is highly suggestive of an active infection since these serologies will generally revert back to negative after the infection has cleared.
Coccidioidomycosis is a granulomatous disease that induces a T helper 1 response which leads to a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction when it is injected intradermally which is the basis behind the skin test for Coccidioides [9]. Historically, 99% of otherwise healthy patients were shown to convert their Coccidioides skin test within 3 weeks of symptoms of primary pulmonary coccidioidomycosis. This test was recently approved again by the FDA after being discontinued in the 1990s, and has been shown to have a sensitivity and specificity of >98% [10]. An RT PCR diagnostic was recently approved by the FDA that is reported to have 100% sensitivity and between 94% - 100% specificity both retrospectively and prospectively with the caveat that it has only been FDA approved for broncheoalveolar lavage specimens [11].
This patient had coccidioidal meningitis which is notoriously challenging to diagnose. As in this case, diagnosis is usually made by detection of antibodies against Coccidioides in the CSF. CSF in coccidioidal meningitis usually displays a lymphocytic pleocytosis with high total protein and low glucose. CSF Coccidioides antigen testing to diagnose meningitis was recently described [12]. In this study, fungal culture of CSF was only positive in 7% of the cases. However, they showed that CSF Coccidioides antigen had a sensitivity and specificity of 93% and 100%, respectively. Historically, intrathecal amphotericin B was the gold standard of care for coccidioidal meningitis for many decades until a pivotal, albeit uncontrolled, trial published in 1993 changed the field [13]. It showed that treatment with oral fluconazole compared favorably to historical outcomes achieved with intrathecal amphotericin B. Treatment with oral fluconazole daily remains the recommendation in the guidelines published by the Infectious Diseases Society of America in 2016 [8].
Unlike coccidioidal meningitis, which has high mortality if left untreated, there is no evidence that treatment of primary pulmonary coccidioidomycosis actually improves outcomes or prevents complications in most patients, as shown by two prospective observational studies [14, 15]. It is estimated that between 20-30% of community-acquired pneumonia in endemic regions is caused by Coccidioides and primary infection is usually self limited and results in lifelong immunity [1, 16].
Coccidioidal meningitis presents with sub acute to chronic symptoms that may be seen in a variety of infectious and non-infectious conditions. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion in those with a compatible clinical syndrome and epidemiological risk factors, especially in non-endemic areas. The CSF immunodiffusion antibody test and CSF complement fixation antibody test each have a sensitivity of about 70% for diagnosing Coccidioidal meningitis [12]. Clinical suspicion needs to remain high in non-endemic areas as more cases are being diagnosed where these fungi are not typically described and rates of endemic Coccidioides infections continue to increase [17-19].
Final Diagnosis
Disseminated coccidioidomycosis
