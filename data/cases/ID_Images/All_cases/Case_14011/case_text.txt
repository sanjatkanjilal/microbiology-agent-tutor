History of Present Illness
A man in his sixties with a recently diagnosed HIV infection was admitted because of skin lesions progressing up his right arm over a month prior to admission.
Approximately 3 months before this admission, he developed nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and a thirty-pound weight loss. He was admitted to a hospital and was found to be HIV-positive. His CD4 T-lymphocyte cell count was 54 cells cells per microliter (reference range 328–1404) and the HIV RNA level was 602,870 copies per milliliter. During that hospitalization, he was also diagnosed with Campylobacter gastroenteritis, cytomegalovirus (CMV) enteritis and oral candidiasis. On examination, there was a small non-inflamed nodule on the distal interphalangeal joint of his right index finger, which was biopsied. Histopathologic staining of the biopsy revealed acid-fast organisms and the specimen was sent for culture. Emtricitabine-tenofovir, raltegravir, atovaquone, valganciclovir, ethambutol and azithromycin were started. He was discharged with outpatient followup.
During the four weeks following discharge, the lesion on his hand became inflamed, with erythema and swelling, and lesions progressed up his arm toward the axilla. He reported night sweats, but had no fever, nausea, vomiting or diarrhea and was gaining weight.

Epidemiological History
The patient was born in Mexico and immigrated to the United States more than two decades earlier. He lived in Arizona where he worked as a landscaper. He reported possibly pricking his right index finger with a thorn in the past. There was no recent travel, animal exposures, use of illicit drugs or new sexual contacts.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared chronically ill. The blood pressure was 107/68 mmHg, pulse 95 beats per minute, temperature 99°F and respirations 20 breaths per minute. The right index finger was swollen and inflamed in the area of the previous biopsy with patchy inflammatory lesions extended up his arm in a lymphatic distribution (Figures 1 and 2). The lesions were not tender and there was no lymphadenopathy.

Studies
The white blood cell count was 4,300 per microliter (reference range (ref) 4,500-10,700), hemoglobin 10.7 g/dL (ref 12.4-15.7), creatinine 0.76 mg/dL (ref 0.66-1.25), alkaline phosphatase 76 U/L (ref 56-119), aspartate aminotransferase 37 U/L (ref 17-59), alanine aminotransferase 70 U/L (ref 21-72). A chest radiograph was normal. In the month between the two admissions his CD4 T-lymphocytes had increased from 54 to 84 cells per cubic millimeter and the HIV-RNA level had decreased from 602,870 to 351 copies per milliliter.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was readmitted to the hospital. Cultures from the biopsy performed during the first admission had remained negative. Amikacin, ciprofloxacin and rifampin were administered empirically for possible mycobacterial infection. When the lesions worsened, surgical debridement was performed.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Cultures obtained by biopsy of his right index finger during his first admission eventually grew Mycobacterium haemophilum. Cultures for group A streptococcus, Sporothrix and Nocardia were negative. A diagnosis of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) associated with Mycobacterium haemophilum infection was made based on the clinical presentation and testing results.
Treatment and Followup
Shortly after admission and the diagnosis of IRIS was made, prednisone, amikacin, rifampin and ciprofloxacin were administered. Sensitivity testing of the Mycobacterium haemophilum grown in culture suggested resistance to amikacin and ethambutol and they were discontinued; azithromycin was restarted. Antiretroviral therapy was held and his lesions improved. After 2 weeks, antiretroviral therapy was resumed. Four months later, his lesions had healed, although there was scarring.
Discussion
This patient’s right arm inflammatory lesions were due to Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS), which is defined as the unmasking of a previously unrecognized infection (or malignancy) or paradoxical worsening of a previously existing condition following the initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) in an HIV-infected person [Shelburne, et al., 2002]. The mechanism for IRIS is presumed to involve reconstitution of pathogen-specific immunity occurring during the rapid increase in CD4 cells and rapid drop in viral load in response to antiretroviral therapy [Crum-Cianflone 2006]. Diagnosis of IRIS requires clinical suspicion since there is no diagnostic test. Management must be individualized; steroids are often used to manage IRIS, as in this patient [Crum-Cianflone 2006].
Mycobacteria are a common group of infectious agents associated with IRIS. This patient developed IRIS caused by a non-tuberculous mycobacterium, Mycobacterium haemophilum, a pathogen increasingly recognized in immunocompromised patients [Saubolle et al., 1996; Shah et al., 2001]. Mycobacterium haemophilum is an environmental mycobacterium that can cause skin and soft tissue infections. Growth in culture requires specialized conditions, e.g. supplementation with hemin or ferric ammonium citrate. In addition, optimal growth is at 30-32°C and the organism grows slowly or not at all at higher temperatures. In this patient, these growth requirements contributed to the slow identification of the acid fast organism.
Final Diagnosis
Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS) associated with Mycobacterium haemophilum infection in a patient with HIV/AIDS.
