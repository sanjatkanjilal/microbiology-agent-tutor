History of Present Illness
Summary: A woman with right-sided chest pain and abdominal symptoms.
A woman in her sixties, who had a history of extensive colonic diverticuli, presented with vague right-sided chest and abdominal pain of six-months duration. Initially, the pain was intermittent, radiated to the right neck, and was not related to eating. Laboratory testing had included complete blood count, liver function testing, electrocardiography and chest radiography that were normal.
She presented for further evaluation of the chest and abdominal symptoms after nausea, vomiting, fevers, and chills developed and the right upper quadrant pain worsened.
The patient denied headache, diarrhea, melena, or dysuria.
Past Medical History
Colonic diverticuli had been noted on a colonoscopy four months before the onset of symptoms. The patient had dental work performed eight months before her admission.
Epidemiological History
There was a 45-pack-year smoking history and no history of recent travel.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared toxic. The temperature was 101.6°F (38.7°C), blood pressure was stable and oxygen saturation was 99% on ambient air. Examination of the oropharynx showed good dentition. There was no gingivitis, tooth abscess, or thrush. The lungs were clear, and there was no cardiac murmur. The abdomen was distended and mildly tender with hepatomegaly. No splenomegaly was palpated and no skin lesions were seen.
Studies
The white blood count was 20,300 cells/mm³ with 47% neutrophils and 38% bands. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was 67 U/liter, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 173 U/liter, alkaline phosphatase 201 U/liter, and total bilirubin 1.2 mg/dL.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Ultrasonography of the right upper quadrant showed multiple hypoechoic masses in the liver with no ductal dilatation, and a normal gallbladder. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen showed multiple cystic lesions with thick enhancing septa and rims in the right lobe of liver (Figures 1 and 2); a small right pleural effusion was also seen.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Aspiration and drainage of the liver masses were performed by interventional radiological techniques. Gram stain of the aspirate revealed thin gram negative rods (Figure 3) and blood cultures grew gram negative rods in the anaerobic bottles (Figure 4). Final speciation of the organism that was cultured from the blood and aspirate identified Fusobacterium nucleatum.
Figure 3. Gram stain of the liver aspirate.
Figure 4. Gram stain of the blood culture.
Treatment and Followup
Metronidazole, penicillin and levofloxacin were administered and multiple drainage procedures of the abscesses performed. CT scanning of the head and neck did not reveal an obvious source of bacteremia.
Discussion
Liver abscesses are generally amebic or pyogenic. Pyogenic liver abscesses are usually due to the spread of bacteria from another source, such as biliary tract disease, portal system infection, hematogenous spread or trauma.
The bacteriology of pyogenic liver abscesses is usually mixed and may include Enterobacteriaceae, streptococci (aerobic, microaerophilic and anaerobic) and anaerobic gram negative rods (e.g. Fusobacterium and Bacteroides). Fusobacterium nucleatum is an anaerobic bacterium that is part of the normal oral flora. It is characteristically spindle-shaped and often appears in pairs arranged end-to-end.
Final Diagnosis
Pyogenic liver abscess and bacteremia due to Fusobacterium nucleatum.
