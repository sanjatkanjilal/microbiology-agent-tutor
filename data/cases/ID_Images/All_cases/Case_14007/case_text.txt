History of Present Illness
A man in his twenties was referred to a pulmonary outpatient clinic because of intermittent hemoptysis of 4-months’ duration (productive of dark clotted blood, estimated at up to 100 milliliters per day on his worst days). He reported no fever, shortness of breath, weight loss or gastrointestinal symptoms.
Previous evaluations at another hospital revealed peripheral eosinophilia. Testing for vasculitis was negative. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed at the other hospital. Microscopic examination of the BAL specimen and cultures for bacteria, fungi, acid-fast bacilli and viruses were negative.
Past Medical History
A recent Mantoux (purified protein derivative or PPD) test performed at work was reportedly negative.
Medications
None.
Social History
The patient was married.
Epidemiological History
The patient had a pet dog. He had traveled one year earlier to a resort in Mexico for one week. The patient reported no consumption of raw pork or fish, or use of any lipid products or petroleum jelly products.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. The body mass index (weight measured in kilograms divided by the square of the height measured in meters) was 27. The vital signs and the physical examination were normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 12,100 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4300 to 10,800), with eosinophils 28% (reference range <3), total eosinophil count 3370 per cubic millimeter (reference range 0-400). Results of a complete metabolic screening panel were normal. Serological tests for Lyme disease, filaria and fungus (including Aspergillus), an interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) including antibodies to myeloperoxidase (MPO) and proteinase-3 (PR3) were negative. Testing for antibodies to Strongyloides (IgG) was 3.40 (negative < or = 1.49). Testing for stool ova and parasites was negative.
A chest radiograph (Figure 1) showed a discrete opacity in the left lung. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest revealed focal consolidation (5.2cm by 3.7cm) in the lingula (Figure 2) and bilateral pleural effusions.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A needle biopsy of the lingular mass showed interstitial inflammation with eosinophils. A course of ivermectin was administered because of the positive titer for Strongyloides.
After four months, hemoptysis and the eosinophilia persisted. A repeat chest radiograph showed no change in the lung opacity. Additional history was obtained: the patient recalled ingesting raw crawfish while drinking beer on a float trip in Missouri.
A left thoracoscopy with wedge resection was performed. Pathological examination of the resected specimen is shown in Figures 3 and 4.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Pathological examination of the wedge resection biopsy specimen of the left lingula revealed an extensive necrotizing granulomatous inflammatory process with associated bronchopneumonia. Within the necrotic tissue there were scattered discrete oval organisms with a birefringent fibrous coat and central proteinaceous material. The organisms were morphologically consistent with a Paragonimus species.
Testing for Paragonimus westermani (IgG) was 1:32 (negative < 1:32). A distorted, fractured egg (consistent in appearance with Paragonimus kellicotti) that has been phagocytosed by a giant cell was seen in the fibrotic, inflamed pleural tissue. A Langhans-type giant cell with peripheral fibrosis and an ovoid body at the center, representing a lung fluke in a cystic structure is shown in Figure 3 (by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) stain, 40x magnification), and cross-sections of the human trematode, P. kellicotti are shown in Figure 4 (HE stain, 200x magnification). The adult worm of P. kellicotti in a chitinous capsule within a cystic cavity is shown in Figure 5 (HE stain, 400x magnification). Figure 6 shows lung tissue with GMS stain.

Treatment and Followup
A three-day course of praziquantel was administered for paragonimiasis. After the patient was treated, he did not return for follow-up appointments and no further information was available.
Discussion
This is a case of pulmonary infection by the human trematode, Paragonimus kellicotti. The reddish brown adult worms measure 7 to 16 mm by 4 to 7 mm and usually live in lung parenchyma close to bronchioles.
This case highlights the importance of obtaining a thorough epidemiological history in diagnosing North American paragonimiasis. Even if the morphological features of an egg is not characteristic of Paragonimus kellicotti, the identity of the infecting Paragonimus species could be deduced from the travel and dietary history. Multiple case reports [MMWR 2010; Procop 2009; Diaz 2013; Madariaga et al. 2007; and Lane et al. 2009] highlight the common thread of symptoms and social history (i.e., young male who ingests raw crawfish while under the influence of alcohol) in patients with this lung fluke. Although this case highlights cross-reactivity between P. westermani and P. kellicotti [Lane et al. 2009] when performing Western blot with P. westermani antigen, using Western blot with P. kellicotti antigen is much more reliable and has higher sensitivity and specificity [Fischer et al. 2013]. (Unfortunately, this latter test was not available at the time of this patient's evaluation and was not performed.)
This case also illustrates the importance of considering Paragonimus species when a pulmonary lesion and peripheral eosinophilia are present. Although Strongyloides stercoralis larvae can be found in the lungs, Paragonimus is the only human parasite in which the adult organism resides in the lung, as demonstrated in this case. Because patients with Paragonimus infections often present with cough, hemoptysis, cavitary changes and live in geographic areas also endemic for tuberculosis, these patients are frequently misdiagnosed as having tuberculosis [Procop 2009].
Paragonimus westermani is the most widely distributed of the many Paragonimus species that are found throughout the world; its range extends from Japan and Southeast Asia to India.
North American paragonimiasis is relatively rare compared to the approximately 20.7 million people estimated to be infected with Paragonimus worldwide [Procop 2009]. Paragonimus kellicotti is found in North America. Paragonimus mexicanus is present in Central and South America.
Final Diagnosis
Pulmonary paragonimiasis caused by Paragonimus kellicotti.
