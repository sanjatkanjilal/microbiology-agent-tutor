History of Present Illness
Summary of IDSA 2006 Case Submission: A man in his fifties with recurrent skin abscesses.
A man in his fifties presented for evaluation because of recurrent abscesses over the abdomen and groin. Eight months before, painless pustules had developed. Each lesion began as a small pustule, growing in size before yielding a grainy, yellow discharge. Initially, the wounds healed, each leaving behind a 1-2 inch (2.5 - 5.1 cm) scar. Two weeks before admission, increasing scrotal and abdominal wall edema developed. Ten days before presentation, he was evaluated at a local clinic and metronidazole and cephalexin were administered, without improvement.
Past Medical History
The patient reported a similar (but much milder) skin infection that had affected his right calf and resolved without intervention, which occurred when he lived in Mexico more than ten years earlier. There was a history of diabetes mellitus that was diet-controlled, and an inguinal hernia repair on the left, six years before.
Medications
Medications included metronidazole and cephalexin, for ten days.
Epidemiological History
The patient had been born in Mexico, and immigrated to United States a decade earlier. He was heterosexual and monogamous, and had been a field worker in Mexico and a maintainance worker in the United States.
Physical Examination
The temperature was 99.1°F (37.3°C). The right lower abdomen had multiple, well-healed scars with surrounding induration. The left lower abdomen had multiple wounds draining a small amount of yellow discharge. The overlying skin was indurated, but not erythematous. The right calf had multiple pitted, well-healed scars (Figures 1 and 2). The remainder of the examination was normal.

Treatment and Followup
Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (2 double-strength tablets three times daily) was administered for nine months. Once on therapy, no new lesions arose. After therapy, residual scar tissue remained, but symptoms of induration, pain, and discharge resolved completely.
Discussion
Mycetomas are chronic, granulomatous infections of the subcutaneous tissue leading to necrotic abscess, sinus tract, and fistula formation. Untreated, lesions may expand to include adjacent muscle and bone. Mycetomas are caused by true fungi (eumycetomas) or aerobic bacteria (actinomycetomas) and typically occur in tropical and semitropical climates. Mycetomas in the United States are most commonly due to Pseudoallescheria boydii, however, in Mexico, Nocardia brasiliensis is responsible for 86% of cases [Welsh 1993].
The initial infection usually results from trauma, leading to subcutaneous inoculation. Affected patients are often herdsmen, farmers, or other field workers in direct contact with the environment [McGinnis 1996]. This patient reports that he frequently worked outdoors in Mexico, and as a result, likely sustained the initial injury to his right leg (Figure 2). While cases of mycetoma typically affect the feet and hands, in highly endemic areas, infection may include the thighs and perineum, less commonly involving the chest and abdominal walls [Ahmed et al 2004].
Mycetomas progress over months to years, and periods of latency or regression are not uncommon [Lederman and Crum 2004]. Agents of infection spread locally or via the lymphatic system. Secondary nodules subsequently arise and progress to sinus tract formation. Subcutaneous masses consist of grains, or aggregates of bacterial elements, embedded in tough, fibrinous tissue.
Cutaneous infection due to Nocardia spp. may also manifest as a cellulitis, isolated subcutaneous abscess, or lymphonodular process clinically indistinguishable from sporotrichosis (sporotrichoid nocardiosis) [Lerner 1996]. In 10% of cases, cutaneous involvement may be indicative of disseminated Nocardia infection [Kalb et al 1985].
While any pathogenic Nocardia species may cause cutaneous or subcutaneous disease, invasive and locally progressive infection leading to actinomycetoma formation is most commonly due to Nocardia brasiliensis. While systemic Nocardia infection suggests underlying host immunodeficiency, cutaneous infection often occurs in healthy patients with an antecedent superficial injury [Georghiou and Blacklock 1992]. An animal model study suggests that cutaneous inoculation leading to mycetoma formation may be hastened by an impaired cell-mediated immune system [Folb et al 1977].
Mycetoma diagnosis, once suspected, is usually confirmed by culture of purulent drainage or punch biopsy. Nocardia spp. will grow on standard blood agar, however, plating on an enriched media such as Lowenstein Jensen or Sabourand-dextrose agar allows for optimal detection. Gram-positive, variably acid-fast, and filamentous, Nocardia spp. are slow-growing, often requiring 2-5 days for identification. Delayed growth may lead to the premature disposal of cultures and therefore the microbiology lab should be alerted in suspected cases [Lerner 1996]. On occasion, close evaluation of grain morphology may allow preliminary organism identification. Grains due to N. brasiliensis are invisible to the naked eye, but by microscopy they are characterized as white-yellow [McElroy et al 1992].
Actinomycetoma can be treated with medical therapy alone; however, adjuvant surgical excision or drainage may be warranted in complicated cases [Georghiou and Blacklock 1992]. Sulfonamides are the drug of choice for actinomycetoma due to N. brasiliensis; in patients intolerant of sulfa-containing drugs, minocycline may be considered [Lerner 1996]. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is commonly administered. Linezolid also has activity against Nocardia [Gomez-Flores et al 2004], although clincial experience with this agent is limited. Actinomycetoma therapy is lengthy, requiring several months to two years for symptom resolution.
Final Diagnosis
Actinomycetoma caused by Nocardia brasiliensis.
