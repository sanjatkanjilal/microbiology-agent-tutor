History of Present Illness
A Ugandan gentleman with HIV in his thirties presented to a hospital in Uganda with an eight-month history of a slowly-progressive ulcerating facial lesion (Figure 1). The patient initially noted three small dark “bumps” on his face about eight months prior to admission: one on the left side of his forehead, one on the tip of his nose, and one just under the left side of his nose. While the lesions on his forehead and nose remained stable, the lesion under his nose slowly grew, ulcerated, and began to drain purulent material. The patient was treated by multiple local physicians with numerous courses of both intravenous and oral antibiotics as well as systemic steroids without any improvement. Given his lack of response to therapy, he was referred to otolaryngology clinic for further evaluation.
In the otolaryngology clinic, the patient underwent a biopsy of the forehead lesion as well as the leading edge of the larger ulcerating lesion. The ulcerated lesion showed only granulation tissue with fibrinosuppurative membrane. However, the forehead biopsy showed proliferating spindle cells associated with vascular clefts concerning for Kaposi sarcoma (KS) so he was referred to oncology clinic.
Upon presentation to oncology clinic, the patient noted subjective fevers and night-sweats but denied any other systemic symptoms. He denied any history of facial trauma or any insect or animal bites to the face and had no other lesions elsewhere on the skin. Given his systemic symptoms and extensive facial wound, the patient was admitted to the inpatient oncology ward for ongoing management.

Past Medical History
The patient was diagnosed with HIV two months after the skin lesions initially appeared. CD4 cell count and HIV RNA at the time of diagnosis are unknown. He was started on antiretrovirals (lamivudine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/efavirenz) but only took them for 2-3 months before self-discontinuing the medications. About one month prior to hospital admission, he restarted his antiretrovirals (ARVs) and was taking them consistently at the time of admission.
Medications
Lamivudine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/efavirenz
Epidemiological History
The patient lives in a city in Uganda and works as a manual laborer. He had visited a few cities in Northern Uganda 4 months prior to the lesion formation. During this trip, he did not travel outside of city limits. He has no other travel history. He has no exposure to rural or forested areas. He owns a cat but does not recall any prior bites or scratches. Chickens, goats, and dogs are present in his neighborhood but he has no direct contact with these animals.
Physical Examination
Upon presentation to the oncology clinic, the patient was diaphoretic, appeared to be in pain, and was in mild distress. His vital signs were: axillary temperature: 37.5°C (99.5°F), pulse rate: 133 bpm, blood pressure: 134/91 mmHg, and oxygen saturation: 96% on room air.
He had a large central facial lesion measuring 9.5 x 12.2 cm spreading from the bridge of his nose to his chin and covering the lateral aspects of his cheeks bilaterally (Figure 1). The lesion had an eschar over a majority of the surface and there was significant loss of his nasal architecture. The edges of the lesion had a “punched-out” quality and several small satellite lesions were seen on the left inferior aspect of the face and on the chin (Figure 1). Soft tissue edema and tenderness to palpation was noted inferior to the orbits. Purulent discharge could be seen at the wound edges. Oral exam showed no evidence of lesions or ulcerations on the palate. No dental decay was noted and no intraoral tenderness or dental abscesses observed. There was a non-tender 2 cm hyperpigmented nodule noted on his left forehead with a well-healing biopsy site.
On neurologic exam, his pupils were equal and reactive to light and he had full visual acuity. There were no limitations to intraocular muscle movement and all cranial nerves remained intact. Nuchal rigidity was not noted.
Further skin exam revealed no skin rashes or lesions on the trunk or extremities. The rest of his physical exam was within normal limits.

White blood cell count (WBC): 10,330/uL (reference range (RR): 4,000-10,000/uL) with an absolute neutrophil count of 8,080/uL (RR 2,000-7,000/uL), absolute lymphocyte count of 1,240/uL (RR 600-3,400/uL) and an absolute eosinophil count of 30/µL (RR 0-700/uL); hemoglobin: 13.9 g/dl (RR 12.2-18.1 g/dl); platelets: 313,000/mm 3 (RR 140,000-400,00/mm 3).
Chemistries were sodium: 130 mmol/L (RR 136-145 mmol/L), potassium: 5.1 mmol/L (RR 3.5-5.1 mmol/L), chloride: 98 mmol/L (RR 98-107 mmol/L), urea: 5.8 mg/dL (RR 2.5-6.7 mmol/L), creatinine: 0.78 mg/dL (RR 0.3-1.2 mg/dL).
Liver function tests were alanine aminotransferase: 64 U/L (RR 0-55 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase: 29 U/L (RR 5-34 U/L), alkaline phosphatase: 151 U/L (RR 40- U/L), gamma-glutamyl transferase: 181 U/L (RR 9-6 U/L), total protein: 6.8 g/dL (RR 6.4-8.3 g/dL), albumin: 3.1 g/dL (RR 3.5-5.2 gd/L).
Patient’s CD4 count was 259 cells/mm 3 with a non-detected viral load one month prior to presentation.
Chest radiograph showed no evidence of focal infiltrates, hilar adenopathy, or pleural effusions.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was admitted and started on intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone and metronidazole. Lamivudine/tenofovir-disoproxil fumarate/efavirenz was continued. Cultures of the purulent drainage were obtained and positive only for diphtheroids and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus.
Over the first four days of his hospital stay, the patient continued to experience subjective fevers, his orbital swelling increased, and the wound edges slowly advanced. Consequently, empiric IV amphotericin deoxycholate and high-dose oral acyclovir were initiated. Plastic surgery was consulted and he underwent unroofing of his eschar with some improvement in the purulent drainage (Figure 2).
Figure 2. Photograph of the patient’s facial lesion after eschar debridement.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Skin biopsies of the leading edge of the lesion revealed significant acanthosis of the epidermis with a dense dermal infiltrate of lymphocytes, epithelioid cells, and macrophages on H&E stain (Figure 3). The macrophages were parasitized by small ovoid basophilic organisms consistent with Leishmania. No histologic features of KS were seen.

Treatment and Followup
With the diagnosis of mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, the patient was continued on amphotericin deoxycholate, which was readily available at the hospital. Given the concern for renal toxicity with the anticipated prolonged course of amphotericin needed for treatment, the Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative (DND i) was contacted and liposomal amphotericin B was procured through a charitable donation. In total, the patient received 21 days of amphotericin (11 days of amphotericin deoxycholate and 10 days of liposomal amphotericin) for the treatment of mucocutaneous leishmaniasis. Renal function was closely monitored throughout his treatment course and he tolerated the amphotericin without any adverse events. Given the concern for superimposed bacterial infection, the patient received a 14-day course of IV ceftriaxone and metronidazole with resolution of all purulent drainage prior to hospital discharge. At the time he left the hospital, wound edges demonstrated evidence of healing. At the recommendation of dermatology, wound care was maintained with topical petroleum jelly 2-4 times daily to prevent excessive drying and scar tissue development.
Since discharge, the patient has followed in infectious diseases clinic every 2-3 weeks to monitor for any evidence of disease relapse. Serial exams demonstrated steady improvement with significant disease-resolution six weeks after completion of therapy. He did note some mild purulent drainage from the lesion after returning home which resolved with topical mupirocin ointment. He is currently being followed by plastic surgery for management of his wound and planning of future reconstructive surgery.
Given the initial concern for KS, the original skin biopsy was obtained. Upon review, the forehead biopsy also demonstrated macrophages which were parasitized by small ovoid basophilic organisms, thus consistent with a diagnosis of leishmaniasis. No evidence of KS was seen on the original or subsequent biopsies.

Discussion
Mucosal leishmaniasis (ML), characterized by progressive destruction of the mucous membranes due to infection with Leishmania parasites, most commonly affects the nasal or oral mucosa, and is one of the least common clinical presentations of leishmaniasis (1, 2). When mucosal disease occurs in conjunction with cutaneous lesions it is referred to as mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL). New World ML primarily occurs in the Amazon Basin and is caused by parasites in the Leishmania Viannia subgenus, particularly L.V. braziliensis. Patients with New World ML typically experience a self-limiting cutaneous lesion several months to years prior to development of progressive mucosal disease. The clinical presentation of Old World ML, caused by L. L. major, L. L. infantum, L. donovani, or L. aethiopica, is more variable. Mucosal lesions may develop without history of cutaneous disease, appear concurrently with primary skin lesions, or result from direct extension of a local cutaneous lesion (1-3). The progression of our patient’s cutaneous lesion leading to significant destruction of nasal mucosa is consistent with a diagnosis of MCL.
Diagnosis of ML can be challenging. Since mucosal lesions have a low parasite burden, cultures of lesion aspirates and histologic examination are often negative (1,2). Although sensitivity of serologic studies with indirect immunofluorescence assay (IIFA) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in patients with cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) can be low (20-80%), those with ML have higher rates of positivity (4,5) and often become serologically negative with successful treatment (6). Consequently, serology can play an important diagnostic role in patients with suspected ML whose direct diagnostic studies are negative.
Since our patient had no history of travel to other countries, he must have acquired his disease within Uganda. There is currently little data regarding the epidemiology of leishmaniasis in Uganda (7, 8). Transmission of visceral leishmaniasis due to L. donovani has been described in the northeastern Karamoja region (7), cutaneous leishmaniasis due to L. aethiopica is known to occur on the eastern and south-eastern slopes of Mount Elgon near the border of Kenya (7), and cases of equine leishmaniasis due to L. L. major occur in the Ssesse Islands in northern Lake Victoria (8). Cases of severe mucosal lesions caused by L. donovani have been described in Sudan (9) and, given the strong geographic and trade ties between Uganda and Sudan, it is possible that the catchment area for L. donovani has shifted further south to include northern Uganda where the patient was working around the time of symptom development. Further speciation of the patient’s leishmaniasis may assist in identifying the specific location of his disease transmission.
This case serves to highlight the importance of recognizing HIV/leishmaniasis co-infection. In people with HIV, leishmaniasis may present with severe, widely-disseminated visceral or cutaneous disease (10-13). Skin manifestations are more common and can occur in infections with Leishmania species which are not traditionally considered “dermatotropic”. Skin disease may also present in atypical forms including nodular, cheloid, or plaque-like lesions and can include significant mucosal involvement. Reports of rapid cutaneous or mucosal progression due immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) have been described after initiation of antiretroviral therapy, particularly in those with low CD4 cell counts (10-13). This may have contributed to our patient’s presentation since his lesion seemed to worsen around the time he was first started on antiretroviral therapy (ART) and again worsened when he restarted his ARVs.
As illustrated in our case, patients with HIV/leishmaniasis co-infection are frequently misdiagnosed even when biopsies are obtained (13). Common misdiagnoses include KS, leprosy, and autoimmune disease. As noted, this patient was misdiagnosed with KS on initial pathology evaluation. Histopathology in KS shows dermal proliferation due to spindle cell proliferation of endothelial cells that form sinuous vascular spaces. In leishmaniasis, dermal infiltration by lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells can be seen in histopathology. Diagnosis of leishmaniasis in those with HIV can also be difficult since serology may be negative even in widely disseminated disease, particularly in those with low CD4 cell counts (1,2).
Management of ML can be challenging and disease relapse is not uncommon. Systemic therapy with pentavalent antimonials (sodium stibogluconate or meglumine antimoniate) or amphotericin should be used (14, 15). Since people with HIV experience higher rates of complications with antimonials and case reports have demonstrated improved ML cure rates with the use of amphotericin (16), we opted to use amphotericin B when treating our patient. Although most of the studies of leishmaniasis therapy were done using amphotericin deoxycholate, liposomal amphotericin poses fewer side effects, demonstrates good efficacy and is considered equivalent for treatment purposes (14, 15). Small studies using oral miltefosine either alone or in combination with amphotericin for New World ML caused by L.V. braziliensis demonstrated good response (17-18) but the use of miltefosine has not been studied in Old World ML. Since patients with HIV are slower to respond to treatment and experience higher relapse rates, close clinical monitoring after treatment is important to ensure all skin lesions continue to resolve. Determination of cure is based solely on clinical response (14,15). Complete reepithelization and resolution of ulcer base induration should occur within three months and total lesion regression should be seen by six months after treatment. If these criteria are not met, the patient should be re-treated with a full course of systemic therapy, either with the same or an alternate treatment regimen. There is no data to support use of secondary prophylaxis to prevent disease relapse in ML, even in patients with HIV (15).
As seen in the photos (Figure 4) our patient’s lesions continue to epithelize and heal. He will be regularly monitored for complete disease resolution over the next six months and is currently being followed by plastic surgery in order to determine optimal timing of any necessary reconstructive surgery.
Final Diagnosis
Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis
