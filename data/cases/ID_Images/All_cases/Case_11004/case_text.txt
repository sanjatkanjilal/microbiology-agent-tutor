History of Present Illness
2011 IDSA Fellows’ Day Case Presentation Summary: A man with HIV infection and new fever and hypoxia, after recent Pneumocystis infection.
A man in his fifties with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, and admission for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) four weeks before, developed fever and acute respiratory distress with hypoxia.
During the previous admission for PCP, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) and a tapering course of corticosteroids were administered, with clinical improvement. He was then transferred to another facility.
Antiretroviral therapy was begun two weeks after the initiation of treatment for PCP and included raltegravir, etravirine, and ritonavir-boosted darunavir, which were chosen based on a genotype obtained several years earlier that revealed high-level nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) resistance. Eight days after the initiation of antiretrovirals, altered mental status and hypoxemia developed.
He was transferred to an intensive care unit (ICU), and methylprednisolone (one dose of 120 mg, intravenously) was administered; hypoxia and mental status improved. A diagnosis of immune reconstitution syndrome (IRIS) was made.
A second course of tapering corticosteroids was administered; however, respiratory distress and hypoxia recurred, for which the patient was intubated. Methylprednisolone (60 mg daily) was administered intravenously. The clinical course was complicated by pulmonary embolus and an ileus. Vancomycin and imipenem were administered for possible ventilator-associated pneumonia. During two weeks in the ICU, respiratory support was weaned to a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 40% and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to 5 cm H20. However, acute respiratory decompensation recurred, associated with temperatures to 38.5°C (101.3°F). Ventilatory support was increased (FiO2 70% and PEEP 10 cm H20).
Past Medical History
A diagnosis of HIV infection had been made more than 15 years earlier. Antiretroviral therapy was administered intermittently thereafter, and high level resistance to NRTIs was documented (genotypic mutations included M184V, M41L, L210W, T215Y, M46L, L10F, L33F, A71V and T74P). Opportunistic infections included Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) and oral candidiasis.
He also had a chronic psychiatric illness, hyperlipidemia and a history of alcohol abuse.
Medications
Medications included raltegravir, etravirine, ritonavir-boosted darunavir, TMP-SMX, corticosteroids, azithromycin, and risperdone.
Allergies
He had no known drug allergies.
Social History
The patient was born in a city in the Pacific Northwest and lived alone, with an older cat. He had never traveled outside of the United States. He drank alcohol, had a 30 pack-year smoking history, and occasionally smoked marijuana. He had never used injection drugs.
Physical Examination
He appeared chronically ill, thin, and was markedly diaphoretic. He was intubated and sedated, on a respirator. The temperature was 38.5°C (101.3°F), blood pressure 90/63 mm Hg, and pulse 144 beats per minute. The oxygen saturation was 98% on FiO2 70% and PEEP 10 cm H20. Breath sounds were mechanical, and slightly coarse on the right. The pulse rate was regular, and heart sounds were without any murmurs, rubs or gallops. The legs were edematous, and the remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
On admission, the CD4+ T-lymphocyte cell count was 22 cells/cubic millimeter and the level of HIV nucleic acid by PCR was 2.3 million copies/mL.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Bronchoscopic examination revealed white-tan plaques throughout the tracheal and bronchial tree, with some ulcerations (Figures 1 and 2, images courtesy of Dr. Sharon Peach). Endobronchial biopsies were obtained and bronchial alveolar lavage (BAL) performed. Specimens were sent for bacterial, fungal, and acid-fast staining, as well as viral studies and galactomannan. Serologic specimens for viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, and cytomegalovirus were obtained.
Acyclovir was administered intravenously for presumptive viral tracheobronchitis, and high level ventilatory support continued.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Pathological examination of specimens from the endobronchial biopsy revealed mycetomas (Figures 3 and 4, images courtesy of Dr. Rodney Schmidt) and necrotic tissue with eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions (Figure 5, image courtesy of Dr. Rodney Schmidt), consistent with a necrotizing viral infection.
Cultures of both the endobronchial biopsy and bronchoalveolar lavage grew Aspergillus fumigatus (Figure 6, image courtesy of Dr. Lindley Barbee). A galactomannan level obtained from the lavage fluid was 7.49 (normal < 0.499) The CMV shell vial culture was positive on day one, and the level of serum CMV nucleic acid was 14,000,000 copies/mL. Testing of the serum and BAL aspirate for VZV and HSV was negative, as were tests of the BAL aspirate for influenza and other respiratory viruses.

Treatment and Followup
Ganciclovir and voriconazole were administered intravenously, and the acyclovir was stopped.
During the following two weeks, the patient’s fevers resolved and his respiratory status improved, as marked by a decrease in the ventilatory support requirement. Unfortunately, he developed cutaneous Kaposi’s sarcoma (KS), a pneumothorax complicated by cardiac arrest that required resuscitation, and lastly, gastrointestinal bleeding thought to be due to either KS or cytomegalovirus infection. In consultation with the family, he was transitioned to comfort measures and, nearly four months after his original admission, he expired.
Discussion
Aspergillus tracheobronchitis occurs in less than 5% of all cases of invasive aspergillosis [Wu et al 2010]. It can manifest as either a bronchial obstruction from a pseuomembrane consisting of necrotic tissue, exudates and hyphal elements, or as discrete plaques in the trachea and bronchi [Clarke et al 1991, Denning et al 1991, Wu et al 2010], as seen in this patient. The lung parenchyma can also be involved [Clarke et al 1991, Denning et al 1991]. It is most often associated with severe immunosuppression, such as in the case of advanced HIV, stem cell transplant or lung transplant.
Among HIV patients, invasive pulmonary aspergillosis is rarely a cause of pulmonary infections (~1.4%) [Kemper et al 1993], and Aspergillus tracheobronchitis is even less common (3/35 cases) [Lortholary et al 1993]. When diagnosed in patients with HIV, Aspergillus tracheobronchitis is more commonly found in advanced cases (<100 CD4+ T-lymphocytes per cubic millimeter) and in those patients who have concomitant steroids or neutropenia [Kemper et al 1993, Klapholz et al 1991].
Treatment for Aspergillus tracheobronchitis is the same as for invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, with voriconazole being the antifungal agent of choice [Walsh et al 2008]. Amphotericin B is also an option if voriconazole is not tolerated [Walsh et al 2008]. Voriconazole dosing can be problematic among patients on ARVs as both are metabolized by the CYP450 enzymes. Voriconazole is both metabolized by and inhibits CYP3A4, CYP2C19 and CYP2C9. Ritonavir is also an important inhibitor of CYP3A as well as an inducer of CYP2C19. Thus when prescribing both voriconazole and ritonavir, higher levels of voriconazole are expected at first, as inhibition has a faster onset of action than induction. However, in steady state, decreased levels of voriconazole are expected. For this reason, it is recommended to avoid the concomitant administration of voriconazole and ritonavir. Another important ARV interaction with voriconazole is with efavirenz. Efavirenz both induces and inhibits CYP3A4. In studies, co-administration was shown to decrease voriconazole levels by 77% and raise efavirenz levels by 44%. When dosing voriconazole with efavirenz, it is recommended that one double the daily dose of voriconazole and halve the daily dose of efavirenz [Yakiwchu et al 2008]. When planning to co-administer voriconzole and anti-retrovirals, consultation with a pharmacist is recommended.
Final Diagnosis
Tracheobronchitis caused by Aspergillus fumigatus in a patient with HIV infection and AIDS.
