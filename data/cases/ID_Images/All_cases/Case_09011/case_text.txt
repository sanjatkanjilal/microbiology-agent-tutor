History of Present Illness
2009 November Featured Case and 2009 IDSA Pediatric Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A teenager with constipation and weight loss.
A teenaged male was well until 3 months before presentation, when constipation and anorexia developed. Multiple courses of laxatives were prescribed, without improvement, and weight loss of 30 pounds (13.6 kilograms) occurred. He also noted the development of fecal incontinence. There was no history of fever, chills, night-sweats, abdominal pain or diarrhea.
Past Medical History
The patient had previously been well.
Medications
The patient was not taking any medications at the time of presentation. He had reportedly received all childhood immunizations.
Allergies
The patient had no allergies.
Social History
He was a student.
Epidemiological History
The patient was born and lived in the northeastern United States. He did not smoke cigarettes, drink alcoholic beverages or use recreational drugs. He had been sexually active with one female partner and had used condoms. Several months before his presentation, he had traveled to Florida.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared thin. The temperature was 38.2°C (100.8°F), pulse 106 beats per minute, blood pressure 109/60 mm Hg and respirations 17 breaths per minute. The abdominal examination was normal; the rectal examination revealed slightly decreased anal tone and was otherwise normal, without bleeding or masses. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The leukocyte count was 12,000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4,000-10,500) with neutrophils 34% (ref 40-74), band forms 14% (ref 0-10), lymphocytes 50% (ref 19-48) and monocytes 2% (ref 3-11), hemoglobin 10 grams per deciliter (ref 12.5 - 16.1), hematocrit 32% (ref 36-47), platelet count 738,000 per cubic millimeter (ref 130,000-400,000) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 124 mm per hour (ref 0-15). The level of total protein was 8.6 grams per deciliter (ref 6.0-8.5), and albumin 3.2 grams per deciliter (ref 3.3-6.1). The C-reactive protein level was 3.5 mg per deciliter (ref less than or equal to 0.4). Other routine laboratory test results were normal.
Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis after the administration of oral and intravenous contrast revealed findings suggestive of an enhancing lesion in the rectum (Figures 1 and 2, arrows), which was not homogeneous. A large amount of stool was present in the colon, and multiple small inguinal lymph nodes (Figure 2, 'LN') were also noted.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A test for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) test was positive, and the level of CD4 T-lymphocytes was 739 cells per cubic millimeter. The plasma level of HIV ribonucleic acid was 24,000 copies per milliliter.
After receiving these test results, the patient reported having had receptive anal intercourse in the past, with a male partner that he met via the internet.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Colonoscopic examination to 75 centimeters from the anus revealed very edematous and friable rectal mucosa with nodularity. White plaque-like lesions were noted at approximately 5 to 7 cm, and an ulceration was visible at about 10-15 cm from the anus. Multiple samples were obtained for biopsy and microbiologic studies, including microscopic analysis of rectal, sigmoid and colonic tissue. Pathological examination with hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stain (Figure 3), revealed granulation tissue, with granulocytic, lymphocytic and plasmacytoid infiltration, and evidence of acute and chronic inflammation and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) hyperplasia. Gram’s, periodic acid-Schiff and acid-fast stains did not reveal any organisms. Routine bacterial and viral cultures were negative. Culture of tissue from the rectal biopsy for Chlamydia (done in cycloheximide-treated McCoy cells with culture confirmation with fluorescein-conjugated Chlamydia trachomatis species-specific monoclonal antibody) was positive. Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) of the urine for Neisseria gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis were negative.
Figure 3. H and E stain of biopsy.
Treatment and Followup
After the colonoscopic results were available, doxycycline (100 mg orally twice daily for three weeks) was administered, the currently recommended treatment [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2006, Mclean et al. 2007]. The patient’s symptoms improved within 48 hours of starting doxycycline and completely resolved within a week. The patient was followed in an adolescent HIV clinic. He was initially monitored off antiretroviral therapy, but subsequently started antiretroviral therapy after a decrease in his CD4 T-lymphocyte cell count.
Discussion
Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) was relatively rare in the United States and other industrialized countries until recently, and seen almost exclusively in cases imported from areas where LGV is still endemic, such as West and East Africa, India, Southeast Asia, South and Central America, and some Caribbean islands. In 2003, a cluster of LGV was reported in men who have sex with men (MSM) in the Netherlands [Nieuwenhuis et al. 2004]. This report was followed by several reports of similar cases throughout Europe, Canada and the United States (US) [Kropp et al. 2005, Meyer et al. 2005, Ward et al. 2007, NYC DOHMH BSTDC 2005].
Common characteristics of these outbreaks included the patient population affected, who were predominantly Caucasian men who had sex with men (MSM), the presentation as proctocolitis (without inguinal syndrome) and very high HIV co-infection rate (approximately 70%). Patients often reported multiple sexual contacts met at casual sex gatherings: 71% met partners at sex-on-premises venues or sex parties, and 23% had met partners via the internet. While LGV can be caused by Chlamydia trachomatis serotypes L1, L2, L3, most of the cases from the recent clusters in Europe and the US were due to L2 [Nieuwenhuis et al. 2004].
To our knowledge, cases in the pediatric population have not been reported since the emergence of the new clusters of cases in 2003. The diagnosis of LGV, in particular when it presents with proctocolitis, relies on a high index of suspicion that would lead to emphasizing certain aspects of the history and ordering the pertinent diagnostic tests. The diagnosis can be made by C. trachomatis testing: culturing the organism - such as in our case – or, more commonly by nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT). NAATs for C. trachomatis are not currently FDA-approved for testing rectal specimens. The role of serology in establishing the diagnosis of LGV is limited. A complement fixation (CF) titer of greater than 1:64 in the appropriate clinical setting can be supportive of the diagnosis. The diagnostic utility of other serologic tests commercially available has not been well established. Screening for other sexually-transmitted co-infections, including syphilis, gonorrhea and herpes simplex virus, should also be performed.
Complications of LGV proctocolitis, including rectal stricture, may occur if the diagnosis is not made and effective treatment is not given in a timely manner.
Final Diagnosis
Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) proctocolitis caused by Chlamydia trachomatis in an adolescent with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection.
