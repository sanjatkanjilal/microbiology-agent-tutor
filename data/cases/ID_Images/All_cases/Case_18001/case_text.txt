History of Present Illness
A teenage girl with a background history of cystic fibrosis and tracheomalacia presented with persistent moist cough of 6 weeks’ duration that had little response to oral antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate and azithromycin sequentially; sputum was negative for any bacteria). Prior to this episode, she did not have a daily cough. She had no fever, dyspnea or weight loss. Clinical examination was unremarkable. Her spirometry revealed pulmonary function deterioration: her forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) dropped from 2.62 L (94% predicted) six months earlier to 2.39 L (81% predicted) and forced vital capacity from 2.87 L (91% predicted) to 2.59 L (78% predicted). She was subsequently hospitalized for intravenous antibiotic therapy and intensive airway clearance.
The patient had been well until 3 years earlier when she required hospitalization for intravenous antibiotics for her first hospitalized pulmonary exacerbation. Her previous sputum cultures and annual cough swabs isolated Aspergillus fumigatus and Candidia spp. Pseudomonas aeruginosa had not previously been isolated from her sputum.
Past Medical History
She had meconium ileus soon after birth and her cystic fibrosis genotype was homozygous for delta F508, with pancreatic insufficiency. She had a functional endoscopic sinus surgery for nasal polyps a year earlier. She has no known allergies.
Medications
She was on both inhaled and oral medications for cystic fibrosis.
Inhaled medications: Dornase alfa (rhDNAse), mannitol, hypertonic saline and salbutamol.
Oral medication: Pancrealipase, vitamins A, D, E and K, salt.
Epidemiological History
She was born in Western Australia and moved to our center in Queensland, Australia 2 years earlier. She has no sick contacts, recent travel or household tobacco smoke exposure.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well with no dyspnea or tachypnea. Her body mass index was 19kg/m². Her cough was wet. She had no digital clubbing. Her chest was clear. Remainder of other examination was unremarkable.
Studies
The peripheral blood white blood cell count was 6.3x10 9 /L (reference range 4.5-13.0) with neutrophils 3.11 (49%), lymphocytes 2.19 (35%), monocytes 0.50 (8%), eosinophils 0.47 (8%) and basophils 0.03 (0%). The hemoglobin, platelets, electrolytes and liver function test were normal.
A chest radiograph showed increased bilateral perihilar markings.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
On day 13 of the admission, a chest CT scan was undertaken (Figures 1 & 2) because she still had a productive cough and a single sputum (on admission) isolated Mycobacterium abscessus. This showed incomplete anterior segmental collapse and mucus plugging with mild bronchiectasis of the right upper lobe (Figure 1) and moderate lingular cylindrical bronchiectasis with peribronchial thickening and the adjacent tree-in-bud opacification (Figure 2). Given the CT findings and the possibility of Mycobacterium abscessus disease, a bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was then performed and demonstrated viscous yellowish mucus plug obstructing the anterior segment of the right upper lobe (Figure 3). The tenacious plug was subsequently removed bronchoscopically via repeated suctioning (Figures 4 & 5).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The BAL total cell count was 80x10 6 /L and the differential cell count showed airway neutrophilia (62%) but no eosinophilia (0%). The BAL cultured Aspergillus fumigatus, no acid-fast bacilli or mycobacteria. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for respiratory viruses were negative.
Her total immunoglobulin E (IgE) was elevated - 165 kU/L on admission (was 55kU/L six months earlier). Radioallergosorbent test (RAST) for Aspergillus fumigatus reported as high positive. Serum Aspergillus precipitins was positive in one line.
Treatment and Followup
She was started on oral prednisolone and itraconazole. At follow up a month post treatment, she reported complete resolution of her respiratory symptoms. Prednisolone was gradually tapered off.
Discussion
Aspergillosis is a mycotic disease caused by Aspergillus spp., a filamentous fungus that is commonly encountered in organic waste, dust, compost and rotted materials. There are approximately 300 species of Aspergillus but only a few are known to be pathogenic in humans. 80% of clinical infections are caused by Aspergillus fumigatus complex. Its spores measure only 2-3 microns in diameter, therefore easily reach the terminal airways and alveoli where they can grow at body temperature [Chabi et al., 2015].
Pulmonary aspergillosis manifests as a wide spectrum of diseases, from simple aspergilloma to allergic or invasive disease [Soubani and Chandrasekar, 2002]. A more well-recognized form, simple aspergilloma usually occurs in a pre-existing cavitating lung disease. While invasive pulmonary aspergillosis is more commonly encountered in immunocompromised individuals, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), driven by an allergic reaction to Aspergillus antigens, almost exclusively seen in patients with asthma and cystic fibrosis (CF). Though these disease entities appear distinct, occasionally one may transform to another, or different forms can co-exist [Tunnicliffe et al., 2013].
The prevalence of ABPA in asthma ranges from 1-3.5% and in CF from 2-15% [Agarwal et al., 2013]. ABPA is clinically characterized by impaired mucociliary clearance, mucoid impaction with airway obstruction, and pulmonary infiltrates. The pathogenesis of ABPA is complex; it involves a non-invasive growth of Aspergillus spp. aided by poor airway clearance and a hypersensitive response via T-helper 2 (Th 2) pathway in genetically predisposed individuals. The release of fungal enzymes along with the sensitization lead to persistent airway inflammation and subsequent lung damage [Stevens et al., 2003; Thia and Lynn, 2009].
There is no single test, nor universally applied diagnostic criteria, for ABPA. The diagnostic approach has evolved over the past few decades, made on combination of clinical, radiological and immunological findings. Recently, a working group of ‘ABPA in asthmatics’ formed by the International Society of Human and Animal Mycology (ISHAM) proposed new diagnostic criteria to improve the accuracy in diagnosing ABPA [Agarwal et al., 2013]. These criteria include predisposing condition (asthma or cystic fibrosis) associated with both obligatory criteria and at least two out of three of other criteria. Obligatory criteria are positive skin test or elevated specific IgE to Aspergillus fumigatus and raised total IgE of >1000 IU/ml (equivalent to 1000 kU/L). Three other criteria are presence of precipitins or elevated serum IgG for Aspergillus fumigatus, radiographic changes consistent with ABPA and eosinophilia of >500 cells per microliter. The child did not meet all the criteria of ABPA but there is on-going debate on the diagnostic criteria, including the threshold for IgE [Greenberger et al., 2014].
Making a definite diagnosis of ABPA in patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) can be challenging as clinical and laboratory features overlap. Bronchiectasis and mucus plugging that may account for the chest symptoms commonly seen in ABPA are often present at baseline in CF. Central bronchiectasis and mucoid impaction involving predominantly the segmental and subsegmental bronchi of upper lobes typically described in ABPA may also occur in CF without ABPA [Kaur and Sudan, 2014]. Suspicion of ABPA should be raised if a patient fails to respond to intravenous antibiotics. Our patient did not fulfill all the diagnostic criteria of ABPA but given the supportive tests, her poor response to multiple courses of antibiotics and no other etiology encountered, bronchopulmonary aspergillosis was the most likely diagnosis.
Bronchoscopic examination of the tracheobronchial tree has diagnostic and therapeutic value, as illustrated in this patient. The tenacious mucoid impaction was described as plastic bronchitis, a syndrome of pulmonary aspergillosis but this is controversial as there remains disagreement [Chetty, 2003].
Systemic corticosteroids are the mainstays of therapy to reduce the inflammation and the immunological activity. Antifungal agents act as steroid sparing agent with attenuation of the antigen burden arising from fungal colonization [Patterson and E, 2014]. Early recognition and treatment of Aspergillus infection may prevent progressive lung damage particularly in those with pre-existing lung disease.
Final Diagnosis
Allergic or non-allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis in a patient with cystic fibrosis
