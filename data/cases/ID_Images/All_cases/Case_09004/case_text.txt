History of Present Illness
2009 April Featured Case Summary: A woman in her thirties with fever and diarrhea after traveling to Africa.
A woman in her thirties was admitted to the hospital after developing fever and diarrhea after traveling to Africa.
In mid-summer, five weeks before her departure for Kenya and Tanzania, the patient was seen in a travel clinic. Immunizations for tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis and yellow fever were administered parenterally, and oral typhoid vaccine given. Malarial prophylaxis with atovaquone and proguanil, and azithromycin as needed for diarrhea, were prescribed.
In Africa, she traveled to rural areas and went camping in the plains and mountainous regions. She ate local fare, drank boiled water and used iodine tablets. During the first week, she noted 2 mosquito bites, and none thereafter. One week after her arrival in Africa, episodes of diarrhea developed, for which she took loperamide, with improvement but not complete resolution of her symptoms. On the return flight home, 16 days after her arrival, she felt feverish and developed shaking chills, myalgias, and arthralgias, of 4 hours’ duration, and generalized malaise, associated with a mild cough and post-nasal drip.
During the following 4 days, malaise and fatigue gradually improved. On the 5th day, fever to 100.3°F (37.9°C) developed, associated with headache, chills, myalgias, and nausea. The next day, the temperature rose to 102°F (38.9°C), and mild abdominal pain developed. She vomited once and had multiple episodes of watery diarrhea, without blood. She also reported a frontal headache, stiff neck, and photophobia. She was not aware of any travel companions’ illnesses, and was not sexually active during the trip.
Past Medical History
She had a history of eosinophilic esophagitis. She had received the complete vaccination series for Hepatitis A virus, Hepatitis B virus, rabies, measles, mumps and rubella, tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis, polio (with a booster dose as an adult) and had had chickenpox in the past. Testing for human immunodeficiency virus had been negative a decade earlier.
Medications
Medications included atovaquone-proguanil, and ibuprofen and acetaminophen as needed for fever and pain.
Allergies
She was allergic to sulfa drugs.
Epidemiological History
She was born on the west coast of the United States. She had recently traveled to Kenya and Tanzania, and to Europe, India, Southeast Asia and Istanbul within the previous 4 years. She was monogamous with one male partner, and had always had protected intercourse. She occasionally drank alcohol and did not smoke or use illicit drugs. Paternal relatives had eosinophilic esophagitis.
Physical Examination
The temperature was 103.0°F (39.4°C), blood pressure 89/61 mm Hg, pulse 80 beats per minute, respirations 12 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 100% while breathing ambient air. The mucous membranes were dry, the cheeks were flushed, and there was tenderness with percussion over the right maxillary sinus and flexion of the neck. The abdomen was soft, with normal bowel sounds and minimal tenderness to deep palpation in the lower abdomen. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 11,500 cells per cubic millimeter (reference range 4500-11,000) with 81% neutrophils, 12% lymphocytes, 4% monocytes, 2% eosinophils and 1% basophils, hemoglobin 14.6 gm/dl (ref 12.0-16.0) and platelets 484,000 per cubic millimeter (ref 150,000-400,000). The level of potassium was 3.2 mmol/liter (ref 3.4-4.8), chloride 97 mmol/liter (ref 100-108), and glucose 136 mg/dl (non-fasting); the levels of other electrolytes, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, amylase, lipase, bilirubin, total protein, albumin, globulin, and tests of coagulation, renal and liver function were normal. Urine analysis showed yellow clear urine with pH 6.0, 1+ bilirubin, 2+ ketones, and 3-5 white cells, few squamous cells and few bacteria per high-powered field. An electrocardiogram was normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
In the emergency department, vancomycin (1 gram), ceftriaxone (1 gram), ciprofloxacin (500 mg), atovaquone and proguanil were administered, as well as normal saline (4 liters over 24 hours, intravenously). The blood pressure rose to 114/74 mm Hg.
Computed tomography of the brain showed bilateral maxillary sinus thickening, and was otherwise normal. A lumbar puncture revealed 1 white cell per cubic millimeter (cmm)(47% neutrophils and 53% lymphocytes) and 1805 red cells/cmm in Tube 1 and 1 white cell/cmm (20% neutrophils and 70% lymphocytes) and 49 red cells/cmm in tube 4, with a glucose level of 63 mg/dl and total protein of 22 mg/dl. A chest radiograph was normal. Gram stain of the cerebrospinal fluid demonstrated moderate mononuclear cells and few red cells, with no neutrophils or organisms. A blood smear for malaria was negative.

Treatment and Followup
After results implicating Cryptosporidium parvum were received, nitazoxanide was administered for 3 days, with marked improvement but without total resolution of her symptoms. One day later, stool cultures grew Campylobacter jejuni; no additional antibiotic treatment was administered. She returned to the Infectious Disease clinic 2.5 weeks later because of persistent intermittent soft stools and abdominal cramping. Follow-up stool cultures and ova and parasites were negative. She was advised to abstain from dairy consumption for 1 week, and then gradually increase her dietary intake, with improvement. Testing for antibodies to Strongyloides was negative one month later; test results for evidence of human immunodeficiency virus were also negative.
Discussion
Fever and diarrhea in a returning traveler may be caused by multiple infectious agents, most of which are self-limited, but some of which are potentially life-threatening. Information should be sought regarding the diseases endemic to the areas visited, and the patient’s risk factors and potential exposures. In febrile patients who have traveled to a malaria-endemic area, specimens of blood should be examined for Plasmodium species. Infectious gastroenteritides caused by bacteria (including enteropathogenic Escherichia coli, Salmonella (such as S. enteritidis, S. typhi, the cause of typhoid fever, or S. paratyphi), Shigella, Campylobacter, and Vibrio cholerae) or parasitic agents (such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium) should also be strongly considered. Viral gastroenteritides (such as rotovirus or Norwalk virus) have been implicated in significant outbreaks in traveler’s diarrhea, and may be associated with low-grade fevers, but are not usually as prolonged as in this case. Dengue fever is relatively uncommon in Africa, and usually presents with fever, myalgias, and thrombocytopenia. Other illnesses, such as leptospirosis, rickettsial diseases, and acute human immunodeficiency virus infection, should also be considered, particularly in patients with potential exposure histories.
This case illustrates how travelers may be exposed to multiple infectious agents. Cryptosporidium parvum, an intracellular protozoan pathogen found worldwide, causes about 2% of traveler’s diarrhea, and is a common cause of childhood diarrhea in developing countries. It can be transmitted by animal contact, person-to-person and environmental (waterborne) exposure. Oocysts can be found in lakes, rivers, swimming pools (usually due to fecal accidents), vegetables, unpasteurized milk and contaminated apple, and are highly resistant to chlorination. The largest outbreak related to contaminated drinking water occurred in Milwaukee in 1993, with an estimated 403,000 people stricken with a diarrheal illness [Kramer et al., 1996]. Clinical manifestations of infection usually begin within 5-7 days of ingestion, and include watery diarrhea, fever, and abdominal cramps and, less commonly, symptoms related to the biliary tract (acalculous cholecystitis, sclerosing cholangitis) and respiratory tract (bilateral respiratory infiltrates). A prodrome of fever, mylagia and headache may occur around the time of onset of diarrhea. The median duration of illness is between 5 to 10 days. It is usually self-limited, but anti-parasitic agents nitazoxanide and paromomycin have also been used in the setting of severe illness and/or immunocompromised state. Infection in immunocompetent patients is generally localized to distal small bowel and proximal colon. However, in immunocompromised patients, parasites have been found throughout the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, hepatobiliary system and respiratory tract. Chronic intestinal cryptosporidiosis is an AIDS-defining illness.
Campylobacter jejuni is a comma-shaped gram-negative rod found in the GI tract of cattle, goats, sheep and poultry, and may cause both enteric and extraintestinal illness. It may be transmitted via consumption of contaminated water and food, including poultry, raw clams, undercooked beef and unpasteurized cheeses. Other modes of transmission include direct contact with animals, person-to-person transmission, or fecal-oral contact. The incubation period after exposure ranges between 1 and 7 days and most commonly a prodrome of fever, headache and malaise develops before the onset of acute enteritis, with crampy abdominal pain and watery diarrhea (which may be bloody). The clinical course is usually a self-limited, typically lasting between 1 to 7 days. Treatment includes fluid and electrolyte replacement, with antibiotic treatment generally reserved for patients with high fever, bloody diarrhea, symptoms lasting more than 1 week, or those with eight or more bowel movements a day. Other manifestations include colitis, pseudoappendicitis, and bacteremia. The choice of antibiotics may include macrolides or fluoroquinolones, depending on resistance patterns in the area. Complications arising from C. jejuni infections include reactive arthritis which may be especially severe and prolonged in patients with HLA-B27 antigens, interstitial nephritis, hemolytic uremic syndrome and Guillain-Barre syndrome (GBS). Reports suggest that 20-40% of GBS cases occur after C. jejuni infections, usually 2-3 weeks after diarrheal illness. Of note, a significant percentage of Campylobacter isolates, especially from Southeast Asia, are now resistant to fluoroquinolone antibiotics.
Supportive care is often all that is needed for traveler’s diarrhea, as many episodes are self-limited. Travel clinics may prescribe antibiotics for self-administration by travelers going to remote areas. In some circumstances, however, antibiotics have been shown to prolong symptoms and shedding of the infectious agents, especially when combined with other anti-diarrheal agents such as loperamide. In this patient, because of the duration and increasing symptoms, hypotension, and possible meningeal signs, broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered until test results, including blood and stool cultures, were available.
Final Diagnosis
Gastroenteritis caused by Campylobacter jejuni and Cryptosporidium parvum infection in a traveler returning from Africa.
