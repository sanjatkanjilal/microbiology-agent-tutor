History of Present Illness
A woman in her forties with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) associated with HIV who previously declined antiretroviral therapy (ART) presented with 4 months of progressive ataxia.
She developed unsteadiness with ambulation 4 months before admission. She reported that her symptoms began abruptly and slowly progressed in severity. Over the next 4 months, her progressive imbalance required using a cane, and then later, a walker, to even stand up. She also noted mild coordination impairment in her upper extremities and reported frequently dropping objects. She had no loss of strength or sensory deficit in her extremities.
On review of symptoms, the patient denied cognitive decline, dysarthria, visual or hearing deficits, bowel or bladder incontinence, fever, constitutional symptoms, respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms, or rash.
Past Medical History
She was diagnosed with HIV infection during pregnancy. She briefly took ART during her previous pregnancies but otherwise had not been on therapy. She had no history of ART resistance and had been off antiretrovirals or chemoprophylaxis for opportunistic infections for years.
Medications
None
Allergies
None
Epidemiological History
The patient was born in the Northeastern United States and lived there with her son and cat, for whom she cleaned the litter box. She was not sexually active. The patient had a distant history of heavy alcohol use; she did not smoke and denied recreational drug use.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. Her blood pressure was 167/95 mm Hg, pulse 79 beats per minute, temperature 36.8° C (98.3° F), and respiratory rate 16 breaths per minute. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal exams were normal. Extremities were without edema, and no rash was noted on the skin.
On neurologic exam, she was alert and oriented, her speech was fluent, and her short-term memory was not significantly impaired. Her cranial nerve exam was within normal limits; no nystagmus was noted, but she did have mild dysarthria. Her strength was normal in all extremities, and her sensation was intact to light touch, vibration, and position. She had appendicular dysmetria with abnormal finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin tests bilaterally. She also had bilateral dysdiadochokinesia and truncal ataxia with a wide-based gait. Her deep tendon reflexes were brisk and symmetric.
Studies
Laboratory findings were notable for a normal metabolic panel, white blood cell count 4.2 K/uL [reference range 4.0-10.0 K/µL] with 69.3% neutrophils [reference range 48-76%], and 19.4% lymphocytes [reference range 18-41%]. HIV plasma viral load was 89,300 copies/ml, CD4+ lymphocyte count was 48 cells/µL (6.1%) [reference range 441-2,156 cells/µL (10-40%)], and HIV genotyping did not evidence of resistance.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with and without contrast did not show acute infarct, mass, hemorrhage, or abnormal enhancement but did demonstrate disproportionate cerebellar parenchymal volume loss (Figure 1). A CT neck obtained 6 years before for an unrelated odontogenic infection is shown (Figure 2) to provide a better comparison of the parenchymal volume loss. A spine MRI with and without contrast showed non-specific faint leptomeningeal enhancement along the cauda equina nerve roots.
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies showed 4 total nucleated cells [reference range 0 - 5 /uL] with 0% neutrophils, 84% lymphocytes, and 16% monocytes. The total protein was elevated at 78.2 mg/d [reference range 10.0 - 44.0 mg/dL], and glucose was 55 mg/dL [reference range: 40 - 70 mg/dL].

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
CSF studies showed a non-reactive VDRL and negative cryptococcus antigen. CSF PCR was negative for herpes simplex type 1 and 2, cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, varicella-zoster, and Toxoplasma gondii, but positive for JC virus (JCV). JC virus PCR in blood was also detectable. CSF cytology, paraneoplastic, and autoimmune panels were negative.
Based on her clinical presentation, radiologic findings, and the presence of the JC virus in blood and CSF, a final diagnosis of JC virus granule cell neuronopathy (JCV-GCN) was determined.
Treatment and Followup
The patient agreed to resume antiretroviral therapy and was started on Bictegravir-emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide. Cessation of alcohol intake was also recommended. She was seen in the clinic 2 weeks after restarting antiretrovirals. Her cerebellar symptoms did not progress, and she noted a mild improvement in her balance and functional status. A repeat HIV PCR showed a significant decline to 91 copies/ml. A repeat brain MRI obtained after three months of therapy did not show significant changes in cerebellar atrophy.
Discussion
There are several clinical syndromes associated with the reactivation of JC virus in the central nervous system. Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) is the more classical presentation characterized by a lytic infection of glial cells resulting in areas of demyelination in the brain parenchyma (1). Importantly, JCV can infect cells other than oligodendrocytes and astrocytes, leading to the description of novel neurological presentations, including JCV-associated encephalopathy, JCV-associated meningitis, and JCV-GCN (2,3).
JC-GCN is characterized by cerebellar granule cell destruction with sparing of Purkinje cells and may occur in isolation or in conjunction with classic PML findings (3,4). It has been proposed that specific mutations in the VP1 capsid gene of the virus are responsible for its unique tropism (5). The destruction of granule cell neurons causes a subacute to chronic presentation characterized by cerebellar dysfunction, with symptoms including incoordination, gait ataxia, and dysarthria (2).
While JC-GCN appears to be relatively rare, this syndrome may be under-recognized. Cases of previously unexplained cerebellar atrophy in patients with HIV may not have been attributed to JCV infection (6), as not all previously reported cases underwent cerebellar biopsy or JCV studies. Since its original description in patients with HIV infection (3), JCV-GCN has been reported in other immunosuppressive conditions unrelated to HIV infection, including primary immunodeficiencies (7), autoimmune conditions (8), and associated with rituximab (9) and natalizumab treatment (10,11).
MRI characteristically shows bilateral and diffuse cerebellar atrophy and may demonstrate confluent T2 and FLAIR hyperintensities in the cerebellum without contrast enhancement or diffusion restriction (12). Similar concomitant lesions within the cerebellar peduncles and the pons have also been reported (13).
A confirmatory diagnosis can be established by cerebellar biopsy, showing JCV-mediated lytic infection of the granule cell neurons; however, JC-GCN is generally diagnosed in the proper clinical and radiological setting, with the concurrent detection of JCV DNA by PCR in the CSF (2,13), as this patient had. In addition, the patient possibly had a component of concomitant alcohol-induced cerebellar ataxia, even though her alcohol consumption history and acuity of symptoms are not conclusive.
Currently, no JCV-targeted treatment is available; treatment mainly focuses on reversing the underlying immunosuppressive conditions. In patients with HIV infection, rapid initiation of antiretroviral treatment is considered the cornerstone of treatment (14).
This case report highlights the clinical and radiological presentation of JC-GCN, a unique neurologic presentation associated with JCV infection, and a likely underreported cause of cerebellar disorders in patients with advanced HIV and other immunosuppressive conditions. Infectious disease providers should be aware of its distinctive presentation and have a high index of suspicion in the correct clinical setting. Isolated cerebellar disease in an immunocompromised patient should suggest the possibility of JC-GCN even in the absence of typical findings of PML.
Final Diagnosis
Cerebellar atrophy due to JC virus granule cell neuronopathy in a patient with HIV/AIDS.
