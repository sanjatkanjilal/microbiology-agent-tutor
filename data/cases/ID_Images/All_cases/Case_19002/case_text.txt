History of Present Illness
A female in her twenties, originally from Central America, presented with an 8-week history of dizziness and headache. She described her dizziness as a spinning sensation and endorsed unsteadiness and subjective weakness in her legs. She complained of a daily headache which was sharp in quality and frontotemporal in distribution, made worse with neck flexion and bending down. This was associated with nausea, vomiting and photophobia.
She endorsed visual disturbance, describing objects jumping across her visual field. She otherwise denied fever, weight loss, cough, abdominal pain, changes in bladder or bowel habits, rash, arthralgia/myalgia, seizures or gait disturbance. Denied sick contacts.
Epidemiological History
She immigrated from Central America where she lived on a farm. She is an omnivore. No travel in the past year otherwise.
Physical Examination
Patient appeared uncomfortable, retching intermittently, with a bedpan on her lap. Vitals: blood pressure 111/57, pulse 63, temperature 98.1ºF (36.7ºC), respiratory rate 16, SpO 2 98% on room air.
She was alert and oriented and following commands. Her neurological examination revealed intact cranial nerves though the neurology service appreciated mild papilledema in the right eye and was unable to visualize the optic disc on the left. Her peripheral strength was normal and sensation intact. There were no cerebellar signs and reflexes were equal. Her gait was slow and hesitant but otherwise she was able to walk unassisted. There was no meningismus with forward flexion.
Studies
Her Complete Blood Count was notable for a Hemoglobin of 11.7 K/uL (Reference: 12-16) and Hematocrit of 34.2% (Reference: 36-46) with no leukocytosis. Her chemistry was unremarkable. Microbiology studies notable for recent negative HIV and Hepatitis B serologies.
Her MRI Brain (Figure 1) was notable for evidence of acute hydrocephalus with diffuse ventricular and cerebral aqueduct dilatation, with an irregular multiloculated cyst along the floor of the fourth ventricle.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Given the hydrocephalus and ventricular lesion, the patient was admitted to the Neurosurgery service with plan for surgical evacuation of cyst and decompression.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
She underwent a suboccipital craniotomy and evacuation of the cyst. The operative report noted that the fourth ventricle was opened with moderate pressure of cerebrospinal fluid. Yellow cystic material was appreciated immediately underneath this opening, which slid out with very gentle pressure (Figure 2). The ventricle was irrigated and the walls of the fourth ventricle appeared yellowish and gliotic. Ultrasound navigation confirmed that the fourth ventricle was decompressed and there were no obvious cysts remaining.
Pathology confirmed the presence of non-viable, degenerated, hyalinized material recognizable as a neurocysticercosis cyst wall with a cuticular layer, middle cellular layer and an inner reticular layer (Figures 3-5). Adjacent brain showed chronic inflammation and reactive gliosis. She tested positive for serum IgG antibodies against Taenia solium (T solium) glycoproteins.

Treatment and Followup
Postoperatively, MRI spine was performed and detected no additional cysticerci. With no intra-parenchymal or spinal disease, her disease was categorized as purely intraventricular, and she was considered cured after surgical removal of the cyst. Thus, antiparasitic therapy was deferred and follow-up MRI ordered in 6 months to ensure resolution.
At a follow-up visit one month after discharge, she expressed resolution of her dizziness, headache, and nausea, and her surgical wounds were healing well.
Discussion
Neurocysticercosis (NCC) remains an important cause of central nervous system (CNS) infection in the developing world, with an estimated prevalence of 50 million infected worldwide. [1] The causative microbe is the parasite Taenia solium and the disease has two manifestations depending on where humans interact with the life cycle: egg versus cyst. Ingestion of eggs shed into the feces of a human tapeworm carrier leads to cysticercosis, characterized by systemic tissue deposition of cysts harboring the larvae. NCC is a subtype of cysticercosis where the site of cyst deposition involves the brain and spinal cord. [2] Pigs represent the other major hosts outside humans for larval cysts. Ingestion of pork infected with these cysts leads to the second disease entity, tapeworm infection in the gut, known as Taeniasis. When cysts find their way into the human gut, they undergo development into adult tapeworms and establish infection to continue the life cycle.
NCC is further characterized by its distribution within the CNS and can be separated into intra-parenchymal and extra-parenchymal disease. Intra-parenchymal NCC is the most common form of cysticercosis and often presents clinically with seizures. [3] Extra-parenchymal disease can be further sub-divided into intraventricular, subarachnoid, spinal, and ocular lesions, which more commonly present with symptoms related to hydrocephalus and mass effect. The diagnosis of NCC is made primarily on clinical and radiographic grounds and can be reinforced with serologies. Both CT and MR imaging should be performed where possible when NCC is suspected. CT imaging performs well at identifying parenchymal cysts and calcifications, while MRI can detect edema around lesions, degenerative changes, as well as scolices (head of cyst facilitating attachment to bowel wall) within lesions. [4]
Serologies can support the diagnosis of NCC when imaging is suggestive but not pathognomonic. Enzyme-linked immunotransferase blot (EITB) has replaced ELISA as the test of choice for cysticercosis as it demonstrates higher sensitivity and specificity in serum, with sensitivity approaching 100% in patients with multiple parenchymal lesions or with disease in the ventricles or subarachnoid space. [5] Indeed, our patient tested positive for serum IgG antibodies against T solium glycoproteins. In our patient case, we were fortunate to have pathology confirming the diagnosis of neurocysticercosis. The other differential diagnoses like tuberculoma and toxoplasmosis were less likely given her negative T-SPOT during this admission and HIV-negative status during recent prenatal screening. While Echinococcus can also cause cystic disease, her positive serologies against T solium lent additional support to the diagnosis.
The management of NCC varies by the site of disease. In 2017, the Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) published consensus guidelines for the management of NCC. [5] In our patient case, intraoperative findings along with head and spine imaging confirmed that her disease was confined to the 4th ventricle without parenchymal involvement. Cystic obstruction of the 4th ventricle led to hydrocephalus and symptoms consistent with increased intracranial pressure. Per IDSA guidelines, the recommended management of intraventricular NCC confined to the fourth ventricle is surgical removal rather than medical therapy where possible. Preoperative antiparasitic therapy was not provided given the concern that treatment can disrupt cyst integrity and complicate surgical removal. [6] Moreover, we had yet to exonerate any spinal involvement, and case reports demonstrate the potential for treatment to cause cystic degeneration and a robust inflammatory response which, in the confines of the spinal canal, can cause irreversible damage. [7]
The major clinical question following successful removal of our patient’s intraventricular cyst was whether to provide adjunctive postoperative antiparasitic therapy. IDSA guidelines recommend adjunctive treatment for intraventricular NCC only if complete cyst removal is not possible and shunt insertion is performed instead. In this subset of patients, antiparasitic therapy may reduce the likelihood of subsequent shunt failure. [4, 8] In our patient case, pre- and post-operative MRI of the head and spine confirmed disease confinement to the fourth ventricle, and thus surgery was deemed curative. Medical treatment with anti-parasitics (albendazole and/or praziquantel) are used in patients with intra-parenchymal disease that has not calcified, or in patients with extra-parenchymal disease where cysts cannot be removed surgically. Anti-parasitics are often used with corticosteroids to minimize damage secondary to the inflammatory response.
At one month after discharge, the patient endorsed resolution of her headache and dizziness and her surgical site was healing well. She will undergo repeat MRI in six months post-discharge to confirm resolution and stability.
Final Diagnosis
Intraventricular Neurocysticercosis of the 4th ventricle
