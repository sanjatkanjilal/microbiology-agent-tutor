History of Present Illness
IDSA 2007 Fellows Day Case Presentation Summary: A newborn with a rash and hepatosplenomegaly.
A male infant was delivered by emergency cesarean section because of suspected maternal genital herpes infection. The neonate was the product of a full-term pregnancy. The mother was in her thirties, gravida 2 para 1, and had a history of cocaine addiction. Her previous pregnancy was complicated by gonorrhea and syphilis, for which she had been treated one year earlier. The last known rapid plasma reagin (RPR) titer was 1:32. Serological test results for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) had been negative. Amniotic membranes were ruptured at home; the period of elapsed time to presentation at the hospital was not known.
Past Medical History
There had not been any maternal prenatal care and prenatal laboratory test results were not known.
Physical Examination
The weight was 2.5 kilograms; weight and head circumference were at the 50th percentile. Apgar scores were 3, 6 and 8 at 1, 5 and 10 minutes, respectively. There was poor respiratory effort, and the patient was intubated emergently and placed under a radiant warmer. The temperature was 97.8°F (36.5°C); other vital signs were normal. Examination of the skin revealed multiple petechiae (mostly on the torso), and rounded cutaneous lesions that measured 0.3 centimeters (cm) to 1 cm on the palms and soles (Figures 1 through 4, the feet are shown after ink was applied for footprinting). The heart sounds were normal. There were bilateral rales in the lungs, hepatosplenomegaly, bilateral axillary lymphadenopathy and hypotonia. He was poorly responsive to the examination.

Studies
The white cell count was 25,000 cells/mm³ (neutrophils 21%, lymphocytes 47%, monocytes 22%, metamyelocytes 2%), hemoglobin 11.8 g/dL, platelet count 9,000/mm³, aspartate aminotransferase 1,219 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 219 U/L; prothrombin time 28.7 seconds and partial thromboplastin time 48.1 seconds. Other laboratory test results were normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A chest radiograph revealed an increased cardiac silhouette and no radiographic evidence of pneumonia. A computed brain tomography scan and abdominal ultrasound were normal.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Maternal rapid plasma reagin (RPR) testing at the time of delivery was positive at 1:256, and the infant’s RPR at 1:16. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed 350 white cells per cubic millimeter (14% segmented polymorphonuclear leukocytes), protein 423 mg/dL, and a reactive Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test, with a titer of 4. Radiographs of the long bones showed metaphyseal osteochondritis and periostitis (Figure 5), commonly found in congenital syphilis. The findings were consistent with the diagnosis of congenital syphilis, caused by Treponema pallidum infection.
Laboratory test results of the mother's serum did not show evidence of infection with hepatitis B virus or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and immunity to rubella was documented. Cultures of the infant's blood at birth were negative. Cytomegalovirus was not detected in the urine by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or culture. Bacterial cultures of the CSF and testing for herpes simplex virus PCR were negative. Viral cultures of the skin, eyes, rectum, and nasopharynx were negative, and cultures of the cutaneous lesions did not reveal bacterial pathogens. Newborn laboratory screening tests for metabolic diseases and hemoglobinopathies were normal.
Figure 5. Radiographs of long bones.
Treatment and Followup
The infant was treated with intravenous penicillin G crystalline (150,000 U/kg/day) for 14 days. Follow-up laboratory test results are shown in Figure 6, and measurement of the RPR at 2 months was positive at 1:4, reflecting a four-fold decrease.

Discussion
The constellation of findings at birth in this case indicated the need to consider the TORCH group of infections, as well as other unusual disorders. (The TORCH acronym represents the following infections: toxoplasmosis; other infections including hepatitis B, syphilis, and herpes zoster; rubella; cytomegalovirus; and herpes simplex virus.)
Serious involvement of the central nervous system, intrauterine growth retardation, and petechiae and purpura (i.e., blueberry muffin baby) are characteristic of symptomatic congenital cytomegalovirus infection. Infants with staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome are usually very ill and have diffuse skin involvement. Kawasaki disease is an extremely rare diagnosis in a neonate and presents with rather atypical features.
Congenital syphilis continues to be diagnosed in the United States, particularly in infants of mothers with no or inadequate prenatal care. Pregnant women with syphilis should receive adequate treatment and follow up to document cure.
Cutaneous lesions of the palms and soles are classic manifestations of Treponema pallidum infection and are highly contagious until 24 hours after institution of penicillin therapy. Significant hepatitis can occur, and may resolve slowly after institution of therapy. Serious neurologic sequelae may occur as a result of congenital syphilis; audiology testing, ophthalmologic exam and early neurodevelopment intervention are necessary to improve the outcome of affected infants.
Final Diagnosis
Congenital syphilis, caused by Treponema pallidum infection, with hepatitis and central nervous system involvement.
