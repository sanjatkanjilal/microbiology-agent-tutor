History of Present Illness
2014 May-June Featured Case: A man in his thirties with a history of Hepatitis C virus infection and substance use disorder was seen in an emergency department after a fall from a bicycle; he reported recent illicit narcotic use. On examination, he initially appeared intoxicated, somnolent, and awoke intermittently to name and sternal rub. The temperature was 101.2°F (38.4°C) and rose to 104.2°F (40.1°C); other vital signs were normal. An acute laceration on the head and numerous abrasions were present, as well as injection marks on both arms. Breath sounds were shallow and coarse; heart sounds were regular, with a 1/6 systolic ejection murmur. He moved all extremities, and the remainder of the examination was normal. The white cell count was 5800 cells per cubic millimeter (reference range (ref) 4500-11,000), with 76.9% neutrophils, 11.6% lymphocytes, 10.5% monocytes, 0.3% eosinophils and 0.5% basophils; the hematocrit was 34.4% (ref 41.0-53.0). The blood level of the potassium was 3.0 mmol per liter (ref 3.4-4.8), alanine aminotransferase 95 U/L (ref 10-55) and aspartate aminotransferase 151 U/L (ref 10-40). Three sets of blood cultures were drawn. A chest radiograph showed no acute infiltrate. The mental status improved and the temperature returned to normal. Additional evaluation was planned, but was not completed as the patient requested discharge from the hospital.
Two days later, the patient was brought back to the emergency department by paramedical personnel after involvement in a motor vehicle accident; a cervical collar had been applied. On examination, numerous lacerations and a fractured fibula were noted. On review of the previously obtained blood cultures, two of three aerobic bottles were noted to be growing gram-positive bacilli.
The patient reported injecting narcotics with a needle and syringe someone had given him on the day before the blood cultures had been obtained. He reported using only clean needles, with tap water for injection, and noted he did not lick his needles. He was otherwise in his usual health, with no recent history of chills, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, chest pain, shortness of breath, joint pain, back pain or rashes.
Past Medical History
He had a history of alcohol use (6 beers per night) and substance use disorder, including heroin, cocaine, amphetamines and benzodiazepines. He reportedly had had seizures in the past that were thought to be caused by alcohol withdrawal. He had a history of hepatitis C virus infection; a viral nucleic acid level was undetectable approximately 8 months earlier. He also had depression, anxiety, and a neuropathy.
Medications
He was on no other medications.
Social History
He was homeless and had been incarcerated in the past.
Epidemiological History
He reported no recent travel, sick contacts, or animal exposures.
Physical Examination
The patient was somnolent and in no apparent distress; his drowsiness was thought to be caused by intoxication. The temperature was 100.5°F (38.1°C), blood pressure 103/63mm Hg, pulse 57 beats per minute, respirations 18 per minute and oxygen saturation 96% while breathing ambient air. There was a laceration over the left forehead and clotted blood scattered around the nose; a laceration across the chin had been sutured. There were missing teeth. No conjunctival hemorrhages were noted. The heart rate and rhythm were regular and the sounds were soft, with no murmurs, rubs or gallops. Breath sounds were diminished but clear. The abdomen was soft, non-distended and diffusely tender. The range of motion of the right leg was limited by pain. The extremities were warm and well-perfused. Abrasions over the dorsum of both hands were present, without Osler nodes or Janeway lesions. Neurological examination was limited by somnolence, but he was arousable, with no gross focal deficits.
Studies
The hematocrit was 31.2% and the white cell count was 4000 per cubic millimeter with 54% neutrophils, 26% lymphocytes, 14% monocytes, and 4% eosinophils. The blood level of alanine aminotransferase was 204 U/liter and aspartate aminotransferase 200 U/liter; the platelet count and blood levels of alkaline phosphatase, total and direct bilirubin, urea nitrogen and creatinine were normal. Toxicology screening of the urine and blood revealed methadone, diazepam, citalopram, cocaine, benzodiazepines, opioids, and tetrahydrocannabinol (THC).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Of the three sets of blood cultures drawn during the patient's first evaluation in the emergency department, two aerobic bottles grew gram-positive bacilli within 24 and 48 hours respectively. Figure 1, from the growth at 24-48 hours, reveals pleomorphic gram-positive bacilli. Colony growth on Brucella horse blood plate appeared pale pink, wet and mucoid on day 4 (Figure 2).
Morphology on gram stain varied from pleomorphic gram-positive bacilli at 24 hours (Figure 3) to gram-positive cocci at 48 hr (Figure 4, below). This appearance suggested the organism was Rhodococcus.
The organism was then identified by rapid API Coryne V3.0 (bioMerieux) as Rhodococcus spp at 94.1% probability. The Christie, Atkins, Munch-Peterson (CAMP) test, a phenotypic test to detect extracellular hemolytic protein or CAMP factor, yielded a positive result distinguishing Rhodococcus equi from other species. Additionally, the matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Vitek MS V2.0) identified the organism as Rhodococcus equi at 99.9% confidence value.
Figure 4. Gram stain of the colony at 48 hours.
Treatment and Followup
A splint was applied to his leg and outpatient follow up with orthopedic surgery was scheduled. A transesophogeal echocardiogram revealed no vegetations. Antibiotics were administered and fevers resolved. Ciprofloxacin, orally, was prescribed for 2 weeks, and follow-up with his primary care provider scheduled.
Discussion
Rhodococcus is a member of aerobic Actinomycetes, a large and diverse group of gram-positive bacilli, similar to clinically encountered genera Nocardia, Gordonia and Tsukamurella. These genera are aerobic, gram-positive, catalase-positive, partially acid-fast, branching, filamentous bacteria that can fragment into rods and cocci. Rhodococcus can appear coccoid or bacillary depending upon culture age, growth condition, and type of media (Figures 3 and 4). Colonies on solid media appear large, salmon-pink to red-colored, slimy or mucoid (Figure 2); however, these characteristics usually occur at days 4 to 7 of incubation [McNeil and Brown, 1994; Tille 2013].
Rhodococcus equi is the most important species of Rhodococcus species causing disease in humans. Greater than 80% of reported cases occurred in immunocompromised hosts particularly those with defects in cell-mediated immunity, e.g., patients with AIDS, chemotherapy recipients, those with leukemia or lymphoma, or stem cell or solid organ transplant recipients. R. equi has been found in soil, and is highly prevalent in some horse farms. Patients infected with R. equi frequently, but not always, have history of contact with farm animals, soil or both. Pneumonia accounts for about 80% of human cases of infection reported in the literature. Radiographically, pulmonary rhodococcal infection can present with nodules, infiltrates, cavitary lesions, effusion or abscesses which mimic tuberculosis, nocardiosis, fungal infections, or malignancy [Yamshchikov et al. 2010].
Extrapulmonary infection occurs in about 20% of cases, and may involve the central nervous system (CNS), subcutaneous tissue and internal organs causing abscesses. Bacteremia, catheter-associated infection, peritonitis, and osteomyelitis have also been described [Meyer and Reboli, 2009].
Owing to the small number of reported cases, there are no definitive guidelines for treatment. Similarly, there are no standardized methods for antimicrobial susceptibility testing. In vitro, Rhodococcus is generally susceptible to aminoglycosides, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, linezolid, macrolides, rifampin and vancomycin, whereas it is often resistant to penicillin, ampicillin, cephalosporins and clindamycin. Treatment of localized, non-CNS diseases in a normal host may be achieved with oral agents. In contrast, immunocompromised hosts, and those with serious infections, require treatment with 2-drug, and sometimes 3-drug, regimens that include intravenous antibiotics. It has been suggested that intravenous therapy be continued until clinical improvement occurs or for a minimum of 2 to 3 weeks, followed by oral agents until cultures are negative and signs and symptoms have resolved [Meyer and Reboli, 2009].
Of the other organisms in the differential, Staphylococcus aureus and Rothia mucilaginosa are gram-positive cocci that generally appear in clusters or tetrads, and would be unlikely to appear in the primary gram stain as a bacillary organism [Que and Moreillon 2010; Vaccher et al. 2007]. Rapidly growing Mycobacterium species are typically beaded gram-positive bacilli in clusters [El Helou et al. 2013]. Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae is a pleomorphic gram-positive rod that may appear as gram-negative rods at times because of rapid decolorization [Kichloo et al. 2013].
Final Diagnosis
Bacteremia caused by Rhodococcus equi in a patient with injection drug use.
