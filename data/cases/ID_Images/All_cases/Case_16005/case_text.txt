History of Present Illness
2016 November-December Featured Case and 2016 IDWeek Fellows' Day Case Presentation: A woman in her thirties developed left hand swelling and fevers. On the fourth day of symptoms, she presented to an emergency department for evaluation.
The patient had previously been well until approximately 1 week earlier when she pricked an unspecified finger on a ‘goathead’ plant (Tribulus terrestris) while weeding in her garden. Two days later she noticed pain and swelling in the 4th finger of her left hand, which spread to involve the left hand over the next 4 days. On the fourth day, a fever occurred, to a maximum temperature of 38.6ºC (101.5ºF), and she sought medical attention.
Social History
She was married and monogamous.
Epidemiological History
The patient was born and raised in the Southwestern United States, and was of Native American ancestry. She lived in a rural area, had a large garden and owned pet dogs. She reported no other animal exposures. She did not smoke tobacco, drink alcohol, or use illicit drugs.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared mildly ill but not toxic. The temperature was 38.4ºC (101.1ºF), blood pressure 124/78 mmHg, pulse 118 beats per minute, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 94% while breathing room air. The left hand was erythematous and swollen, with an ulcer on the tip of the fourth finger (Figure 1) and no streaking. There were 1-2 centimeter lymph nodes, which were firm and mildly tender, in the left supratrochlear and axillary regions. The remainder of the physical examination was normal.
Figure 1. Physical finding of left hand.
Studies
The white cell count was 8000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4000-10,000), with 80% neutrophils. The hemoglobin was 17 g/dL (reference range 12-16 g/dL in women) and the level of platelets was 123,000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 150,000-500,000). The serum level of glucose was 285 mg/dL (reference range 80-100), bilirubin 2 mg/dL (reference range 0.3-1.1), albumin 2.0 g/dL (reference range 3.1-4.3) and aspartate aminotranferase 157 U/L (reference range 10-40); the level of alanine transaminase was normal.
Blood cultures were obtained.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Cefazolin (1 gram every 8 hrs) was administered intravenously. The erythema and swelling improved slightly but fevers persisted. Intravenous cefazolin was stopped, and oral cephalexin 500 mg by mouth every 8 hrs given. Despite these measures, she had persistent fevers.
Two days after admission, blood culture bottles were noted to have positive growth. Gram stain of a blood culture is shown (Figure 2).
Figure 2. Gram stain from blood culture.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Gram stains from both blood culture bottles were initially reported to be negative. Review of a gram stain showed tiny, faintly staining gram-negative coccobacilli (Figure 2). A sample from the positive blood cultures was plated and, after 48 hours, grew pin-point colonies on blood and chocolate agar, with no growth on MacConkey agar. The specimen was sent to the state laboratory where it was identified as Francisella tularensis, first by direct fluorescent antigen (DFA) testing and then confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).
Treatment and Followup
Oral ciprofloxacin (500 mg twice daily) was begun. Within 24 hours, she was afebrile and within 72 hours the cellulitis of her hand resolved completely. She was discharged on oral ciprofloxacin (500 mg twice daily), with instructions to complete a 10-day course. At one month follow-up, her symptoms had completely resolved and she had no further complications from the infection.
Discussion
Francisella tularensis is a gram negative coccobacilli intracellular pathogen, known for its association with rabbits and as a potential agent of bioterrorism [Carvalho et al., 2014]. The disease is spread through rodents by ticks or deer flies, and to humans when they come in contact with either infected rodents or infected ticks [Carvalho et al., 2014]. Tularemia has six clinical presentations: ulceroglandular, glandular, oculoglandular, oropharyngeal, typhoidal and pneumonic tularemia [Hepburn and Simpson, 2008]. All presentations typically involve fever and regional lymphadenopathy at the point of entry [CDC website, Tularemia, Signs and Symptoms; https://www.cdc.gov/tularemia/signssymptoms/index.html, Accessed:5.7.2016)].
Ulceroglandular disease is the most common clinical presentation of tularemia. This usually occurs after a direct bite by a tick or deer fly, or after handling an infected animal [CDC website, Tularemia, Signs and Symptoms; https://www.cdc.gov/tularemia/signssymptoms/index.html, Accessed: 5.7.2016)]. An ulcer appears at the site of bacterial entry and there is swelling of the regional lymph nodes; isolation of the bacteria from the blood stream is rare [CDC website Tularemia, Clinicians;, https://www.cdc.gov/tularemia/clinicians/index.html Accessed: 5.7.2016)]. Upon repeat questioning about animal exposures, the patient noted that her husband had completed a hunting trip 2 days prior to her injury while gardening, after which she skinned about 50 rabbits. This was thought to be the likely the source of her infection.
Final Diagnosis
Ulceroglandular tularemia complicated by bacteremia and sepsis, caused by Francisella tularensis.
