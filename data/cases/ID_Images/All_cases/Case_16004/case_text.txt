History of Present Illness
2016 IDWeek Fellows' Day Case Presentation: A man in his sixties developed blurred vision in his left eye. On the third day of his symptoms, he presented for evaluation.
He reported having had a flu-like illness 2 months earlier, which was followed one week later with the onset of intermittent fevers, chills, night sweats, fatigue and almost daily mild headaches. He had also noted early satiety, decreased appetite, and a 20-pound weight loss. One week before this presentation, his hands and feet became swollen, and he developed occasional tingling and burning sensations. He had no joint pain, skin rashes or painful skin nodules.
Past Medical History
He was in good health before the flu-like illness two months earlier.
Medications
He took acetaminophen and naproxen as needed for headache and fevers.
Social History
He lived with his wife and children in Southern California and had worked as a landscaper until this illness, when he became unable to work due to his symptoms.
Epidemiological History
He was born on an island in the South Pacific and immigrated to the United States many years earlier. He had no recent international or national travel, no sick contacts and no known exposure to tuberculosis. He owned several dogs, with no other recent exposure to animals. He had smoked marijuana daily but stopped one month before this presentation. He had no history of tobacco, alcohol or intravenous drug use.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. The blood pressure was 143/67 mm Hg, pulse 82 beats per minute, temperature 101.7°F (38.7°C), respirations 18 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation of 98% while breathing room air. His hands and feet were edematous and there were splinter hemorrhages on all finger nails. The remainder of the general examination was normal. An ophthalmologic examination performed in the emergency department revealed branch retinal artery occlusion of left eye.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 13,000 cells per microliter (reference range (ref) 4000-10,500), hemoglobin 11g/dL (ref 13.5 -16.9), hematocrit 39.7% (ref 39.5 - 50) and platelets 279,000 per microliter (ref 150,000 - 400,000). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 103 mm/hr (ref 0 - 15) and C-reactive protein 0.4 mg/dl (ref 0 - 1.0). The level of albumin was 3.4 g/dl (ref 4.2 - 5.5), total protein 9 g/dl (ref 6 - 8.3) and other tests of liver function were normal. An HIV test was negative. Urinalysis revealed microscopic hematuria, with 23 red cells per high-power field (ref 0-3). Other routine laboratory test results were normal. A chest radiograph was normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Three sets of blood cultures were obtained, followed by the administration of intravenous vancomycin, and the patient was admitted to the hospital.
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), performed on the day of admission, demonstrated trace aortic regurgitation, and was otherwise normal. Vancomycin was subsequently stopped, as the patient appeared clinically well and hemodynamically stable. Multiple additional sets of blood cultures were obtained, and prolonged incubation periods requested to evaluate for possible culture negative endocarditis.
Computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen with the administration of intravenous contrast showed mild diffuse bladder-wall thickening and multiple subcentimeter mesenteric lymph nodes that were thought to represent a reactive process. An extensive rheumatologic evaluation revealed a mildly elevated level of rheumatoid factor at 20 IU/ml (ref <14).
Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated a possible very thin (2 mm) nodular thickening on the aortic valve.
The patient was discharged from the hospital with symptomatic improvement along with multiple negative blood cultures, with a plan to repeat the echocardiogram in 1 or 2 weeks. Results of some other serologic studies to look for culture-negative causes of endocarditis were pending at discharge.
One week after discharge, the patient did not attend a scheduled outpatient follow-up appointment. Two weeks later, he returned to hospital because of a 3-to-4 day history of bilateral hand pain, swelling and bluish discoloration (Figures 1 and 2). He appeared otherwise well, with normal vital signs. Physical examination revealed bilateral swollen, cool, dusky fingers and hands. The remainder of the examination was normal.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Results of serological studies from the previous admission returned and were positive fo r Bartonella henselae IgG antibodies at greater than 1:1024 dilution and IgM antiboidies at 1:256 dilution. Coxiella burnetii IgG titers were also positive (phase I at 1:32 dilution and phase II at 1:128 dilution), which were thought to be false positive due to cross-reaction with Bartonella species antigens. Repeat TEE revealed possibly smaller or unchanged non-mobile small thickening or vegetation on the aortic valve.
Treatment and Followup
A diagnosis of Bartonella endocarditis was made and treatment with gentamicin and doxycycline begun; additional treatment included sildenafil, nifedipine and anticoagulation therapy with intravenous heparin for suspected vasospasm or thromboemboli.
Arterial doppler non-invasive studies were negative. CT angiography of the arms demonstrated patent arteries in the subclavian to digital arch regions. There was persistent cyanosis of all fingers that progressed to dry gangrene. His hospital course was further complicated by sudden onset of an expressive aphasia, with no other focal sensory and motor deficits identified on neurology examination. CT of the head, performed emergently, showed multiple hypo-attenuating foci in the bilateral brain parenchyma concerning for acute cerebrovascular accident (CVA), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain confirmed acute multifocal infarctions (Figure 3). A repeat TEE was unchanged from the previous study.
Pathological examination of a skin biopsy from a finger revealed a vasculopathy with microemboli occluding the small vessels. A 2-week course of gentamicin was administered, and the patient was discharged from hospital to complete a course of oral doxycycline of 3-to-6 months' duration.
The patient was evaluated in outpatient follow-up visits after hospital discharge. His speech improved, and vision in the left eye remained poor, but did not worsen. The digital gangrene increased, and progressed to autoamputation (Figures 4 and 5).

Discussion
Bartonella species are gram-negative fastidious intracellular bacilli, responsible for about 3% of culture negative endocarditis [Raoult et al., 2003]. HACEK organisms have been traditionally thought to be the most common agents of culture-negative endocarditis. However, studies have found that HACEK organisms can be easily isolated with current blood-culture systems when incubated for at least five days [Petti et al., 2006; Baron et al., 2005]. The most common causative agents of culture-negative infective endocarditis are fastidious organisms, zoonotic agents and fungi.
There are nearly 40 different Bartonella species with 6 species related to infective endocarditis in humans: B. quintana, B. henselae, B. elizabethae, B. vinsonii, B. koehlerae, and B. alsatica. More than 95 percent of the cases have involved either B. quintana or B. henselae. Data suggest that homelessness, alcoholism, and infestation with body lice are associated with B. quintana endocarditis whereas contact with cats and previous valvular disease serve as the major risk factors for B. henselae endocarditis [Raoult et al., 1996]. Our patient did not have any of those typical risk factors.
Approximately 60 percent of patients with Bartonella endocarditis have involvement of the aortic valve. Most cases involve native valves and are mostly seen in adult males. Clinical manifestations are similar to other patients with subacute bacterial endocarditis, including a wide variety of rheumatologic symptoms. There have been reports of Bartonella vasculitis in the literature [Balakrishnan et al., 2016; Chaudhry et al., 2015]..
Because of the difficulty identifying the organism in pathological specimens or growing it in culture, the diagnosis of B. henselae infection is more commonly made by serologic testing by indirect immunofluorescence and/or by identification of B. henselae nucleic acid by polymerase chain reaction analysis or immunohistochemical analysis of valvular biopsy specimens [Fournier et al., 2002; Ruimy et al., 1994]. In an international study, all but one patient with Bartonella endocarditis had titers ≥1:1600 using a microimmunofluorescence test with a positive predictive value of 0.88 [Raoult et al., 1996]. Studies have shown an immunogenic protein, dihydrolipoamide succinyltransferase protein (SucB) causing cross-reactivity between Bartonella species and Brucella melitensis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Franscisella tularensis, Coxiella burnetii and Rickettsia typhi resulting in false positive immunologic diagnostic tests [Litwin et al., 2004]. In this case, the seropositivity for Coxiella was thought to be false positive due to cross-reaction with Bartonella antigens. It is unlikely that our patient has Coxiella endocarditis based on the pattern of Coxiella serologies. In Coxiella endocarditis, phase I IgG antibodies should be significantly elevated (> 1:800 dilution) and typically higher than phase II IgG antibodies.
There are insufficient data to make any definitive recommendations for treating Bartonella endocarditis. In a retrospective study that included 101 cases of Bartonella endocarditis, it was shown that effective antibiotic therapy should include an aminoglycoside prescribed for a minimum of 2 weeks. The guidelines recommend treating with a regimen consisting of doxycycline for a minimum of six weeks plus 14 days course of gentamicin [Rolain et al., 2004]. Most expert recommend extending doxycycline therapy orally for three to six months.
This case report describes a very unusual presentation of native valve Bartonella endocarditis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case of Bartonella endocardits associated with retinal artery occlusion, profound bilateral digital gangrene and acute multifocal cerebral infarctions in a previously healthy person without significant associated risk factors.
Final Diagnosis
Endocarditis with septic emboli caused by Bartonella infection.
