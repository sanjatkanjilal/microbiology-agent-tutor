History of Present Illness
2008 ICAAC/IDSA Pediatric Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A child with fever and emesis.
A child, under 10 years of age, was admitted because of fever and emesis. She had been well until 2 weeks before admission, when sore throat, cough, chest discomfort, emesis and abdominal distension had developed, associated with subjective fevers. She was evaluated as an outpatient at three visits to healthcare facilities; pharyngitis was initially diagnosed, followed by pneumonia and finally urinary tract infection. Treatment had included trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. On the day of admission, increasing discomfort in the right side of her abdomen also occurred, and she went to the emergency department.
Past Medical History
She had been well, and her immunizations were up-to-date.
Epidemiological History
She had moved with her family from Mexico to the United States approximately 15 months earlier. While in Mexico, she lived on a small farm, drank bottled water and ate unpasteurized home-made cheeses. Food was cooked with tap water, not well-water, and she did have contact with farm animals. No family members were ill.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared ill. The temperature was 39.7°C (103.5°F). The blood pressure was 80/60 mm Hg, pulse 113 beats per minute, and respirations 40 breaths per minute. Respirations were shallow with mild splinting. She took deep breaths on command but reported discomfort over the right flank; clear breath sounds were auscultated over the anterior and posterior left lung fields, but air entry was decreased in lower 1/3-1/4 right posterior and lateral lung fields. No dullness to percussion was elicited, and no adventitious sounds were heard. There was moderate distension of the abdomen, and tenderness to superficial palpation in right upper and lower quadrants with involuntary guarding; no rebound tenderness was elicited. The liver spanned approximately 14 cm in right mid-clavicular line.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 32,700 cells per cubic millimeter (reference range 4,500-14,500/cmm) with 83.2% granulocytes and a left shift. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) measured 109 mm/hr (ref. range 0-20 mm/hr) and C- reactive protein (CRP) 34.4 mg/dl (ref. range 0.0-0.8 mg/dl). Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) was 153 U/L (ref. range 14-36 U/L). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were within normal. Urinalysis revealed positive leukocyte esterase (250/ul), proteinuria (100 mg/dl), and 14 white cells per high power field but negative nitrite test.
A radiograph of the chest and abdomen (Figure 1) showed under-aerated lungs and prominence of the liver. Ultrasonography of the abdomen (Figure 2) demonstrated a well circumscribed focal liver lesion (7.3 cm by 7.0 cm by 7.3 cm) with heterogeneous echogenicity seen in the right lobe.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Computed tomography (CT) scan (shown in Figures 3 through 5) revealed lesions that appeared cystic, and which were thought to be consistent with a large liver abscess and satellite lesions.
Cefotaxime (intravenously) was begun empirically on admission for treatment of possible pyelonephritis, pending the results of laboratory tests, and Pediatric Infectious Disease consultation was obtained following the imaging studies.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Antibody studies for Entamoeba histolytica were highly positive; immunoglobulin (Ig) G measured 16.3 (where greater than 1.2 is suggestive of a current or past infection). Culture of the liver aspirate was sterile, with no growth of aerobic or anaerobic organisms. Stains for ova and parasites, performed on abscess fluid specimens, were negative; numerous leukocytes and numerous red blood cells were seen. An antigen test was not performed. Stool studies were negative for ova or parasites. The clinical picture was thought to be most consistent with an amebic liver abscess.
Treatment and Followup
She received empiric cefotaxime, vancomycin and metronidazole pending the results of the serologic studies. Percutaneous abscess drainage was performed and reddish-brown (‘anchovy paste’) fluid was aspirated, and a hepatic drain placed. Amebic liver abscess was diagnosed based on the serologic test results, and supported by the epidemiologic and clinical findings. Vancomycin and cefotaxime were discontinued, and oral metronidazole (10-day course), followed by paromomycin (1-week course) were given. She was followed in the outpatient clinic after discharge, and made a complete clinical recovery.
Discussion
Entamoeba histolytica is the parasitic organism responsible for invasive amebiasis. Amebiasis occurs in 10% of the world’s population [Chung and Friedman 2006], and is found mostly in tropical or sub-tropical regions. Amebic liver abscess is the most common manifestation of extra-intestinal amebiasis, and is a rare disease in children [Haque et al. 2003, Hotez and Strickland 2004]. Among adults, amebic liver abscesses are most common in men (male:female ratio may be as high as 9:1); among children, amebic abscesses affect males and females equivalently.
Presentation of amebic liver abscess may be indolent, sub-acute or acute. There may be a latency period of years between intestinal and liver infection [Chung and Friedman 2006]. It is thought that this patient most likely acquired the disease in Mexico, and had a latency period of more than one year before development of liver abscess.
Symptoms and signs include fever, right upper quadrant pain, malaise and myalgias, cough and right sided chest pain, and jaundice. [Chung and Friedman 2006, Hotez and Strickland 2004] The toxic appearance of this patient was atypical for a localized amebic liver process, and may reflect thoracic or parapneumonic extension. A moderate sized pleural effusion was evident on the right side of the chest, although no radiographic evidence of rupture of the abscess on ultrasound or CT scan was seen. The pleural fluid was not sampled. The septated nature of this patient's abscess is also atypical since amebae actively lyse tissues without respecting borders.
In this case, the diagnosis was based on a combination of a high index of clinical suspicion, targeted imaging and serology. Serologic testing has a sensitivity of 95% [Chung and Friedman 2006], but must be interpreted in the context of the presentation. Serologic testing modalities include enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), indirect immunofluorescent test, indirect hemagglutination, countercurrent immunoelectrophoresis, latex fixation, complement fixation, and gel diffusion [Chung and Friedman 2006, Hotez and Strickland 2004]. Aspiration of the abscess is recommended for those patients for which the diagnosis is uncertain (microscopic yield is low) or for impending rupture into pericardium, pleural or peritoneal space [Chung and Friedman 2006]. In patients with amebic liver abscess, E. histolytica antigen is found in almost 100% of serum samples and 40% of stool samples [Johannsen and Madoff 2005]. In cases of amebic invasive liver disease, the organism is isolated from the stool in only 50% of cases [Chung and Friedman 2006]. An antigen assay would have established a definitive diagnosis, but was not performed in this case.
At the time of this publication, therapy for liver amebiasis includes metronidazole (intravenously or orally, for 5-10 days) [Chung and Friedman 2006, Haque et al. 2003, Hotez and Strickland 2004, Stanley 2003], or alternatively, tinidazole [Haque et al. 2003]. Chloroquine may also be used adjunctively with metronidazole [Hughes and Petri 2000], but its use is rarely indicated. Adjunctive therapeutic modalities include luminal therapy to clear intestinal colonization, iodoquinol (three times daily) for 20 days [Stanley 2003], diloxanide furoate (three times daily) for 10 days [Haque et al. 2003] or paromomycin (three times daily) for 7 days [Haque et al. 2003].
Final Diagnosis
Amebic liver abscess, consistent with Entamoeba histolytica infection.
