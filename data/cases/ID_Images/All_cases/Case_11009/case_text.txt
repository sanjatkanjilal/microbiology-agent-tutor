History of Present Illness
2011 November-December Featured Case and 2011 IDSA Fellows’ Day Case Presentation Summary: A man with fevers, vomiting, and cyanosis of the feet.
A man in his fifties was well until one week before admission when nausea, vomiting, malaise, and headaches developed after eating a hamburger. The symptoms were associated with polydipsia, polyuria, and decreased oral intake. Approximately three days before admission, fevers and chills occurred, and his feet became mottled. His weight reportedly decreased by 10 pounds (4.5 kilograms).
On the day of admission, a friend noted changes in his mental status and slurred speech.
Past Medical History
He had a history of hypertension, colonic diverticulosis, osteoarthritis and a remote history of alcohol dependence. He had no history of peripheral vascular disease or claudication.
Social History
He lived with his girlfriend and was retired.
Epidemiological History
He lived in California and owned cats. He was monogamous. He did not smoke or use illicit drugs, had not traveled recently and had had no known sick contacts. His yard was visited by opossums, coyotes and deer.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared drowsy, with slightly slurred speech. The blood pressure was 132/61 mm Hg, pulse 146 beats per minute irregular, temperature 98.6°F (37.0°C), and respirations 35 per minute. Breath sounds were coarse. The pulse was rapid and irregularly irregular, without cardiac murmurs. The scrotum was erythematous and mildly edematous. There was violaceous to dusky-gray, non-blanching, non-palpable purpura involving all the toes, with overlying hemorrhagic bullae, and necrosis just proximal to the tips of toes. Distal pedal pulses were 1 to 2+, and there was 1+ pitting edema, one hemorrhagic bulla on the left shin, and reticulate erythema of the palms. He was able to follow simple commands.
Studies
The hematocrit was 56% (reference range 39-54), white count 17,400 cells per cubic millimeter (ref 4,100 -10,800) with 90% neutrophils (ref 35-80), and the platelet count 16,000 per cubic millimeter (ref 150,000-450,000). The peripheral smear showed 1-2 schistocytes or fragmented red cells per high power field. The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was 44.5 seconds (ref 25.2- 36.8) and fibrinogen 86 mg/dl (ref 269-589) and INR 1.4 (ref 0.9-1.2). The serum level of sodium was 125 mEq/L(ref 133-146), potassium 3.8 mEq/L (ref 3.6-5.0), urea nitrogen 65 mg/dL (ref 7-21), creatinine 2.2 mg/dL (ref 0.5-1.4), alanine aminotransferase 40 U/L (ref 7-56), aspartate aminotransferase 112 U/L (ref 38-126), alkaline phosphatase 128 U/L (ref 44-147), total bilirubin 1.2 mg/dL (ref 0.2-1.3), lactate dehydrogenase 403 U/L (ref 50-150), D- Dimer >6000 ng/ml (ref < or = 250 ng/mL), and creatine kinase 1324 ng/ml (ref 38-120).
Serologic testing for antibodies to human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and tests for hepatitis B and C viruses were negative. Urinalysis screening revealed a large quantity of blood but was negative for leukocyte esterase and ketones; microscopic examination showed 15 red cells per high power field and hyaline casts were present. Results of stool analysis, including testing for Escherichia coli 0:157, were negative.
A chest radiograph was normal. Computed tomography (CT), without contrast enhancement, of the chest, abdomen and pelvis, showed bilateral paraseptal emphysematous changes and dependent bilateral groundglass opacities in all lung fields. CT of the head was normal. Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60% and no evidence of vegetations, and there was no evidence of thrombosis by non-invasive testing (Doppler) of the legs.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Ceftriaxone, azithromycin, intravenous fluids, and high dose corticosteroids were administered and cryoprecipitate was infused for possible pulmonary infection, acute kidney injury cryoglobulinemia and vasculitis. After one dose, azithromycin was stopped and acyclovir and vancomycin were added for possible meningitis. Lumbar puncture was not performed because of thrombocytopenia.
During the next two days, dyspnea increased, scrotal edema and pain worsened and the mottled appearance of the feet evolved into frank necrosis. Physical findings are shown in Figures 1 and 2. CT of the chest demonstrated bilateral opacities.
A punch skin biopsy of the proximal first toe was performed; pathological examination of the biopsy with hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stain is shown in Figures 3 and 4.
Ultrasonography demonstrated evidence of epididymo-orchitis on the right.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Pathological examination of the biopsy, shown in Figure 3 at 20 x magnification, revealed epidermis with full thickness necrosis and focal detachment. The papillary dermis was edematous, with separation of collagen bundles. Figure 4, shown at 600 x magnification, demonstrates the reticular dermis with small blood vessels involved by a leukocytoclastic vasculitis surrounding eccrine ducts. Trans mural fibrinoid necrosis (solid arrow), characteristic of a leukocytoclastic vasculitis, is seen, with nuclear dust (open arrow) from degenerating neutrophils. There were also swollen vascular endothelial cells (solid arrow with notched tail) and intraluminal fibrin thrombi (open arrow with notched tail), as well as white cells in the vessel wall, and reactive endothelial cells.
Testing for antibodies to Rickettsia typhi was positive: IgG Rickettsia typhi >1:1024 (reference range <1:64 = Negative, 1:64-1:28 = Equivocal, >1: 256 = Positive) and IgM Rickettsia typhi >1:1024 (ref <1:64 = Negative and >1:64 = Positive).
Treatment and Followup
Doxycycline was administered empirically, before the results of the diagnostic tests were available, with improvement in mentation, fevers, and shortness of breath. The platelet count improved and acute renal failure resolved. Necrotic toes required amputation.
Discussion
Rickettsia typhi is the etiologic agent of murine typhus, which is often referred to as “endemic typhus” or “fleaborne typhus.” R. typhi is a small (0.4×1.3 µm), gram-negative, obligate intracellular bacterium that depends on hematophagous arthropods (e.g., fleas and ticks) and mammals to maintain its life cycle [Civen and Ngo 2008]. In the United States, many of the cases of murine typhus are reported in Texas, California and Hawaii [Civen and Ngo 2008], and there has been a recent documented outbreak in a community of coastal California [Green et al. 2011]. Given these geographic considerations, a diagnosis of murine typhus merited consideration in this patient.
Murine typhus is commonly a mild illness associated with a rash with an incubation period of 8 to 16 days. However, as in this case, it can cause an illness with significant morbidity, and deaths can occur if appropriate treatment is not administered. The primary pathological lesion of R. typhi infection is an inflammatory vasculitis characterized by perivascular infiltration of lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma, and mast cells. In rare cases, the vasculitis may be accompanied by mural or intimal thrombi in small vessels of the heart, lungs, kidneys, and central nervous system or, as in our case, small vessels of the feet. The pathophysiology of the rickettsial diseases could well explain the formation of clots within blood vessels. Rickettsiae are known to invade the endothelium of blood vessels - arterial, capillary, as well as venous. The damage to the integrity of the endothlial lining may thus begin the clotting cascade and cases of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolus have been reported in the literature as well [Potasman and Bassan 1986]. The clinical and lab findings in murine typhus are frequently non-specific; because patients respond well to treatment, prompt consideration of the diagnosis and empiric therapy are essential [Potasman and Bassan 1986]. The drug of choice in adults is doxycycline. Doxycycline should be continued for 48 hours after the patient becomes afebrile or for a minimum of five days
Final Diagnosis
Murine typhus caused by Rickettsia typhi.
