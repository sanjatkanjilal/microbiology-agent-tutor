History of Present Illness
Summary: A middle-aged woman with a fever and rash.
A middle-aged woman noted a “bite” on her left thigh seven days before her presentation. The lesion enlarged over the next several days. Four days before her admission, she had the acute onset of fatigue, arthralgias, myalgias, a severe headache and a fever to 103.4°F (39.7°C). On the day of her presentation, she developed a rash on her extremities, trunk and back.
Epidemiological History
She lived in a city in the Northeast, and had been in Georgia six months before. She owned a dog, and had noted one mouse in her basement.
Physical Examination
Her temperature was 100.5°F (38.1°C), pulse 104 beats per minute, blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg, respirations 16 breaths per minute. She was in no apparent distress, and there was no lymphadenopathy. There was a blanching papular erythematous rash on her extremities, back, and chest (Figures 1 and 2). A lesion was present on her left thigh (Figures 3 and 4).

Studies
The white blood cell (WBC) count was 3,200 cells/mm³, hematocrit 34.6% and platelet count 180,000/mm³. The level of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 64 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 57 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 95 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 454 U/L; the bilirubin level and chest radiograph were normal. Blood cultures were sterile.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Serologic testing at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) showed a titer of 1:256 against Rickettsia rickettsii and other members of the spotted fever group of rickettsiae, including Rickettsia akari.
Treatment and Followup
The patient was treated with doxycycline, with rapid improvement in her fever and rash.
Discussion
Rickettsialpox is caused by Rickettsia akari, a member of the spotted fever group of rickettsiae. The organism is transmitted to humans by a mouse mite. After the bite of an infected mite, Rickettsia akari proliferates locally resulting in a papule, which ulcerates to form an eschar. Three to seven days later, patients develop high fever, chills and headache. Two to three days after the onset of fever, many patients develop a generalized papulovesicular rash.
Diagnosis of rickettsialpox is generally by serologic testing. At the time of this publication, treatment usually includes a tetracycline for 3-5 days, and clinical response is typically rapid.
One of the main possibilities in the differential diagnosis in a patient with suspected rickettsialpox is chickenpox, which, of course, is much more common. The following features may help distinguish rickettsialpox from chickenpox: in rickettsialpox, an eschar is typically present, whereas this does not occur in patients with chickenpox; the lesions of chickenpox often occur in crops, whereas this does not generally happen with rickettsialpox; patients with rickettsialpox usually have relatively few skin lesions (20-40), whereas individuals with chickenpox may have many more and, lastly, the skin lesion in rickettsialpox is usually a papulovesicle, whereas vesicles characterize the rash of chickenpox.
Final Diagnosis
Rickettsialpox.
