History of Present Illness
Summary: An African pre-teen girl with abdominal swelling.
An East African pre-teen girl noted progressive swelling in her abdomen. She had also had early satiety and round worms in her stools. She denied any fevers or night sweats, clumsiness, vision changes, headaches, dysphagia, changes in her urine, or other complaints.
Past Medical History
She had a history of malaria.
Epidemiological History
She lived with her parents and four siblings on a small farm in East Africa with cows, goats, and a dog. She attended secondary school in the morning and tended the tea fields in the afternoon.
An uncle had tuberculosis and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).
Physical Examination
She was a thin well-appearing adolescent female. The temperature was 36.6°C (97.9°F), pulse 76 beats per minute, respiratory rate 14 per minute, and blood pressure 90/60 mm Hg. The oropharynx was clear and there was no nystagmus. Examination of the neck and chest was normal. Cardiovascular examination revealed a regular rate and rhythm, and a 2/6 systolic ejection murmur at the left upper sternal border. There was a 10-cm, non-tender, non-mobile abdominal mass palpable in the midline, superior to the umbilicus. Neurological exam had intact cranial nerves (2-12 tested), with no dysmetria or asterixis, and normal deep tendon reflexes (DTRs).
Studies
The white blood cell count (WBC) was 8,300/mm³, hematocrit 32%, and platelet count 240,000/mm³. Serum levels of electrolytes and tests of liver functions were normal. Abdominal ultrasound showed an indistinct, heterogeneous mass anterior to the stomach. It was not clear if the bowel was involved. The liver and kidneys appeared normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
An exploratory laparotomy was performed, and a large mass was found in the greater omentum (Figure 1). It was separate from the stomach, small bowel and large bowel.
Figure 1. Findings on surgery.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Gross pathologic examination revealed a thick rind surrounding the mass. When the mass was incised, multiple small gelatinous masses were found (Figure 1), which were diagnostic of an echinococcal cyst, caused by tapeworm species of Echinococcus.
Treatment and Followup
Discussion
The pathologic finding of a primary cyst with multiple daughter cysts within is classic for echinococcal disease. It is important to remove cysts completely, as the daughter cysts are potentially infectious if 'spilled' in vivo and the cyst contents can cause an anaphylactic reaction.
Final Diagnosis
Echinococcal cyst, caused by tapeworm species of Echinococcus.
