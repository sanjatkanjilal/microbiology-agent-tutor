History of Present Illness
2008 ICAAC/IDSA Pediatric Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A teenaged boy with fever and a petechial rash.
The patient had been well until the day before admission, when fever and petechial rash developed. He was brought to the hospital for evaluation and admitted.
Past Medical History
The patient was otherwise healthy. His immunizations were complete for his age.
Allergies
He had no known drug allergies.
Social History
The patient lived with his parents and four siblings.
Epidemiological History
There were no pets at home, and no history of recent travel.
Ten days earlier, a younger teenaged brother had developed a febrile illness and sore throat. Amoxicillin therapy was begun; three days later, a diffuse rash erupted and he was admitted to the hospital. An extensive evaluation for infectious etiologies was negative. Ceftriaxone was administered for 7 days, and he recovered completely. A second brother developed fever and a petechial rash on the same day as the patient. The other members of the family were well.
Physical Examination
On examination, he appeared ill, but was awake and oriented. The temperature was 39.2°C (102.6°F), blood pressure of 88/35 mm Hg, pulse 87 beats per minute, and respirations 16 breaths per minute. The pharynx was erythematous, with ulcers and a few petechiae on the soft palate, which appeared similar to those seen on his brother's examination. A maculopapular petechial rash was distributed predominately on the distal extremities, and his hands and feet were erythematous and swollen. (See Figures 1 and 2.) There were no meningeal signs, conjunctival injection, or swollen joints. The remainder of the examination was normal.

Studies
The white blood cell count was 1,800/mm³ (reference range: 4,100-10,100/mm³) with 40% neutrophils, 40% lymphocytes, 10% eosinophils, and 10% monocytes. The hemoglobin was 12 g/dl, and the platelet count was 108,000/mm³.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for parvovirus B19 nucleic acid was positive for both the patient and the brother admitted at the same time as he, and confirmed viremia with parvovirus. On the second day of admission, parvovirus IgG antibodies and parvovirus IgM antibodies remained undetected in this patient, whereas the brother admitted with him had parvovirus serum IgG antibodies at a level of 5.33 units (reference range 0 to 0.89) and positive parvovirus IgM antibodies.
The other brother, who had first become ill, was discharged and parvovirus testing was not performed.
Treatment and Followup
Ceftriaxone was administered for 5 days until PCR results became available, and was then stopped. The patient did not return for convalescent serologic testing.
Discussion
Parvovirus B19 is the etiologic agent of the common childhood exanthem erythema infectiosum. It is a small, non-enveloped, single stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (ssDNA) virus. Humans are the only known host for parvovirus B19. [Kaufmann et al. 2004] Viremia occurs seven to 10 days after exposure and usually lasts approximately five days, with virus titers peaking on the first few days of infection. [Young and Brown 2004] Approximately 25 percent of infected individuals will be completely asymptomatic during their infection, while 50 percent will have only non-specific flu-like symptoms. The remaining 25 percent of infected individuals will present with the classic symptoms of B19 infection including rash and/or arthralgia. [Chorba et al. 1986, Hayakawa et al. 2002, Woolf et al. 1989]
Parvovirus B19 infection is responsible for a wide range of clinical manifestations depending on the underlying host. Erythema infectiosum is commonly seen among children infected with parvovirus B19 infection. In sickle cell disease, parvovirus B 19 infection could result in transient aplastic crisis. [Serjeant et al. 2001] Furthermore, parvovirus B19 infection during pregnancy can result in fetal complications including miscarriage, intrauterine fetal death and/or non-immune hydrops fetalis. Some other rare disease associations are arthritis, vasculitis, myocarditis, nephritis, lymphadenitis, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, and hemophagocytic syndrome, Papular-purpuric “gloves & socks” syndrome, encephalopathy, and fulminant liver disease. [Bakhshi et al. 2002, Brown 2007, Harms et al. 1990]
Final Diagnosis
Parvovirus B19 infection, mimicking familial outbreak of meningococcemia.
