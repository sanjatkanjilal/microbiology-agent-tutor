History of Present Illness
2011 IDSA Fellows’ Day Case Presentation Summary: A woman with sarcoidosis and new cough and dyspnea.
A woman in her forties with pulmonary sarcoidosis developed a new cough and dyspnea. Amoxicillin and a short tapering course of corticosteroids were administered, with symptomatic improvement.
One month later, slurred speech, lightheadedness, loss of balance, and a posterior headache developed. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figures 1 and 2) revealed hyperintense white matter lesions in the cerebellum (Fig. 1 see arrows) and brain stem on fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences.
A diagnosis of possible neurosarcoidosis was made, and prednisone was administered (60 mg daily, decreasing to 40 mg daily). Ataxia and dysarthria increased, and she was referred to another hospital. Neurology and Infectious Diseases consultations were obtained.

Past Medical History
Pulmonary sarcoidosis was diagnosed 6 years earlier by mediastinoscopy. The most recent symptomatic flare had occurred 3 years before admission, when corticosteroids were administered. Three months before the onset of the current symptoms, shingles involving the right chest and flank had occurred.
She had also been exposed to tuberculosis in the past; subsequent tuberculin skin testing was negative.
Medications
Medications included prednisone (40mg orally, daily).
Epidemiological History
She worked in a medical facility. The patient had lived in the Midwest United States before moving to the Southeast more than 5 years before admission. Three months before admission, she received a tick bite near her home.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well. The temperature was 37.1°C (98.8°F), blood pressure 191/107 mm Hg, pulse 65 beats per minute, and respirations 18 per minute. There were scattered crackles in the lung fields. Her speech was dysarthric. Horizontal nystagmus was present bilaterally. Muscle strength and deep tendon reflexes were normal, and Babinski reflexes were plantar. Finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin examination revealed dysmetria bilaterally, which was worse on the left compared to the right. Gait was ataxic, requiring assistance.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 15,000 cells/µL (reference range (ref.) 3,500-10,500) with 87% segmented neutrophils (ref. 44.4-70.9). Results of testing for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody testing, rapid plasma reagin, immunoglobulin subclasses, Histoplasma and Lyme serological tests, anti-neutrophil antibody, rheumatoid factor and blood interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis were negative. Other results, including the erythrocyte sedimentation rate, were normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Lumbar puncture examination for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed no white blood cells/µL (ref. 0-5), 2 red blood cells/µL (ref. 0), glucose 59 mg/dL (45-80mg/dL) and protein 20 mg/dL (ref. 15-45).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Polymerase chain reaction amplification (PCR) of the CSF for herpes simplex virus (HSV), Epstein Barr virus (EBV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), cytomegalovirus (CMV) and Tropheryma whipplei were negative. Testing of the CSF for cryptococcus antigen, arbovirus and West Nile virus antibody panels and Histoplasma antibodies was negative. Aerobic, anaerobic, fungal, acid fast bacilli and viral cultures of the CSF were all negative.
JC virus was identified in the CSF by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing. Subsequent quantitative testing revealed a JC virus nucleic acid level of 25,205 copies per mL (ref: <500).
Treatment and Followup
Mefloquine (500mg, orally biweekly for 1 week, followed by 250mg orally, weekly) and mirtazipine (30mg orally, daily) were administered, based on prior studies and case reports of PML in patients with sarcoidosis [Gofton et al. 2010, Owczarczyk et al. 2007]. Prednisone was tapered and discontinued. However, at follow-up 1 month later, her clinical course had worsened. Repeat MRI of the brain revealed progression of the lesions (Figures 3 and 4). A repeat JC viral level of the CSF was 31,102 copies per mL.
A stereotactic brain biopsy was performed, and showed the presence of demyelination, and oligodendritic viral inclusions in the cerebellar white matter, confirming the diagnosis of JC virus-associated PML (see Figures 5 through 7. Pathological images courtesy of Dennis Dickson, M.D.)
Cytarabine (2mg/kg daily) intravenous infusion was administered for 5 consecutive days, with stabilization but without improvement in ataxia, dysarthria or dysphagia.

Discussion
JC virus is a small, enveloped polyomavirus named after the first patient in which it was isolated. The virus is ubiquitous in nature, causing an asymptomatic primary infection; seropositivity occurs in approximately 86% of adults. It lays dormant in the kidneys, tonsils, bone marrow lymphocytes and other lymphoid tissue, reactivating during immunosuppressed states when it enters the peripheral blood with tropism for the brain [Berger 2007].
JC virus-associated progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) was initially described in 1958 in patients with hematologic cancers [Berger 2007]. In the 1980s, prevalence increased with the advent of HIV and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). The virus infects oligodendrocytes and astrocytes, causing white matter disease by destruction of the myelin sheath. Spinal cord involvement is rare, but has been described [Tan et al. 2010].
PML is a neurologic disease of the immunocompromised host, specifically in relation to defects in cell-mediated immunity. More recently, it has been recognized in patients receiving monoclonal antibodies, such as natalizumab for multiple sclerosis and rituximab for lymphoma, rheumatoid arthritis or lupus. At the time of this report, 26 cases of PML in patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis have been described, in whom five were not on any immunosuppression for active disease [Goften et al. 2010, Volker et al. 2007, Goldbecker et al. 2010]. Our patient received only a brief steroid taper 1 month prior to onset of her neurological symptoms, which suggests her sarcoidosis, a T-lymphocyte mediated disorder, may be an underlying cause of her immunosuppressed state.
Clinical symptoms vary by the area of brain involvement. Our patient demonstrated significant dysarthria, dysmetria and ataxia, consistent with her cerebellar involvement. In classic PML, MRI of the brain shows asymmetric, subcortical white matter lesions that are hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences.
Brain biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosis. A less invasive alternative is PCR testing for JC virus in the CSF. In HIV-infected patients, sensitivity of this test in the era of highly active anti-retroviral therapy (HAART) ranges from 58-75%, with a specificity of 96-100% [Fong et al.1995, Marzocchetti et al. 2005]. Viral load testing in CSF is also now available, which may be helpful in assessing response to therapy [Bossolasco et al. 2005].
Current treatments for PML show poor efficacy. The first goal is to reduce immunosuppression. In AIDS patients, HAART must be initiated. Medications that inhibit JC viral replication in vitro include cytarabine (cytosine arabinoside), cidofovir and mefloquine. Most of the experience with using cidofovir has been in AIDS patients; in this patient population, several studies showed no significant difference in 1-year survival compared to HAART alone [Gasnault et al. 2009, Kraemer et al. 2008, De Luca et al. 2008]. An open-label study demonstrated clinical stabilization and even improvement in 7 of 19 non-AIDS PML patients after treatment with cytarabine [Aksamit 2001]. A clinical trial is currently underway examining the effectiveness of mefloquine, a drug traditionally used for malaria [clinicaltrials.gov/ NCT00746941]. Serotonin receptor blockers, specifically mirtazipine, are now being studied because it has been found that the JC virus enters cultured cells via the serotonin (5-HT2a) receptor [Tan et al. 2010].
Unfortunately, non-AIDS PML has a poor prognosis; one-year survival is estimated between 36-50% [Aksamit 2001]. Realistic goals of treatment are for stabilization, not complete reversal, of neurological symptoms.
Final Diagnosis
Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) caused by JC virus in a patient with sarcoidosis.
