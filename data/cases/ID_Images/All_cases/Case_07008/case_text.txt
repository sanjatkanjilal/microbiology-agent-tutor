History of Present Illness
2007 August Featured Case Summary: A man with HIV infection, fevers and abdominal symptoms.
A man in his forties presented with fevers to 104°F (40.0°C) of one month's duration. He also reported chills, abdominal bloating and nausea. There was a history of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; recent CD4 T-lymphocytes measured 14 cells per cubic millimeter and HIV-RNA was 83,000 copies per microliter.
A positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scan was performed in a city in Asia and revealed diffuse lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. The patient came to the United States for a second opinion.
Past Medical History
As above, a history of HIV infection, acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) and herpetic zoster.
Medications
The patient took azithromycin weekly and was also on ritonavir, indinavir, delavirdine, fosamprenavir, valgancyclovir, enfuvirtide, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and tenofovir.
Social History
The patient worked in the service industry.
Epidemiological History
The patient was from a large city in southeastern China. There was no history of travel to rural Southeast Asia and no intravenous drug use.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared unwell and fatigued. The temperature was 96.7°F (35.9°C), blood pressure was 100/60 mm Hg, pulse 70 beats per minute and oxygen saturation 97% on ambient air. Mild scleral icterus was present without hepatosplenomegaly. One small umbilicated papule was seen on his left hand. There was no palpable lymphadenopathy.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 5,900 cells/mm³, hematocrit 29.5% and platelets 94,000/mm³. Serum sodium was 126 mmol/L, urea nitrogen 29 mg/dL, creatinine 2.3 mg/dL, alanine aminotransferase 95 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 288 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase 771 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 434 U/L and total bilirubin 5.7 mg/dL.

Treatment and Followup
The patient received intravenous amphotericin B (liposomal) for two weeks followed by a course of oral itraconazole. The fevers resolved and the blood cultures cleared on therapy.
At follow-up, highly active anti-retroviral therapy (HAART) was re-instated with lamivudine, zidovudine, tenofovir, ritonavir, and tipranavir. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and azithromycin prophylaxis medications were also administered.
Discussion
Penicillium marneffei is a dimorphic fungus that causes disseminated infection in patients with AIDS. It is the third most common opportunistic infection seen in AIDS patients in Northern Thailand and has been described throughout Southeast Asia as well as southern China and Hong Kong. Patients present with a subacute illness characterized by fever, weight loss and skin lesions; these lesions tend to be papular with central umbilication reminiscent of molluscum. Fifty percent of patients present with fungemia, lymphadenopathy and cough. Diagnosis of Penicillium marneffei infection is made when a smear of blood or tissue reveals yeast forms extracellularly and within phagocytes. The extracellular forms may have a transverse septum. Culture at 30°C reveals a mold that produces a characteristic soluble red pigment (Figure 3). As of this writing, patients are often treated with amphotericin for two weeks, followed by ten weeks of itraconazole. Secondary prophylaxis with daily itraconazole is standard.
Final Diagnosis
Penicillium marneffei in a patient with HIV infection and AIDS from a large city in southeastern China.
