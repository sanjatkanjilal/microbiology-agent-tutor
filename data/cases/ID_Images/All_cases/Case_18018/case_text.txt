History of Present Illness
A woman in her thirties with diabetes and psychiatric disease was admitted to an inpatient ward in a general hospital in Lima, Peru with five days of crampy and generalized abdominal pain, loss of appetite, nausea and vomiting. She was noted to be drowsy for two days and developed persistent vomiting. She denied any fevers, dysuria and urinary frequency. She was brought to the hospital by her mother due to the persistence of symptoms and inability to tolerate her usual medications.
Past Medical History
She has a history of admissions to psychiatric centers for psychiatric disease.
Medications
Metformin, Valproic acid, Risperidone, Clonazepam
Physical Examination
The patient looked unwell and pale. Her skin was dry. Her vital signs were: BP 120/70 mmHg, pulse rate 98 bpm, respiratory rate 22 bpm, temperature 37°C (98.6°F), and oxygen saturation 95% at room air. She had cool extremities. There was no rash. She was tender in the hypogastric area and right hypochondriac and right renal angle. There were no masses and Murphy’s sign was negative.
On neurological assessment, she was drowsy and disoriented to time and place. The remainder of the physical exam was unremarkable.
Studies
White blood cell count (WBC): 15,300 cells/mL (reference range 4000-10,000) with neutrophils 76% and 5% bands; hemoglobin: 9 g/dL, blood glucose: 1,050 mg/dL (reference range 70-110 mg/dL), sodium 135 mEq/dL (reference range 135-145), potassium 4.1 mEq/dL (reference range 3.5-4.5), chloride 89 mEq/mL (reference range 87-110), urea: 28 mg/dL (reference range 10-45), serum creatinine: 1.0 mg/dL (reference range 10-45), plasma osmolarity: 330 mOsm/Kg, amylase: 36 U/L (reference range 30-60), lipase: 386 U/L (reference range 30-90). Arterial blood gas was normal.
Her urine dipstick was positive for leucocytes (Figure 1). Urinalysis showed >100 leukocytes/hpf, 2 – 3 RBCs/hpf, bacteria ++. Blood cultures were negative. Urine culture grew more than 100,000 colony forming units of Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
The chest radiograph showed no pulmonary infiltrates or effusion. Renal ultrasound: kidneys with parenchymal inflammatory process in both kidneys and preserved cortical thickness, no stones or urolithiasis were found.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was treated for a hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state based on laboratory findings with the possibility of pyelonephritis as the triggering factor. Empirical therapy with Ceftriaxone 2 grams daily was started. Her fevers persisted over the next five days and her WBC continued to rise, thus, this was changed to Meropenem 1 gram every 8 hours.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Susceptibility testing using disk diffusion showed resistance to antipseudomonal penicillins and cephalosporins, carbapenems, aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones (Table 1). A double disk diffusion assay using Imipenem plus EDTA suggested the presence of a metallo-beta-lactamase (MBL) (Figure 2).

Treatment and Followup
The full antibiogram demonstrating meropenem resistance became available two days after the commencement of meropenem by which time she had improved with resolution of her fevers and normalization of her WBC. Meropenem was continued for 10 days and she recovered completely.
Discussion
Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a common gram-negative pathogen associated with nosocomial infections. Over the last two decades, multi-drug resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa has become a problem, particularly the strains that produce MBLs (1). There have been several reports of outbreaks of (MDR) P. aeruginosa isolated from urine but the appropriate therapeutic regimen to combat infection with the organism have not been well established (1,2).
Risk factors associated with MBL-producing P. aeruginosa infections are insertion of ureteral stent; urologic diseases; end stage renal disease; female sex; advanced age (>60 years); previous beta-lactam, fluoroquinolone or carbapenem use; diabetes; previous hospitalization, and admission into an intensive care unit. (3, 4) Her multiple admissions to a psychiatric care facility may have played a role in the development of this event. (4) Eveillard et al. reported that the main multiple-drug resistant bacteria in psychiatric facilities were methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, followed P. aeruginosa, where patient-to-patient transmission was suspected (5).
One of the emergent problems in nosocomial transmission of P. aeruginosa infection is the transference of plasmids including MBLs (6). In Peru, the knowledge of the molecular epidemiology of these multidrug- resistant microorganisms is scarce. However, in one local study it was found that the most frequent mechanism of resistance to carbapenems among Pseudomonas was the production of MBLs (7,8).
We highlight the potential utility of a phenotypic method for the presumptive identification of MBL’s in P. aeruginosa. This test relies on combination disk testing using Imipenem and EDTA disks and is easy to perform and accessible. It shows a sensitivity of close to 93% and specificity of 100% in comparison with PCR (9). It should be noted, however, that this method is not endorsed by Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) for the detection of MBL. The reference methods such as PCR are not systematically applied to characterize MBL-producers in our setting, due to their high cost (10). We implemented these alternative techniques in our setting to enable antibiotic stewardship and reduce nosocomial transmission (10, 11).
These pathogens leave us with scarce options for treatment. Current guidelines recommend the use of high dose carbapenems or colistin, fosfomycin, tigecycline or aminoglycosides, depending on the organism’s susceptibility (12).
The new beta-lactam with beta-lactamase combinations, like ceftolozane/tazobactam, ceftazidime/avibactam and meropenem/vaborbactam, are not active against MBL but may be active against strains of Pseudomonas spp. that harbor other resistance mechanisms. (13, 14) Ceftolozane-tazobactam is the only combination available in Peru. It is not affordable by many of our patients and it is not available at our hospital.
In the present case, MIC quantification for carbapenems was not performed and the patient presented significant improvement only with the use of high doses of Meropenem.
Final Diagnosis
Pyelonephritis caused by metallo-beta-lactamases producing Pseudomonas aeruginosa
