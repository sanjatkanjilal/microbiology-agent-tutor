History of Present Illness
A gravida 2, para 1 (G2P1) woman in her thirties with a past medical history of a left breast fibroadenoma excised several years ago presented with a 3-week history of an enlarging, painful left breast mass. Two months prior to presentation, the patient had an asymptomatic ectopic pregnancy for which she received methotrexate without complication. Approximately five weeks later, she developed focal swelling and tenderness of her left medial breast without any antecedent trauma. This swelling evolved into a painful, palpable mass with associated nipple retraction, but no fevers, chills, or overlying erythema. She was not breastfeeding and denied any weight loss, cough, or sick contacts. Her last menstrual period was normal and ended just prior to the onset of her symptoms.
Past Medical History
A few years previously, she delivered a healthy baby and was no longer breastfeeding. She had received isoniazid as a child for a positive tuberculin purified protein derivative (PPD) test.
Medications
Prenatal vitamins
Epidemiological History
The patient was originally from Asia and immigrated to the United States as a teenager. She was married and lived with her husband and child. She denied any recent travel or sick contacts, including contact with persons known to have tuberculosis (TB). She denied use of tobacco, drugs, or alcohol and had no family history of breast or ovarian cancer.
Physical Examination
At presentation, the patient was well-appearing with a temperature of 98.2ºF (36.8ºC), blood pressure of 104/71, heart rate of 80, and respiratory rate of 16. Her breast examination was notable for a 2-3cm mass on the medial aspect of the left breast that was tender to palpation and poorly circumscribed. There was no overlying erythema or peau d’orange change noted. The left nipple was slightly retracted, but there was no nipple discharge. The right breast was normal, and no infraclavicular, supraclavicular, or axillary lymphadenopathy was appreciated. Her physical examination was otherwise normal.
Studies
Her leukocyte count was 10.7 K/uL (reference range (RR): 4.0-10.0 K/uL) with 75.2% neutrophils. Hemoglobin, platelet count, chemistries, and liver function testing were all normal. C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 6.5 mg/L (RR: 0.0-3.0 mg/L), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate was normal. Ultrasound of the left breast revealed an irregular, thick-walled complex fluid collection approximately 3cm medial to the nipple (Figure 1).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Ultrasound-guided aspiration yielded 6mL of purulent fluid and gram stain returned 4+ polys, no epithelial cells, and few Gram-positive rods. Routine cultures grew 1+ Corynebacterium species susceptible to doxycycline and amoxicillin/clavulanate, later identified as C. kroppenstedtii by 16s rRNA sequencing. In spite of a one-week course of cephalexin, followed by a three-month course of doxycycline for suspected cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis (CNGM), she remained symptomatic. Doxycycline was transitioned to amoxicillin/clavulanate, with no change in her breast pain and induration despite another three months of this treatment. Therefore, the patient underwent an incisional biopsy and left breast abscess drainage with histopathology that returned consistent with CNGM (Figures 2 and 3). Gram-positive rods consistent with Corynebacterium spp. were visualized on gram stain of the breast tissue histopathologic specimen. Routine, anaerobic, fungal, acid fast bacilli (AFB), and modified AFB stains and cultures returned negative. Additional studies including interferon-gamma release assay for TB and Bartonella serologies were negative and 1,3 beta-D-glucan and angiotensin converting enzyme were within normal limits.
Treatment and Followup
Given inadequate response to a prolonged (> 6 months) course of antibiotic therapy prior to the incisional biopsy, she was treated with prednisone 40mg oral daily for two weeks, followed by a slow taper over two months with complete resolution of her residual breast mass and tenderness. She remains asymptomatic one year later.
Discussion
This case demonstrates the classic clinical and pathologic findings of cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis, which is associated with Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii infection. Granulomatous mastitis is a rare inflammatory condition more common in parous, reproductive-aged women and usually presents as a unilateral, painful breast mass.[1-3] A broad array of infectious and non-infectious conditions have been implicated in the development of granulomatous mastitis, including tuberculosis, bartonellosis, fungal infections, sarcoidosis, and granulomatosis with polyangiitis.[4,5] Granulomatous mastitis can mimic breast carcinoma, and requires histologic examination for diagnosis.[3,4,6]
Cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis (CNGM) is a distinct type of granulomatous mastitis featuring lipogranulomas rimmed by neutrophils on histopathology (Figures 2 and 3), often with gram positive organisms visualized within the vacuoles.[2,4,7,8] This histologic pattern is associated with Corynebacteria infection, particularly infection with Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii. [1,4,8,9] Although identification of C. kroppenstedtii from breast tissue or abscess culture should prompt strong suspicion of CNGM, it is not entirely clear whether this organism is a causative agent or rather a secondary colonizer in these cases.[10] C. kroppenstedtii lacks a mycolic acid in the cell envelope, which makes it lipophilic, and well-suited for growth in breast tissue; it is infrequently isolated from other sites.[1,10] CNGM is typically seen in parous women who have developed lipid-rich mammary glands.[4] Several cases of Corynebacterium associated granulomatous mastitis have also been reported in nulliparous women with hyperprolactinemia, suggesting that lactational changes triggered by prolactin may increase risk of CNGM.[11] The role for therapy that reduces prolactin in treatment of CNGM remains unclear.
Management of CNGM can be challenging, with many patients undergoing multiple rounds of incision and drainage, repeated courses of antibiotics, and occasionally even immunosuppression as part of therapy.[4] Most antibiotics are hydrophilic, and therefore do not penetrate well into lipid-rich areas of the breast tissue.[12] Doxycycline is lipid soluble and has good activity against Corynebacterium, so is recommended for initial therapy.[7,12] Second-line agents include trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, clarithromycin, clindamycin, and rifampin, which are lipophilic and active against C. kroppenstedtii.[12] C. kroppenstedtii is also generally susceptible to vancomycin and linezolid, but is often resistant to beta-lactams.[12] While fluoroquinolones are used for other granulomatous infections, their low lipid solubility makes them less effective for CNGM.[13]
Corticosteroid therapy is another option for patients who do not respond to antibiotic therapy. In an observational, prospective, single-center cohort summary of women with idiopathic granulomatous mastitis 100% (44/44) of women who received steroids reported an initial response to therapy within two weeks.[3] Of these women, 35/44 (80%) achieved complete symptom resolution.[3] However, median time to resolution was 5.3 months, and one in four women experienced symptom recurrence within a year.[3] Lumpectomy and mastectomy are reserved for patients with antibiotic- and steroid-refractory disease.[3]
In summary, cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis is associated with C. kroppenstedtii infection and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of subacute unilateral breast swelling in women of reproductive age without clear epidemiologic risk factors for tuberculosis, autoimmune disease, or malignancy. Treatment options include lipophilic antibiotics active against Corynebacteria (e.g. doxycycline), surgical drainage, and corticosteroids.
Final Diagnosis
Cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis associated with C. kroppenstedtii infection
