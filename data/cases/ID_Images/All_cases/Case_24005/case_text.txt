History of Present Illness
A man in his 40s with a history of untreated HIV presented to the emergency department for subacute, progressive cough and shortness of breath. He reported associated fatigue, headache, and generalized weakness.
He was initially diagnosed with HIV one year prior to presentation but declined to start antiretroviral therapy. Two months prior to presentation he was admitted due to respiratory failure and multifocal pneumonia. At that time HIV RNA and CD4 count were 981,000 copies/mL and 20 cells/µL, 3%, respectively. He was hospitalized and treated empirically for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and prednisone. Bronchoscopy was recommended but was not completed. Initiation of antiretroviral therapy was deferred due to concern for immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, and he did not follow up in infectious disease clinic to start antiretroviral therapy. He returned to an emergency department one month later with persistent symptoms. He was again prescribed trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and prednisone for presumed Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia.

Physical Examination
The patient appeared thin and cachectic. Blood pressure was 119/81 mmHg, pulse 116 beats per minute, temperature 36.6 degrees C (97.9 degrees F), respirations 20 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation was 92% on 4 L by nasal cannula. He was noted to be coughing on exam with moderate increased work of breathing. His lung sounds were diminished to auscultation with bilateral apical wheezing. He was noted to have non-tender purple papules on the anterior trunk. The remainder of the physical exam was within normal limits.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
On admission the patient was started on ceftriaxone 2 grams IV daily and doxycycline 100 mg by mouth twice daily for empiric treatment of bacterial pneumonia. The prednisone dose was tapered from 80 to 40 mg. He had progressively increasing oxygen requirements, and on hospital day 5 was placed on high flow nasal cannula and transferred to the medical intensive care unit. IV ceftriaxone was changed to IV piperacillin-tazobactam and IV ganciclovir added for cytomegalovirus viremia. His respiratory status continued to decline, and he was intubated on hospital day 7. IV Liposomal amphotericin B was added for empiric antifungal coverage and prednisone was weaned to 30 mg daily. He continued to have rising oxygen requirement and was cannulated for extracorporeal membranous oxygenation on hospital day 9.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Transthoracic echocardiogram showed no evidence of valvular abnormality or vegetation to suggest infective endocarditis. Dermatologic pathology of the anterior chest wall lesion demonstrated elongated spindle shaped cells and immunohistochemical testing was positive for human-herpesvirus-8. Bronchoscopy was completed and pathology of lung tissue demonstrated spindle cell neoplasm consistent with Kaposi sarcoma with immunohistochemical testing positive for human-herpesvirus-8. Bronchoscopic cultures and pathologic staining for fungus and acid-fast bacilli were negative.
Treatment and Followup
He was started on antiretroviral therapy with bictegravir-emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide on hospital day 6. Following pathologic confirmation of Kaposi sarcoma diagnosis on skin and bronchoscopic biopsy, oncology was consulted and he was initiated on chemotherapy with intravenous doxorubicin (hospital day 8). He received a 10-day course of intravenous piperacillin tazobactam for treatment of pneumonia. Intravenous liposomal amphotericin B was discontinued after 3 days in the absence of confirmed fungal infection. Intravenous ganciclovir was stopped on hospital day 15 after cytomegalovirus virus blood polymerase chain reaction resulted below the limit of quantification (reference range not detected). He was decannulated from extracorporeal membranous oxygenation on hospital day 16 and extubated on hospital day 19. He received his second dose of intravenous doxorubicin on hospital day 29.
He was discharged to home on hospital day 31 with bictegravir-emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide for antiretroviral therapy and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis prophylaxis. Following discharge, he moved to a different state and transferred his care to a new health system. Five months following his discharge he has completed 5 cycles of intravenous doxorubicin and continues on bictegravir-emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide for antiretroviral therapy with HIV quantitative polymerase chain reaction showing 167 copies/mL (reference range < 20 copies/mL).
Discussion
This patient with advanced HIV presented with subacute respiratory failure after treatment with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and prednisone for presumed Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. In the setting of his advanced HIV, he was at risk of AIDS-associated opportunistic infections including Pneumocystis jirovecii, toxoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, tuberculosis, and endemic fungal infections. Physical exam of the patient’s trunk rash was suspicious for cutaneous Kaposi sarcoma and this was subsequently confirmed with skin biopsy. Additional testing for pneumocystis, cryptococcus, tuberculosis, and endemic fungal infections was negative. In the presence of cutaneous Kaposi sarcoma, there was concern for more advanced Kaposi sarcoma with pulmonary involvement. Bronchoscopic biopsy of lung tissue confirmed pulmonary Kaposi sarcoma.
Kaposi sarcoma is a mesenchymal tumor of blood and lymphatic vessels caused by the oncogenic human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8) in the context of immunosuppression. 1-2 HIV/AIDS-associated Kaposi sarcoma typically appears with multifocal, cutaneous, brownish plaques. 2 Visceral Kaposi sarcoma is a less common variant that most often involves the stomach, spleen, bowels, liver, and lungs. 2 Pulmonary Kaposi sarcoma typically manifests as cough, dyspnea, and hemoptysis. In the era of effective antiretroviral therapy the incidence of HIV/AIDS-associated Kaposi sarcoma has rapidly declined, and as a result is often associated with delayed diagnosis. 1-3
The differential diagnosis for hypoxic respiratory failure and multifocal pneumonia in people with advanced HIV/AIDS is broad; however, diagnosis can be challenging and patients are often presumed to have pneumocystis pneumonia and started on steroids. Glucocorticoid use in persons with Kaposi sarcoma has been associated with worsening disease and mortality, with up to a four-fold increase in mortality associated with steroid use. 1-3 This patient’s clinical course complicated by extended high-dose glucocorticoid use prior to presentation may have accelerated progression of Kaposi sarcoma.
This case demonstrates the successful use of veno-venous extracorporeal membranous oxygenation (VV-ECMO) for respiratory failure secondary to pulmonary Kaposi sarcoma. VV-ECMO has been previously used in multiple cases of respiratory failure in other AIDS associated opportunistic infections including Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. 4-5 The use of VV-ECMO for respiratory failure secondary to disseminated Kaposi sarcoma has only been documented in two case reports previously. 6,7 This case represents a successful use of VV-ECMO for respiratory failure due to AIDS-associated visceral Kaposi sarcoma.
Final Diagnosis
Respiratory failure secondary to HIV/AIDS associated visceral Kaposi sarcoma
