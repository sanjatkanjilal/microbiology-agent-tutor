History of Present Illness
2013 July-August Featured Case: A woman in her thirties developed a progressive bifrontal headache associated with a temperature to 100.0°F (37.8°C). She went to sleep in the late afternoon. Approximately 24 hours later, her husband found her difficult to arouse, nonverbal and non-interactive. She was unable to arise from bed, answer questions or follow commands, and appeared confused. She was brought to the emergency department for evaluation.
Past Medical History
She had previously been well.
Medications
None.
Epidemiological History
The patient was born and raised in Guatemala, and moved to the Southeastern United States nine years earlier. Two years before this evaluation, she moved to an urban area of New England. She lived with her husband and young daughter, and worked as a housekeeper. She did not drink alcohol, smoke tobacco, or use illicit drugs.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared somnolent, arousable to painful stimuli, and unable to follow commands consistently. The blood pressure was 143/60 mm Hg, pulse 59 beats per minute, temperature 38.3°C (101.0°F), respirations 20 beats per minute, and oxygenation 98% while breathing room air. On neurological examination, the pupils were symmetric and sluggishly reactive from 4mm to 3mm; extra-ocular movements were grossly intact. She was able to open her eyes and moved all four extremities spontaneously. Deep tendon reflexes were 2+ and symmetric bilaterally. The remainder of the examination was otherwise normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 17,900 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4,500-11,000) with an absolute neutrophil count of 14,770 cells per cubic millimeter (ref. 1800-7700). The hemoglobin and platelet counts were normal. Other routine laboratory tests including blood levels of electrolytes, and tests of coagulation, renal function and liver function were normal. Serum toxicology panel was negative.
Urinalysis revealed a specific gravity of 1.030, trace ketones, trace urobilinogen, 2+ protein, and 10-20 red blood cells and 3-5 white blood cells per high power field. Culture of the urine and blood were obtained, and were sterile.
A chest radiograph was normal. Computed tomography (CT) of the head, performed without the administration of contrast (Figure 1) revealed enlargement of the lateral and third ventricles consistent with hydrocephalus, a fluid-filled vesicular structure in the frontal horn of the left lateral ventricle near the foramen of Monroe with a soft-tissue density inside the cyst, and calcified densities scattered throughout the supratentorial brain.
Figure 1. CT of the brain, sagittal view, without contrast.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was intubated and bilateral external ventricular devices were placed emergently, with improvement in the severity of hydrocephalus on follow-up CT imaging. Dexamethasone (4mg every 6 hours), levetiracetam, ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and acyclovir were administered. Analysis of cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) obtained from the external ventricular drains revealed a glucose level of 79 mg/dl (reference range 50-75), a normal total protein level; 1150 red blood cells per cubic millimeter, and 2 white blood cells per cubic millimeter.
On the second hospital day, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain was performed with the administration of contrast (Figures 2, 3 and 4).
Figure 2. MRI of the brain, sagittal T1 view.
Figure 3. MRI of the brain, axial fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) view.
Figure 4. MRI of the brain, coronal view, reformatted.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The soft-tissue density seen within the cyst on CT of the head (Figure 1) was consistent with a scolex, and the innumerable calcified densities scattered throughout the supratentorial brain were suggestive of neurocysticercosis. MRI of the brain (Figures 2, 3 and 4) revealed a nonenhancing multilobed cystic mass (28.4mm in its greatest dimension) within the left lateral ventricle and numerous calcified foci throughout the cerebral hemispheres, as were seen on CT. MRI of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine were negative. An ophthalmologic examination revealed no evidence of ocular involvement.
On the seventh hospital day, a left-sided mini-craniectomy with left frontal endoscopic removal of the intraventricular cyst (Figure 5) and fenestration of the septum pellucidum was performed. The left-sided external ventricular drain was also removed.
Histopathological examination of the resected tissue with hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed a cysticercus celluosac with the encysted larva (Figures 6 through 8). The scolex with suckers and hooklets were visible within the sac. Testing for cysticercus IgG antibodies was positive in the serum and negative in the cerebrospinal fluid.
Testing for serum HIV antibodies, interferon-gamma release assay, and mycobacterial DNA from the cerebrospinal fluid were negative. Cultures of the cerebrospinal fluid for bacteria, fungi, and mycobacteria were sterile.

Treatment and Followup
Ceftriaxone and acyclovir were stopped when cerebrospinal fluid studies returned. Vancomycin was continued while the external ventricular devices remained in place. Dexamethasone (4mg every 6 hours) was continued and ivermectin was administered for empiric treatment of strongyloidiasis.
On the first postoperative day, albendazole (400mg orally twice a day) was started for a two-week course in combination with dexamethasone at a reduced dose (4mg every 12 hours). Approximately two weeks after starting treatment, she was seen in follow up with near resolution of symptoms. The albendazole was stopped and a dexamethasone taper begun. Levetiracetam, which had been started at the time of diagnosis, was continued.
Discussion
Cysticercosis is an infection caused by the larval stage of the pork tapeworm, Taenia solium [Del Brutto et al., 2001; Garcia et al., 2005; John and Petri, 2006; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. Infection occurs through ingestion of Taenia eggs (typically fecal contamination) from an infected individual [Garcia et al., 2005;John and Petri, 2006]. Infections in the United States are largely from immigrant populations from Africa, Asia, and Latin America where prevalence rates are high [John and Petri, 2006;Nash and Garcia, 2011]]. This patient was from an endemic area which increased the clinical suspicion for this parasite as a cause of her symptoms.
Once ingested, the eggs travel through the blood stream to any part of the body where they settle and grow into larvae [Garcia et al., 2005; John and Petri, 2006; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. The most common location that leads to medical attention is the central nervous system, termed neurocysticercosis (NCC) [Garcia et al., 2005;John and Petri, 2006]. Initial infection is often asymptomatic as the parasites evoke mechanisms to avoid immune detection [John and Petri, 2006]. Over time, the cysticerci degenerate, often resulting in a profound inflammatory response leading to a multitude of symptoms dependent on the location of the parasites [Garcia et al., 2005;John and Petri, 2006]. In intraparenchymal NCC in which the cysticerci have lodged within the brain parenchyma, the most common clinical manifestations are headache and seizure due to cortical irritation from the inflammatory response [Garcia et al., 2005;John and Petri, 2006]. In extraparenchymal NCC, in which the cysticerci have lodged within the ventricular and/or subarachnoid spaces, hydrocephalus secondary to mechanical obstruction from the cysts or secondary inflammation is the common clinical presentation [Garcia et al., 2005;John and Petri, 2006], as it was in this patient. Dead cysticerci evoke little inflammatory response and remain as calcified lesions within the brain parenchyma, though patients with calcified lesions may still have periodic seizures [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011].
The diagnosis of NCC is often made clinically in combination with distinct radiographic findings and endemic exposure [Del Brutto et al., 2001; Garcia et al., 2005]. In this patient, the diagnosis was made with computed tomography (CT) of the head revealing hydrocephalus with identification of the cysticercus and its scolex within the lateral ventricle. Although not often seen on computed tomography due to the isodensity of the cyst and cerebrospinal fluid, the radiographic identification of the scolex within a cystic lesion is pathognomonic for NCC [Del Brutto et al., 2001]. The numerous hyperdense lesions also seen were consistent with calcified intraparenchymal NCC.
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain with contrast confirmed the findings with calcified intraparenchymal lesions best seen with susceptibility sequence, and periventricular and intraventricular lesions best appreciated with a combination of T2/FLAIR and T1 sequences, especially FIESTA series [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. In general, magnetic resonance imaging is superior to computed tomography imaging in identifying key anatomy as well as the cysticerci at various stages of degeneration [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. Serology for T. solium remains the gold standard for diagnosis and was positive in this patient. A positive serology alone, however, does not differentiate between active and past infection [Garcia et al., 2000]]. Newer monoclonal antibody T. solium antigen detection assays are also available and have been shown to be helpful in the differentiation of active versus inactive infection and response to treatment [Garcia et al., 2005; Garcia et al., 2002; Nash and Garcia, 2011; Rodriguez et al. 2009; Garcia et al., 2000]. As with this patient, basic serum hematology and chemistry tests are usually normal. Cerebrospinal fluid is often characterized by mild inflammation with a pleocytosis that can be eosinophilic with normal protein and glucose levels [Venna et al., 2012].
Primary treatment of NCC focuses on symptomatic management of the complications due to infection [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. Antihelminthic treatment is deferred until the patient has stabilized as treatment may worsen symptoms [Nash and Garcia, 2011]. Antiepileptic medications and corticosteroids are administered to control seizures and reduce inflammation [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. Urgent surgical treatment of intracranial hypertension, if present, is also required [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. Once medically stable, exoneration of intraocular and spinal infection should be performed [Venna et al., 2012]. This is best done with a direct fundoscopic exam, thorough neurological examination and in certain instances, spinal imaging. Once metastatic sites have been exonerated, further surgical and/or antihelminthic treatment can be considered depending on the location and extent of disease [Nash and Garcia, 2011]. In active intraparenchymal NCC, surgical treatment is often unnecessary. Medical treatment with albendazole (15mg/kg in two divided doses) with prednisone (1mg/kg/day) for 8 to 14 days is the recommended approach, as it reduces the probability of long-term epilepsy [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011; Venna et al., 2012]. Treatment of inactive/calcified parenchymal disease, however, is not indicated as it is of no known benefit [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. In extraventricular NCC, surgical removal of the obstructing cysts, if possible, remains the mainstay of treatment [Nash and Garcia, 2011; Venna et al., 2012]. Neuroendoscopic removal is the preferred method although open surgical procedures are also done [Venna et al., 2012]. With intraventricular disease alone, surgical treatment is the mainstay of management. Assessment for other sites of disease (including subarachnoid and spinal) is necessary as this may require additional management.
If surgery is not possible, combination treatment with corticosteroids and albendazole for 1 to 3 months can be considered [Nash and Garcia, 2011]. In cases of subarachnoid NCC, cure is difficult and longer courses of albendazole and corticosteroids are needed with repeated radiological and serum antigen assays to assess for recurrence [Garcia et al., 2005; Nash and Garcia, 2011]. In ocular and spinal cysticercosis, surgical treatment is often warranted [Venna et al., 2012]. In all cases which might require prolonged corticosteroid administration, screening for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B and strongyloidiasis should also be performed given risk of co-infection [Venna et al., 2012].
Final Diagnosis
Intraventricular and intraparenchymal neurocysticercosis, caused by larval cysts of Taenia solium.
