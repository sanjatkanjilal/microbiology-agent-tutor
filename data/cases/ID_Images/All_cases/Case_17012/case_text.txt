History of Present Illness
A previously well woman in her fifties, presented with fatigue, confusion and fever for 3 days. Her family physician prescribed azithromycin, but later that day, she was brought to the emergency room with worsening confusion and word finding difficulty. She felt that her thinking was not normal. She denied headache, cough, dysuria, or changes in bowel or bladder function.
Allergies
Sulfonamide (rash)
Social History
She lived in a town in Midwestern US. She was an avid outdoors person and was frequently out in wooded areas. She had a desk job and did not use alcohol or illicit drugs.
She had a 40 pack-year smoking history.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared somnolent. Her blood pressure was 135/72 mmHg, pulse 115 beats per minute, temperature 38.5°C (101.3 ° F), and respirations 24 breaths per minute. She was awake, alert, but oriented only to self with limited attention span. There were faint palpable pink papules over the abdomen and anterior thighs (Figure 1). Heart sounds were normal with no murmurs, and respiratory and abdominal exams were normal. She had no neck stiffness, and no motor or sensory changes.
Figure 1. Erythematous papular rash over anterior right thigh. The patient had the same rash over her back, abdomen, and bilateral thighs.
Studies
The complete blood count including platelets and white cell differential were normal. Other routine lab results were normal. Blood cultures were negative. CT of the head without contrast showed no acute intracranial abnormality.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
She was started empirically on vancomycin, ceftriaxone and acyclovir. Lumber puncture (LP) was performed after initiation of antimicrobials. Two hours after the LP was performed, a rapid response team was called for worsening somnolence and tachypnea (respiratory rate > 40 breaths/min). She was transferred to the neurologic intensive care unit and was intubated for airway protection. The infectious disease service was consulted, and meropenem and doxycycline were added 24 hours after hospital admission.
Her cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that was obtained 10 hours after antibiotics showed elevated total nucleated cell count of 146/uL (9% neutrophils, 56% lymphocytes, and 35% monocytes/ macrophages), 14 erythrocytes/uL, a normal glucose of 70 mg/dL (plasma glucose was 140 mg/dL), and an elevated protein of 109 mg/dL (reference range 0-35). Gram stain was negative. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on hospital day 2 showed T2/FLAIR hyperintensity of the left caudate and thalamus and marked leptomeningeal enhancement involving the cerebellum and basal ganglia (Figure 2, 3).
Figure 2. MRI brain without contrast at initial presentation. Axial T2 FLAIR showed T2 hyperintensity at the left thalamus (arrow).
Figure 3. MRI brain with gadolinium contrast at initial presentation. Axial T2 FLAIR showed T2 hyperintensity at the left substantia nigra (arrow) and leptomeningeal enhancement in the temporal horn of the left lateral ventricle (asterisk).
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
CSF gram stain, fungal smear, bacterial and fungal cultures were negative. CSF PCR tests for herpes simplex 1 and 2, Epstein-Barr virus, Varicella Zoster virus, West Nile virus, Cytomegalovirus, Tropheryma whipplei, Borrelia burgdorferi, B. mayonii, and B. garinii were negative. CSF was positive for immunoglobulin M (IgM) against Powassan virus (POWV) via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Additional CSF was sent to Centers for Disease Control (CDC) for Prevention for Plaque Reduction Neutralization Testing (PRNT), which returned positive.
Nasopharyngeal swab was negative for influenza. Blood cultures were negative. Serum polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests for Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella, Anaplasma phagocytophilum and Ehrlichia spp. were negative.
Treatment and Followup
CSF studies for Streptococcal pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenza, and HSV-1, and 2 were negative on hospital day 2, so broad spectrum antibiotics and acyclovir were discontinued. A trial of IV methylprednisolone was initiated on hospital day 3 for possible post-viral encephalitis. Repeat brain MRI on hospital day 5 showed interval improvement. She was extubated on hospital day 13. Patient was transferred out of the ICU on hospital day 18, but she remained abulic, and had residual flaccidity of the right arm accompanied by signs of Parkinsonism. She underwent percutaneous endoscopic gastrotomy (PEG) tube placement for nutrition and was transferred to inpatient rehabilitation unit for a month prior to being discharged to a skilled nursing facility. Unfortunately, she developed additional complications and died a few months after her first presentation to the hospital.
Discussion
When possible, clinicians are encouraged to distinguish between meningitis syndromes and encephalitis syndromes both to help narrow the differentials and to guide empirical therapy. Typically patients with meningitis present with headache, neck stiffness, photophobia and fever. An acute presentation of a meningitis syndrome (1-2 days) is more typical for bacterial causes, 3-10 days more typical of viral meningitis while subacute and chronic headaches raises the possibility of tuberculous meningitis, cryptococcal meningitis and other fungal and parasitic causes. A brain abscess should be considered particularly in those presenting with focal neurological deficits. In this case, the lymphocytic predominance in the CSF and it being culture negative goes against bacterial causes, although the previous use of antibiotics may affect CSF bacterial cultures.
In contrast, an encephalitis syndrome as seen here is typified by confusion and fevers. A wide variety of viruses can infect the central nervous system, and the treatment for most is supportive. Enterovirus is the most common cause of viral encephalitis. HSV encephalitis should be treated with acyclovir.
Host risk factors, epidemiologic and clinical clues are important in determining the exact pathogen. In this case, the patient was an avid outdoor person living in Midwestern U.S. and presented with symptoms during the fall season; she had several risk factors for mosquito and tick exposure, hence raising concern for CNS infection caused by arboviruses. In the U.S., there is an arbovirus surveillance system managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and state public health departments. In 2015, West Nile virus was the most common cause of arboviral disease (2175 of 2282 cases, 95%). Seven cases of Powassan virus infection were reported – variously from Maine, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, and Wisconsin (Krow-Lucal, Lindsey et al. 2017).
Powassan virus (POWV) was first isolated in Powassan, Ontario in 1958. Powassan meningoencephalitis is rare, with approximately 100 cases reported since 1958 (Birge and Sonnesyn 2012). POWV is an arbovirus in the family of Flaviviridae. It is transmitted by Ixodes scapularis, Ixodes cookei, and other Ixodes tick species. There are two distinct genetic lineages of POWV that have been recognized: lineage I is transmitted by I. cookei, and lineage II is by I. scapularis (Hermance and Thangamani 2017). POWV can be transmitted within 15 minutes following attachment to the host (Ebel 2010). The incubation period ranges from 1 to 5 weeks (Ebel 2010, Birge and Sonnesyn 2012). Presenting symptoms of Powassan virus meningoencephalitis include fever, confusion, seizures, and focal neurologic deficits. Rash and gastrointestinal symptoms may also be present.
Pathogenesis is related to the perivascular and parenchymal infiltrate of lymphocytes, more often involving the gray matter (Gholam, Puksa et al. 1999). CSF analysis usually shows lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose and elevated protein. Diagnosis is made by clinical findings with positive IgM antibody against Powassan virus in serum or CSF, and the positive test is confirmed at CDC with PRNT. (Johnson, Staples et al. 2010, Raval, Singhal et al. 2012).
Cases in the United States have been reported in the Northeastern and Midwestern states. Of cases reported, 10-15% cases are fatal, and 50% of survivors have residual neurologic deficits. Treatment is supportive. Empiric treatment with high dose corticosteroids or IVIG have been given to patients with POWV encephalitis but their role in changing outcomes is uncertain (Piantadosi, Rubin et al. 2016). In summary, evaluation of viral encephalitis should include CNS imaging and CSF examination with additional testing based on epidemiologic and clinical clues, and clinicians should consider POWV as a cause for viral encephalitis in patients hailing from or travelling through the Great Lakes region.
Final Diagnosis
Powassan meningoencephalitis
