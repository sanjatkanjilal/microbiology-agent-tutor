History of Present Illness
A man in his thirties with morbid obesity and diabetes mellitus was transferred to a tertiary care hospital in Pennsylvania because of shock, multi-organ failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
One week before admission, fatigue, myalgias, fever, chills, nausea and vomiting developed, for which the patient reportedly took acetaminophen as needed (no more than 2 grams daily). He saw his physician and erythromycin was prescribed for presumed streptococcal pharyngitis. Shortly afterwards, abdominal pain and diarrhea developed. Five days after the onset of symptoms, he presented to another hospital. He reported weight loss of 20 pounds (9.1kg) during the previous month.
At the other hospital, he was reportedly febrile and hypotensive. Laboratory test results reportedly included a white cell count of 1700 cells per cubic millimeter (including 13% band forms), platelet count 62,000 per cubic millimeter, alanine aminotransferase (ALT, SGPT) 1677 IU/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST, SGOT) 5219 IU/L, and acetaminophen was reportedly present at a nontoxic level.
He was admitted to the hospital and vancomycin, levofloxacin, ceftriaxone and cefepime were administered, without improvement. The patient became obtunded and was transferred to a tertiary care hospital the next day.
Past Medical History
The patient had diabetes mellitus type 2, morbid obesity, hypothyroidism and vitamin D deficiency. He had never received influenza vaccinations.
Medications
Medications at home included metformin, testosterone, levothyroxine, calcium and vitamin D supplements.
Social History
He lived with his wife and children. He had been unemployed for several months before presentation.
Epidemiological History
The patient lived in a rural area and had had a tick bite 5 months before this presentation. Three weeks before admission, one of his children was diagnosed with hand-foot-mouth disease. The next week, the patient had stripped the siding of an old house and scratched his wrist. He had been exposed to mud and fresh water 3 days before the onset of symptoms. He had no history of travel and did not drink alcohol, smoke or use intravenous drugs. He was monogamous with his wife. He had 2 pets at home.
Physical Examination
The patient was obese, and appeared in distress, disoriented and confused. The blood pressure was 80/61 mmHg, pulse 94 beats per minute, temperature 38.2°C (100.8°F), and oxygen saturation 94% while breathing oxygen at 6 liters per minute by nasal cannula. The mucous membranes were dry and there were petechiae on the palate. Rhonchi were heard in both lungs. The abdomen was soft, with hepatomegaly and exquisite tenderness in the right upper quadrant and diffuse tenderness in other regions. There were 1-to-2 beats of clonus, intact cranial nerves, and no nuchal rigidity; the patient was unable to cooperate with the remainder of the neurological exam. The remainder of the general examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 3000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 3800-10,600) with a differential including neutrophils 52%, band forms 30%, lymphocytes 6%, hemoglobin 11.6 mg/dl (reference range 13.5-17.5), and platelets 27,000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 156,000-369,000); the blood level of urea nitrogen was 44 mg/dl (reference range 8-26), creatinine 3.8 mg/dl (reference range 0.5-1.4), total bilirubin 2.6 mg/dl (reference range 0.3-1.5), direct bilirubin 1.7mg/dl (reference range 0.1-0.5), alanine aminotransferase 2116 IU/L (reference range 17-63), aspartate aminotransferase 9208 IU/L (reference range 15-41), alkaline phosphatase 346 IU/L (reference range 38-126), GGTP 118 IU/L (reference range <66), and lactate 5.4 mMol/L (reference range 0.5-1.6). Blood gases revealed pH 7.23, partial pressure of carbon dioxide 33 mmHg (reference range 32-48), partial pressure of oxygen 104 mmHg (reference range 83-108), HCO3 13 mEq/L (reference range (22-26). Acetaminophen was not detected.
An ultrasound of the abdomen demonstrated steatosis with no intra- or extrahepatic biliary dilatation, and the gallbladder was not distended. Computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis, performed without the administration of contrast, was negative.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient became somnolent and the trachea was intubated for airway protection. Shortly thereafter, hematemesis occurred; a Minnesota tube was placed to control bleeding and pressors were administered. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) did not reveal the source of bleeding. Piperacillin-tazobactam, doxycycline, metronidazole, fluconazole, vancomycin and acyclovir were administered. Blood, platelets and fresh frozen plasma were transfused.
Acidosis worsened, with pH decreasing to 7.07 and lactate rising to 17mMol/L. Continuous renal replacement therapy was started. Hypoxia occurred while being ventilated with 100% oxygen.
His condition deteriorated rapidly and he died, 36 hours after transfer. An autopsy was performed; pathological examination of the liver (Figures 1 and 2) and adrenal gland (Figure 3) is shown.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Testing for herpes simplex virus (HSV) type-1 DNA in the blood by PCR revealed greater than 560,000 copies/ml. Other testing, including for nucleic acid of herpes simplex virus type-2, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein-Barr virus by PCR; hepatitis B core antigen and antibody; hepatitis B surface antibody; anti-hepatitis A antibodies; anti-hepatitis C antibodies; antibodies to leptospirosis and serum and urine Histoplasma antigen were negative.
On autopsy, there was hepatomegaly, with extensive coagulative necrosis, limited inflammatory infiltrate and hepatocyte nuclear inclusions (Figures 1, 2 and 4). Pathological examination of the adrenal gland (Figures 3 and 5) showed cortical necrosis with viral inclusions. Immune histochemical staining for HSV-1 was positive in the liver and adrenal gland (Figures 6 and 7, respectively). Other findings on autopsy included esophageal mucosal ulceration with necrosis and huge sloughed squamous epithelial cells with nuclear viral inclusions, congestive splenomegaly with acute immunoblastic proliferation suggestive of acute viral infection, and renal enlargement with acute tubular necrosis.

Treatment and Followup
Discussion
This young male patient presented with shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and multi-organ failure one week after the onset of prodromal symptoms of fever, fatigue, nausea and vomiting.
Regarding the diagnoses considered, if untreated, Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF) can progress to multi-organ failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in 7-15 days. Spotless Rocky Mountain fever can be seen in black patients, which may delay diagnosis and treatment. However, RMSF is rare in Pennsylvania (0.1-1.0 per one million persons per year [Walker 2010]).
Weil’s disease can progress to acute renal and liver failure after the acute phase and is usually characterized by a high fever up to 40°C (104.0°F) and, unlike the findings in this patient, marked hyperbilirubinemia and a modest elevation of the transaminases [Levett 2001]. Reyes syndrome is most commonly seen in children but has been reported in adults. It is characterized by a prodromal viral illness followed by vomiting, worsening mental status in the absence of focal neurological deficits. The use of aspirin has been linked to the development of Reyes syndrome [Meythaler and Varma 1987].
Herpes simplex virus (HSV) fulminant liver failure can be seen in immunosuppressed patients, primarily in transplant recipients. HSV hepatitis accounts for less than 1% of all acute liver failure. Around 100 cases have been described in the literature. Fulminant HSV hepatitis may represent primary infection or reactivation. Clues to the diagnosis are mucocutaneous lesions, marked elevation in transaminases with normal or slightly elevated bilirubin and DIC [Wind et al. 2012]. Patients who are male, older age, immunocompromised, and/or presenting with significant liver dysfunction are more likely to progress to death [Norvell et al. 2007].
Herpes simplex virus (HSV)-1 typically causes orolabial infections and has an estimated seroprevalence of 62%. Hepatic involvement during infections with HSV-1 and HSV-2 is rare. Neonates, malnourished children, pregnant women, and patients receiving immunosuppressive medications are particularly at risk. Less than 10 cases of fulminate hepatitis described in immunocompetent patients [Talwani et al. 2011]
HSV hepatitis occurs as a so-called concomitant hepatitis in systemic HSV infection or HSV sepsis and is characterized by fulminant hepatic necrosis, with serum aminotransferase levels 100 to 1000 fold above normal [Riediger et al. 2009]. In one study, 24% were considered immunocompetent. The remaining patients were either pregnant (23%) or immunocompromised from a previous solid organ or hematopoietic cell transplantation (30%) or immunosuppressive agent (23%). The majority were first diagnosed after death on autopsy (57.6%) [Norvell et al. 2007].
In another study, at the time of hospitalization, HSV hepatitis patients had higher median HSV DNA load (6,000,000) compared to disease controls (171±2,854). Viral DNA load correlated with liver transaminase levels and disease severity. [Beersma et al. 2011].
Variables associated with death include age greater than 40 years, male gender, coagulopathy, immunocompromised state, encephalopathy, alanine aminotransferase greater than 5,000U/L, and platelet count less than 75,000 per cubic millimeter. Patients treated with acyclovir were less likely to progress to death or liver transplantation, 51% versus 88% (P value 0.001) [Norvell et al. 2007].
Empiric use of acyclovir, recommended by many authors, should be considered while the diagnostic evaluation of non-acetaminophen induced fulminant hepatitis is underway (Peters et al. 2000). In one study the percentage of treated patients who progressed to death or liver transplantation was 51% compared with 88.1% of those who were untreated [Riediger et al. 2009, Norvell et al. 2009].
Final Diagnosis
Disseminated Herpes Simplex Virus-1 infection with fulminant HSV hepatitis.
