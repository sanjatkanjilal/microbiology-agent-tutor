History of Present Illness
A man in his 30s with well-controlled HIV infection (CD4+ T-cells > 1000 cells/uL) presented with one week of intermittent abdominal pain and cramping. He reported reduced appetite in the week preceding presentation, as well as intermittent nausea, vomiting and watery diarrhea. The patient denied any blood in his stool, and fevers, chills, and rigors.
The patient did not recall any sick contacts and had not recently travelled. He reported that he was last sexually active 2 weeks prior to presentation (reciprocal oral sex with a male partner), but denied any recent oral-anal contact.
He had dental work three months ago for which he received clindamycin prophylaxis.
Past Medical History
The patient reported history of well-controlled HIV without recent change in antiretroviral regimen. He also had gastro-esophageal reflux disease.
Medications
Medications were emtricitabine-tenofovir alafenamide, etravirine and ranitidine.
Physical Examination
The patient’s blood pressure was 123/82, pulse rate 69 bpm and oral temperature 36.7°C (98.1°F).
On physical examination the patient appeared comfortable. His abdomen was soft and non-tender. Bowel sounds were normal and there was no rebound or guarding. Cardiovascular, respiratory and neurological examinations were normal. There was no cervical, axillary, nor inguinal lymphadenopathy.
Studies
A complete blood count was notable for white blood cells of 13,000 cells per microliter of which 55% were neutrophils; he was not anemic and he had normal platelet count. He had normal renal function and hepatic function.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
A stool culture was positive. (Figure 1)
Microbiology
Given the patient’s diarrhea, a stool culture was performed. After 24 hours of incubation a translucent, flat bacterial colony that appeared to spread across the plate was seen on the blood agar plate (Figure 1). Similar colorless colonies were seen on MacConkey and Hektoen enteric agar plates indicating this organism did not ferment lactose, salicin, or sucrose (Figures 2 and 3). Oxidase, indole, and motility testing were negative. An acetate series which included an acetate slant, mucate broth, andrades xylose, andrades salicin, and a citrate slant were also negative. Triple sugar iron agar (TSI) and lysine iron agar (LIA) slants were created. The TSI slant revealed an alkaline reaction in the top and an acidic reaction in the butt of the slant, suggesting glucose fermentation (Figure 4). No gas or hydrogen sulfide was seen. The LIA slant had a purple reaction in the top and a red reaction in the butt of the slant indicating the organism was both lysine deaminase and lysine decarboxylase negative (Figure 5). Based on the above findings, serologic testing for specific Shigella spp. antigens was done via an agglutination assay and identified the organism as Shigella spp. serogroup D. This was confirmed using an automated gram negative identification card (Vitek 2) which gave an excellent identification of Shigella sonnei. Presumptive identification of Shigella sonnei was made and the organism was sent to the State Department of Public Health where the identification of Shigella sonnei was confirmed. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed and identified the organism as resistant to ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. An antimicrobial gradient test strip for azithromycin was performed and resulted in a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) > 256 ug/ml (epidemiological cut-off for wild type Shigella sonnei is ≤ 16 ug/ml).

Figure 2. Growth of the organism on a MacConkey agar plate after 24 hours of incubation. Translucent colonies are seen.
Figure 3. Growth of the organism on Hektoen enteric agar plate after 24 hours of incubation. Translucent colonies are seen.
Figure 4. Triple sugar iron (TSI) agar slant of the organism after 24 hours of incubation. This slant contains glucose, sucrose, and lactose. The top portion of the slant is considered the aerobic portion due to its exposure to air and the butt of the slant is considered the anaerobic portion. Glucose fermentation is indicated by the presence of an acidic (A) reaction in the butt of the slant. Sucrose and lactose fermentation is identified by the presence of an acidic (A) reaction in the top of the slant. Shigella ferment glucose but not sucrose or lactose. Therefore, they produce a characteristic alkaline/acidic (K/A) reaction on TSI as seen here. Sodium thiosulfate is also present in this medium for the detection of hydrogen sulfide (H2S). If present, a black color forms in the slant. Shigella spp. do not produce H2S as evidenced by the lack of a black color on this slant. Finally, Shigella spp. do not produce gas so there are no bubbles seen in the TSI slant.
Figure 5. Lysine iron agar slant of the organism after 24 hours of incubation. This slant is used for the detection of lysine deaminase and lysine decarboxylase. If lysine deaminase is present, the top of the slant will turn a red color and if lysine decarboxylase is present the butt of the slant will turn a purple color. Shigella spp. are negative for both lysine deaminase and lysine decarboxylase producing a purple/yellow (K/A) slant. Sodium thiosulfate is also present in this medium for the detection of hydrogen sulfide (H2S). As stated above, Shigella spp. do not produce H2S and do not produce gas.
Treatment and Followup
He was prescribed empirical azithromycin which was ceased when he improved. Interestingly, antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed a concerning resistance profile.
Azithromycin >256 ug/mL (No interpretation)
Ciprofloxacin 4 ug/mL (Resistant)
Ampicillin ≥32 ug/mL (Resistant)
Ceftriaxone ≤1 ug/mL (Susceptible)
Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole ≥320 ug/mL (Resistant)
Discussion
Shigellosis is characterized by acute-onset diarrhea, vomiting and abdominal cramps. (1) Stools often contain blood, although some patients present with watery diarrhea without blood. Rarely, severe shigellosis may be associated with gastrointestinal complications including rectal prolapse, intestinal obstruction and colonic perforation. Reactive arthritis may also be seen rarely one to two weeks after infection.
Shigella species are highly infectious; as few as 10 organisms may be sufficient to cause disease.(2) Contact with infected individuals or contaminated food is most frequently implicated in transmission. As an emerging sexually-transmitted infection in men who have sex with men (MSM) populations, shigellosis is associated with direct or indirect oral-anal contact. (3) In patients with normal immune function the disease is generally self-limited, resolving after seven days on average; however, patients remain infectious for up to four weeks as pathogens continue to be shed in feces. As such, this is a reportable illness and documentation of stool culture clearance is required in food-handlers. (4) In other populations including healthcare workers, antibiotic therapy is recommended to reduce the duration of disease and decrease the risk of continued transmission. (5)
Shigellosis is the third most common enteric bacterial infection in the United States (6) and the most common bacterial cause of diarrheal deaths in under 5 year-olds worldwide. (7) There are four species of Shigella that cause acute gastrointestinal infection: S. dysenteriae, S. flexnerii, S. boydii and S. sonnei. (8) S. dysenteriae was historically responsible for large epidemics but is now rare. S. flexneri and S. sonnei are common globally, with the former traditionally seen in resource-limited settings and the latter in resource-rich settings however S. sonnei is now emerging in resource-limited settings too. S. sonnei, along with S. flexneri serotypes 2a and 3a, are the strains most frequently implicated in outbreaks in MSM populations. (1)
S. dysenteriae, particularly serotype 1, produces shiga toxin and is considered the most virulent species. However, shiga toxins produced by other species including E. coli can be transferred via a lambda bacteriophage to non-toxin producing Shigella species including S. sonnei. Interspecies transfer of virulence factors in S. sonnei has been reported in a number of recent outbreaks. (9, 10)
Shigella species are often intrinsically resistant to multiple antibiotics including ampicillin, trimethroprim-sulfamethoxazole and quinolones, and reduced susceptibility to azithromycin is now being reported around the world.(11) Approximately 20% of Shigella isolates tested in New York City during 2013–2015 displayed decreased susceptibility to azithromycin. (12) Targeted therapy and improved surveillance will be important factors in limiting the further spread of resistant Shigella strains.
Final Diagnosis
Shigellosis with decreased susceptibility to azithromycin
