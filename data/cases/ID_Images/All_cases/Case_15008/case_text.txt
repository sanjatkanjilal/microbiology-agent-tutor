History of Present Illness
2015 November-December Featured Case: A man in his fifties, who was from Central America, was admitted to the hospital because of progressive shortness of breath.
The patient had been well until approximately 10 years earlier, when he developed shortness of breath with strenuous activities. After several years, however, dyspnea occurred with moderate activity, such as climbing stairs. Two months before this presentation, the shortness of breath began to occur when walking more than a few blocks. His symptoms progressed during the following weeks until he was short of breath with minimal movement and when lying flat. He was no longer able to work, and came to the hospital for an evaluation.
On evaluation, he reported a cough productive of white phlegm that had been present for approximately 3 months. The phlegm occasionally contained streaks of blood, but he reported no frank hemoptysis. He had unintentional weight loss of approximately 10 pounds (4.5 kilograms) during the previous year. He did not have any chest pain, pleurisy, fevers, night sweats, or chills.
Past Medical History
The patient had previously consumed 2 pints of liquor daily for 20 years, but stopped 4 years before this presentation. At age 12 years, he had an illness characterized by 1 month of high fevers and a productive cough, which resolved without treatment. The patient had no other medical history and had not seen a physician in 30 years.
Medications
He took no medications.
Family History
His father died of lung cancer, his brother died of gastric cancer, and 2 of his sisters died of tuberculosis 3 years before this presentation. He did not know any further details of his family’s medical history.
Social History
The patient was married; his wife lived in Central America and visited him twice a year. He worked as a chef in a restaurant and lived with a friend who was also from Central America, and who had last visited the region 2 years earlier. He did not use tobacco or drugs.
Epidemiological History
The patient was born in Central America and had moved to the United States 28 years earlier; he had not been to Central America since. When he lived in Central America, he had resided in a rural area and worked on a family farm, which had chickens and goats. He was only sexually active with his wife.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared well and comfortable at rest, but became slightly short of breath when speaking. He was overweight and did not have signs of rapid weight loss. The temperature was 36.7°C (98.5°F), pulse 76 beats per minute, blood pressure 120/80 mm Hg, respirations 20 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 96% while breathing room air. There was a II/IV systolic ejection murmur at the right upper sternal border of the chest, without radiation. The examination was otherwise normal; he did not have any pulmonary findings, lower extremity edema, abdominal distention, jugular venous distention, lymphadenopathy, or clubbing of the digits.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 6,540 per cubic millimeter (reference range (ref) 3500-10,500), hematocrit 44.6% (ref 34.9-50 0 in men), and platelets 226,000 per cubic millimeter (ref 150,000-450,000). The blood level of creatinine was 1.1 milligrams per deciliter (ref 0.6-1.3); other routine laboratory tests were normal, including liver function tests. An HIV test was negative.
A posterior-anterior and lateral chest radiograph (Figure 1) revealed cardiomegaly with right atrial and right ventricular enlargement, calcified densities in the bilateral upper lungs, and possible noncalcified nodular densities in the bilateral lower lungs. Transesophageal echocardiography showed a mean pulmonary gradient of 72 millimeters of mercury (normal being less than 30), a peak velocity of 5.4 meters per second (normal, less than 3), and near-normal right ventricular function.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Computed tomography (CT) of the chest with the administration of intravenous contrast showed spiculated calcified masses in the bilateral apices, scattered calcified granulomata, extensive mediastinal calcifications, pleural and pericardial calcified plaques, and narrowing of the main pulmonary artery (Figures 2 and 3).
Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest was then obtained, which showed (in addition to the above findings) dense calcifications along the ascending aorta and proximal main pulmonary artery with severe stenosis of the main pulmonary artery (Figure 4). Three-dimensional mapping with the CTA showed focal stenosis in the distal main pulmonary artery secondary to a fibrous band encircling the mid ascending aorta and pulmonary artery (Figure 5).
Based on the patient’s characteristic chest imaging findings, the presence of calcified granulomas in his lungs and spleen (signifying a prior granulomatous infection), and his clinical and epidemiologic history, a diagnosis of fibrosing mediastinitis was made.
Due to his chronic cough, additional testing was performed, including serum galactomannan index, (1-3)-beta-D-glucan, serum histoplasma antigen, urine histoplasma antigen, serum histoplasma antibodies, and an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) blood test for detection of interferon-gamma (IFN-gamma) to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) complex antigens. Of these tests, only the ELISA IFN-gamma test for Mtb complex antigens was positive. Bacterial and fungal sputum cultures were negative, as was a sputum specimen stained for acid-fast bacilli (AFB). A nucleic acid amplification test of a sputum specimen was positive for Mtb genetic material. After approximately 2 weeks, sputum cultures grew Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Treatment and Followup
Rifampin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide, and isoniazid were administered. Cardiac catheterization showed external compression of the right pulmonary artery and no stenosis of the pulmonary valve. Due to his severe stenosis and symptoms, surgical replacement of the pulmonary artery with a pulmonary homograft was performed. Pathological examination of tissue lymph nodes showed non-caseating granulomas and extensive fibrosis; stains were negative for fungal and acid fast organisms, and examination of tissue of the pulmonary artery showed nodular calcifications and no granulomas. The patient did well postoperatively and had significant improvement in his dyspnea.
He was discharged to home 10 days after his surgery in good condition, with plans to receive directly observed tuberculosis therapy from his local health department and to follow up with infectious diseases and cardiothoracic surgery.
Discussion
Fibrosing mediastinitis is a rare process that is characterized by the proliferation of fibrous tissue in the mediastinum. The fibrosis may be diffuse and result in obstruction, compression, and/or obliteration of mediastinal structures. The disease is thought to be the result of an immune response to an inflammatory stimulus, which leads to uncontrolled collagen formation (Schade et al., 2013). It is hypothesized that host immunity plays a role in the fibrotic response; one study found the risk of fibrosing mediastinitis was three times higher in patients with HLA-A2 antigen (Schade et al., 2013; Ackman et al., 2004; Peebles et al., 2000).
The inflammatory stimulus is usually infectious in nature, with histoplasmosis being most common, followed by tuberculosis (Schade et al., 2013; Ackman et al., 2004). Tuberculosis-related fibrosing mediastinitis is often less severe than histoplasmosis-related disease, but there have been reports of extensive fibrosis (Atasoy et al., 2001). Non-infectious precipitants have also been reported, and include radiation, pulmonary hemorrhage, drugs (for example, methysergide maleate), autoimmune processes, idiopathic fibrotic disorders, and a familial variant (Ackman et al., 2004; McNeeley et al., 2012). The course of the disease is not predictable; many patients progress, but some stabilize, and others improve.
There are no established diagnostic criteria for fibrosing mediastinitis. The disease is usually suggested by imaging, with computed tomography demonstrating calcifications and vascular involvement (an exception is the idiopathic variant, which does not present with calcifications) (Ackman et al., 2004; Atasoy et al., 2001). Tissue pathology will show fibrosis, but a pathologic diagnosis may not be required if a patient has evidence of a prior granulomatous infection, epidemiologic risk factors, and consistent imaging (McNeeley et al., 2012). If the clinical presentation is less clear, tissue is often needed to rule out malignancy, but this may be technically difficult due to the involvement of vascular structures and dense fibrosis. Serologic tests for infectious processes and cultures of sputum and tissue are rarely positive (Parish et al., 2002; Peikert et al., 2011).
There is no definitive treatment for infection-related fibrosing mediastinitis. Treating the infectious precipitant in the absence of a demonstrable active infection has not been found to be efficacious, and while there are some reports of positive results with glucocorticoids, this has not been corroborated in larger case series (Schade et al., 2013; Peikert et al., 2011). Surgery is primarily indicated to relieve significant obstruction of mediastinal structures or to repair fistulas, and is most efficacious in patients with focal disease. While traditionally surgical interventions have been associated with a mortality of approximately 30%, recent data suggest outcomes have improved (Peikert et al., 2011).
Final Diagnosis
Fibrosing mediastinitis caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection.
