History of Present Illness
2012 March-April Featured Case: A woman in her twenties was admitted to the hospital because of respiratory failure and supraventricular tachycardia.
The patient was well until 12 days before admission, when throat soreness developed. Two days later, at her doctor's office, testing for rapid streptococcal antigen was negative and a heterophile antibody test was positive. She was counseled regarding supportive care for infectious mononucleosis. One week before admission, she returned to the doctor's office because of increased throat pain and decreased oral intake. A 3-day course of prednisone was administered orally. Her throat pain improved, however, neck pain persisted and her appetite decreased.
Three days before admission, she reportedly returned to the doctor's office because of increased neck pain, chest tightness, and pain in the left upper quadrant. She was afebrile. A chest radiograph reportedly showed a right upper lobe infiltrate. An abdominal ultrasound showed splenomegaly. Azithromycin was begun.
On the day of admission, her parents noted shallow breathing and took her to the emergency department of a local hospital. She reported pleuritic chest pain on the left side, which improved with upright positioning. On evaluation, the blood pressure was 121/77 mm Hg, pulse 106 beats per minute, respirations 24 per minute and oxygen saturation 80% while breathing ambient air. Oxygen was administered via non-rebreather mask. An electrocardiogram showed diffuse ST-segment elevations. A chest radiograph (Figure 1) demonstrated findings that were thought to be consistent with congestive heart failure and large bilateral pleural effusions.
The pulse rose up to 160-169 beats per minute; and supraventricular tachycardia was diagnosed. A bolus of amiodarone was administered, followed by cardioversion. Ceftriaxone (1 gram), azithromycin (500 milligrams) and aspirin (321 mg) were also given. An echocardiogram performed at the bedside by a cardiologist reportedly showed depressed left ventricular function.
She was transferred from the emergency department to a tertiary care hospital for admission to the medical intensive care unit.
Figure 1. Chest radiograph, other hospital.
Past Medical History
She had been healthy before this illness.
Medications
She had recently stopped taking oral contraceptives.
Allergies
Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole caused a rash.
Epidemiological History
She worked in a public service position. She drank alcohol in moderation and had never smoked tobacco or used illicit drugs. During the previous year, she traveled to Western Europe and the New England coast. She had recently cared for indoor, declawed cats. She had a new boyfriend. Her immediate family was healthy.
Physical Examination
On arrival in the medical intensive care unit, she appeared uncomfortable and in respiratory distress. The blood pressure was 109/64 mm Hg, pulse 120 beats per minute, temperature 99.0°F (37.2°C), respirations 36 per minute and oxygen saturation 100% while breathing oxygen via non–rebreather mask. There was a pericardial rub heard best at the upper and lower sternal border on the left side. Breath sounds were decreased at the lung bases. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 28,000 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4,500-11,000) with 90% neutrophils and 4% band forms. The blood level of sodium was 119 mmol/L (ref 135-145), total bilirubin 1.6 mg/dl (ref 0.0-1.0), direct bilirubin 1.4 mg/dl (ref 0.0-0.4), aspartate aminotransferase 53 U/L (ref 10-40), albumin 1.7 gm/dl (ref 3.3-5.0) and NT-proBNP 5444 pg/ml (ref 0-450); levels of the other electrolytes, glucose, globulin, alkaline phosphatase and alanine aminotransferase were normal.
Rapid direct fluorescent antibody testing for influenza A and B, parainfluenza 1, 2, and 3, adenovirus and respiratory syncytial viruses was negative. Gram stain of a sputum specimen showed mixed gram positive and negative flora. Cultures of the blood, urine and sputum, as well as urinary Legionella antigen, were obtained. A chest radiograph on admission (Figure 2) showed bilateral large pleural effusions, greater on the right, which appeared to be loculated.
Figure 2. Chest radiograph, on admission.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The trachea was intubated for hypoxemia and increased work of breathing immediately after arrival, and norepinephrine and vasopressin were administered. Thoracenteses were performed bilaterally, and a liter of foul-smelling fluid removed from each side.
Specimens of pleural fluid had 8400 and 287,500 nucleated cells per cubic millimeter (ref 0-1000), many of which were disintegrated; differential cell counts could not be performed. The level of glucose in the pleural fluid from each side was less than 2 mg/dl. On the right side, the pH was 6.01, the level of lactate dehydrogenase was 18,810 U/L, and albumin 1.7 gm/dl. On the left side, the level of lactate dehydrogenase was 35,040 U/L, albumin 1.8 gm/dl, total protein 3.2 gm/dl, amylase 10 U/L and triglycerides 10 mg/dl.
Cefepime (2 gm intravenously (IV) every 8 hours), metronidazole (500mg IV every 8 hours), vancomycin (1gm IV every 12 hours), levofloxacin (750mg IV daily), and oseltamivir (100mg per nasogastric tube twice daily) were administered.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Computed tomography of the chest, performed after thoracentesis, demonstrated gas in the pre-vertebral fascia (Figure 3 and, with arrows, Figure 7), gas around the thyroid gland (Figure 4), a mediastinal collection and small bilateral pleural effusions, right greater
than left, with fluid tracking into the right minor fissure (Figures 5 and 6). The jugular veins were patent.
Gram stain (Figure 8) of the pleural fluid showed short gram-positive rods, thin gram-positive rods, gram-positive coccobacilli, and gram-negative rods. The morning after admission, esophagoscopy, surgical exploration of the neck, and mediastinal washout were performed, with placement of four Jackson-Pratt drains. Gram stain of the aspirate from the neck showed short gram-positive rods, thin gram positive rods, gram positive coccobacilli, and gram negative rods (Figure 9).
Testing for IgM to Epstein Barr virus (EBV) viral capsid was positive and for IgG was negative; EBV nuclear antigen was negative. Testing for hepatitis A, B and C viruses, HIV antibody and RNA, and urine Legionella antigen was negative.
The cultures of the pleural fluid and neck aspirate grew Streptocococcus anginosus, alpha hemolytic Streptococcus, Prevotella melaninogenica of two types and Fusobacterium necrophorum. Cultures of the pleural fluid also grew Propionibacterium acnes, and the neck aspirate also grew rare Candida albicans. The antibiotics were changed to ampicillin-sulbactam and fluconazole after culture results were known.

Treatment and Followup
Three days after admission, she underwent bronchoscopy, bilateral video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery converted to open thoracotomy, drainage of mediastinal abscess, bilateral decortication and drainage of a serous pericardial effusion; four additional chest tubes were placed. The patient's hospital course was complicated by pancreatitis, hyperthyroidism and thyroid storm, thought to be secondary to necrotizing infection.
Ten days after admission, the trachea was extubated; however, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response and associated hypotension developed, which resolved with cardioversion.
Eleven days after admission, tracheostomy was performed. The next day, pressors were discontinued. She was discharged on the 39th day to a rehabilitation facility, and one month later, to home, with some persistent vocal cord and swallowing dysfunction.
Discussion
This patient had infectious mononucleosis caused by acute Epstein Barr virus infection, complicated by polymicrobial infection in the neck and chest, including pre-vertebral and retropharyngeal abscesses and bilateral pleural empyemas, and mediastinitis.
The majority of patients with infectious mononucleosis recover without apparent sequelae [Luzuriaga and Sullivan 2010]. The respiratory complications of infectious mononucleosis include upper airway obstruction from lymphoid hyperplasia, mucosal edema or pharyngeal abscess, bilateral hilar adenopathy, exudative pleural effusions, interstitial pneumonitis, superimposed bacterial pneumonia, and septic thrombophlebitis. Pleural empyema after infectious mononucleosis has been reported [Andrianakis et al., 2002; Kopec et al., 1997], but is rare.
Pleural empyema most often develops by direct extension from contiguous infections such as pneumonia, osteomyelitis, mediastinal infection or subdiaphragmatic abscess. Empyemas can also develop from direct implantation such as after trauma or surgery. Most empyemas are unilateral; bilateral empyemas most often result from hematogenous spread or from mediastinitis [Kopec et al., 1997]. Peptostreptococcus, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, and Fusobacterium nucleatum are the most common pathogens of anaerobic pulmonary and orodental infection [Bartlett 1993].
The role of steroids in infectious mononucleosis has been evaluated in multiple studies. In the 1950s, a small case series documented favorable effects of steroid administration on the clinical course of infectious mononucleosis, particularly in shortening symptoms. In the 1960s, the routine practice of administering steroids became controversial [Candy and Hotopf, 2006]and concerns were raised that the effect of steroids on T cell immunity may predispose to secondary infections [Hagelskjaer Kristensen and Prag, 2000; Matten and Grecu, 2006]. A Cochrane Review published in 2006 found insufficient evidence to support the use of steroids in patients with infectious mononucleosis [Candy and Hotopf, 2006]. This evaluation of seven studies was limited by the heterogeneity of the trials, including diagnostic criteria, corticosteroid regimens, and symptom assessment scales. Two of seven of the trials found a transient benefit that was not sustained at follow up. Some, but not all, studies found increased complications in the steroid recipients; one study reported a patient who developed peritonsillar abscess. The authors of a review in the New England Journal of Medicine, published in 2010, concluded that "Clinical experience suggests that corticosteroids may be helpful in the management of more severe complications of infectious mononucleosis, including upper-airway obstruction, hemolytic anemia, and thrombocytopenia, although randomized, clinical trials evaluating their efficacy are limited.” [Luziaraga and Sullivan 2010].
Final Diagnosis
Infectious mononucleosis caused by acute Epstein Barr virus infection, complicated by polymicrobial infections in the neck and chest, including pre-vertebral and retropharyngeal abscesses and bilateral pleural empyemas, and mediastinitis. Streptocococcus anginosus, alpha hemolytic Streptococcus, Prevotella melaninogenica, Fusobacterium necrophorum, Propionibacterium acnes, and Candida albicans were cultured.
