History of Present Illness
2008 September Featured Case and Medical Student Curriculum Case Summary: A woman in her thirties with fevers and a pruritic rash.
A woman in her thirties was well until approximately 3 days before presentation in mid-autumn, when fevers up to 103°F (39.4°C) developed, associated with a sore throat and malaise. She took ibuprofen for her symptoms. On the day of presentation, she awoke with a rash over her face, scalp, trunk, and proximal extremities. The lesions were pruritic and tender, and associated with headache and nausea. There was no cough, dysuria, vaginal discharge, or chest, abdomen, or joint pain. She was seen in a walk-in clinic, and referred to an emergency department for evaluation by an infectious disease consultant.
Past Medical History
She reported having had chickenpox as a child and was up-to-date on her immunizations.
Medications
She had taken ibuprofen for her symptoms.
Epidemiological History
She worked in a healthcare setting, and had no pets or known sick contacts. She traveled intermittently to a wooded area in Northern New England to visit relatives. There was no history of any unusual skin contacts, or tick or other insect bites. She did not smoke tobacco, drink alcohol, or use illicit drugs. Her last sexual contact was 5 months earlier, and she reported using condoms, without exception, whenever sexually active.
Physical Examination
The patient appeared fatigued and uncomfortable. The temperature was 96.6°F (35.9°C), blood pressure 133/77 mm Hg, pulse 96 beats per minute, and respirations 20 breaths per minute, with an oxygen saturation of 97% while breathing ambient air. There were numerous discrete, tender, erythematous macules, less than 1 centimeter in diameter with a 1-to-3 millimeter pustular center, on the face, scalp, chest, back, inner thighs, and arms, and one lesion on her palm. The physical examination was otherwise normal.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 6,300 cells/mm³, with 35% neutrophils, 35% lymphocytes, 21% atypical lymphocytes, 6% monocytes, 2% eosinophils, and 1% basophils. A Wright stain of the blood smear is shown in Figures 1 and 2. The remainder of the complete blood count analysis, and levels of electrolytes and renal function, were normal. Additional tests were pending.

Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
The patient was discharged to home to await the remaining test results, with further close follow-up.
She felt worse the following day, with increasing headache and skin lesions. On examination, the temperature was normal; the neck was supple, and examination of the skin revealed approximately 30 pustules on an erythematous base. The remainder of the examination was unchanged from the previous day. She was admitted to the hospital.
Additional test results included an alkaline phosphatase of 154 U/L (reference range 30-100 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase 42 U/L (reference range 9-32 U/L), alanine aminotransferase 47 U/L (reference range 7-30 U/L), and total bilirubin 0.3 mg/dl.
A lumbar puncture was performed, and analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid showed no white blood cells and normal levels of glucose and protein. A chest radiograph and computed tomography of the brain were also normal. An ultrasound of the abdomen showed diffuse fatty infiltration of the liver and the spleen size was normal. The patient was seen by the dermatology service and a skin biopsy was performed.
Acyclovir was administered empirically. Over the next two days, some lesions evolved to become crusted with a central eschar (Figures 3 and 4).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
After the initial history of chickenpox in childhood was elicited, the patient’s mother was contacted and reported that the patient had not had the disease. The varicella immunoglobulin (Ig) G antibody test was negative. Bacterial, fungal, mycobacterial, and viral cultures of the skin biopsy were negative. A direct fluorescent antibody stain of the tissue showed the presence of varicella zoster virus (VZV) antigens (Figure 5).
Additional testing for heterophile antibodies, blood cytomegalovirus (CMV) antigenemia and immunoglobin G antibodies, rapid plasma reagin (RPR) for syphilis, Lyme antibodies, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody and ribonucleic acid (RNA) was negative. Epstein Barr virus (EBV) antibody titers were positive, and consistent with past infection, and IgG antibodies to rubella and measles were also positive. Cultures of the blood, urine, throat, and cerebrospinal fluid were sterile, and VZV was not detected in the CSF by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing.

Treatment and Followup
The patient was initially treated with acyclovir until it was confirmed that she had no evidence of pneumonia, central nervous system involvement, or immunosuppression. The levels of liver function tests rose to approximately double their normal values during the first days of her admission, then declined, and the atypical lymphocytosis resolved. She improved clinically and was discharged on the 5th hospital day.
Discussion
The typical presentation of chickenpox, or primary varicella zoster virus (VZV) infection, includes a prodrome of fever, malaise and, frequently, pharyngitis. The rash usually occurs within 24 hours of the onset of symptoms and is classically described as vesicular lesions on an erythematous base. These lesions are present in a variety of stages from maculopapular to vesicular to crusted.
This patient's presentation was atypical in several ways, and led to the broad differential diagnosis. First, fewer than 10% of chickenpox cases occur in those over the age of 14 years. Secondly, while a pustular-appearing rash can occur in chickenpox (as in this case), the classic findings are of vesicles. Finally, the patient reported that she had had chickenpox as a child. After further inquiry, her mother corrected this history and indicated that the patient had not had the disease. A negative varicella immunoglobulin (Ig) G antibody confirmed that the patient had not been exposed in the past.
Atypical lymphocytes can have several features including increased nuclear granularity, elongated nucleus, vacuolated cytoplasm, and an irregular border that tends to flow around surrounding red blood cells (Figures 1 and 2, above). They are found in various acute viral infections including those resulting from VZV, EBV, CMV, HIV, and hepatitis viruses. They may also be found in other infections such as acute toxoplasmosis, as well as in certain drug reactions, e.g., those secondary to phenytoin.
Adults make up a small percentage of chickenpox cases, but have a high rate of complications and account for more than half of varicella-related deaths. Complications may include bacterial superinfection, encephalitis, pneumonia, and hepatitis. As no baseline liver function tests were available, the patient’s transaminase elevations may well have been a result of the varicella. Subclinical transaminase elevations are common in normal hosts, but hepatitis may be life-threatening in immunosuppressed hosts.
A far more common manifestation of VZV infection seen in adults is the development of herpes zoster, caused by the reactivation of the virus. This is in part due to the waning of cell-mediated immunity to VZV over time. The Shingles Prevention Study, published in 2005, showed that a live attenuated VZV vaccine was able to reduce the incidence of herpes zoster by 51% and postherpetic neuralgia by 67%. The zoster vaccine is now recommended for all individuals aged 60 years and older with the exception of those who are immunocompromised.
Final Diagnosis
Primary varicella zoster virus infection (chickenpox) in an adult.
