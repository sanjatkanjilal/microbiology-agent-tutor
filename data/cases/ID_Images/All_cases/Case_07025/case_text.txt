History of Present Illness
IDSA 2007 Fellows Day Case Submission Summary: A woman with acute myelogenous leukemia, neutropenia and fevers.
A woman in her fifties with acute myelogenous leukemia became neutropenic after receiving the first cycle of chemotherapy (idarubicin and cytarabine). Fevers to 38.8°C (101.8°F) and chills occurred, associated with rhinorrhea and a mild headache. An infectious disease consult was obtained.
Past Medical History
There was a history of acute myelogenous leukemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and seasonal allergies with chronic sinusitis.
Epidemiological History
The patient lived in the Southern United States with her husband, worked in an office and did not use tobacco, alcohol, or drugs. She had not traveled recently and had no pets.
Physical Examination
The temperature was 38.7°C (101.7°F), blood pressure 155/80 mm Hg, pulse 98 beats per minute, respirations 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 98% while breathing ambient air. The patient appeared comfortable. There was clear nasal discharge but no sinus tenderness. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 500 cells/mm³, hemoglobin 8.4 g/dl, and platelet count 4000 cells/mm³. Results of a serum chemistry panel were normal. Cultures of the blood and urine were sterile. A chest radiograph was normal. Computed tomography (CT) of the head showed moderate mucosal thickening of the maxillary, ethmoid, and sphenoid sinuses on the left (Figure 1).
Figure 1. CT of the head.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
Sinusitis was diagnosed, and surgical debridement of the affected sinuses performed. Intraoperatively, specimens were sent for culture and pathological examination. Pathological evaluation of the sinus tissues revealed tissue necrosis and fungal hyphae with invasion of the lamina propria of the respiratory mucosa. (See Figures 2 through 5.) Amphotericin B was initiated pending the results of the cultures.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Fungal tissue cultures of the sinuses obtained intraoperatively showed Curvularia, a dematiaceous filamentous fungus (see Figures 6 and 7).
Figure 6. Fungal culture of biopsy specimen.
Figure 7. Fungal culture.
Treatment and Followup
Amphotericin B was administered for 14 days, followed by therapy with voriconazole. The fevers resolved and her symptoms of headache and rhinorrhea improved. On follow-up endoscopic evaluation by the Otolaryngology service, there was no evidence of recurrence of infection. The neutropenia resolved, and she was discharged from the hospital in good condition. Another cycle of chemotherapy was administered, without evidence of recurrence of fungal sinusitis. She remained on suppressive therapy with voriconazole.
Discussion
Invasive fungal sinusitis is usually seen in immunosuppressed patients, including those with hematologic malignancies, bone marrow transplants, chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, solid organ transplants, advanced HIV disease, and diabetes mellitus. It may be due to invasion of fungi, which have chronically colonized the sinuses in patients with a history of chronic sinusitis or anatomic abnormalities of the sinuses. The fungi establish local infection and then invade tissues. The disease may be rapidly progressive depending on the degree of immunosuppression. Spread is by direct extension but fungemia with metastatic spread can occur.
Some of the fungi implicated in invasive fungal sinusitis in immunosuppressed patients are listed in the differential diagnosis section above. Clinical presentation may include fever, facial pain, nasal congestion, epistaxis, or visual disturbances. A non-specific diagnosis of sinusitis may be made by imaging studies (such as computed tomography) but prompt surgical evaluation is necessary to establish the diagnosis of fungal sinusitis as well as to obtain culture data. Treatment is urgent surgical debridement.
Amphotericin B is often used for initial therapy, followed by continued therapy with an agent appropriate for the organism. Intravenous therapy is generally continued until clinical response or reversal of immunocompromising conditions in achieved. Chronic suppressive therapy may be employed thereafter. Curvularia is a dematiaceous filamentous fungus with a dark cell wall. It can cause sinusitis, brain abscesses, and cutaneous disease in both humans and animals. Limited data is available on management of this organism and there is no standard method for in-vitro susceptibility testing of
Curvularia species. In-vitro data showed that Amphotericin B, ketoconazole, miconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole have low minimum inhibitory concentrations for Curvularia, so these agents are usually employed for treatment of this organism.
Final Diagnosis
Invasive fungal sinusitis caused by Curvularia, a dematiaceous filamentous fungus, in an immunocompromised, neutropenic patient.
