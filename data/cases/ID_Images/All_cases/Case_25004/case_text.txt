History of Present Illness
A male in his early 40s presented to urgent care with a violaceous ulcerative lesion on the dorsum of the left hand, present for one week. Initially presumed to be an insect bite, the lesion progressed to ulceration with surrounding erythema (Figure 1); treatment with a 5 day course of cephalexin and topical antibiotics provided no improvement. Subsequently he presented to his primary care provider with persistence of the lesion and localized pain. Doxycycline was initiated for 10 days, again with no improvement. Roughly three weeks after the initial presentation, dermatologic evaluation noted a healing violaceous plaque at the original site (Figure 2A) and a new pustular lesion distal to it (Figure 2B), along with a tender, enlarging subcutaneous nodule on the medial left elbow. Past Medical History
Hyperlipidemia.
Epidemiological History
The patient resides in New England and works in sales. Within a month prior to symptom onset, he traveled to the American Southwest. His spouse owns exotic pets, including a bearded dragon, a leopard gecko, and two ferrets, both of which died unexpectedly. He was responsible for cleaning their enclosure and burying the deceased animals.

Studies
Dermatology consultation was pursued, and punch biopsies of the dorsal hand and medial elbow lesions were performed, with specimens sent for culture and histopathologic evaluation. Wound swabs were also collected for herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella zoster virus (VZV), and Mpox testing.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Hand radiographs showed mild soft tissue swelling without evidence of fracture or foreign body. Bacterial cultures demonstrated no growth. Histopathology of the hand lesion revealed a mixed lymphohistiocytic and neutrophilic infiltrate with rare acid-fast bacilli identified on Fite and AFB stains. The elbow biopsy showed granulomatous inflammation, consistent with a ruptured follicular process. AFB cultures were negative; however, PCR testing confirmed the presence of Mycobacterium marinum DNA via hsp65 probe. PCR testing for HSV, VZV, and Mpox was initially ordered but canceled following the biopsy findings. Laboratory studies, including Complete Blood Count, Comprehensive Metabolic Panel, Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), ANCA, SPEP, and IFE, and HIV were within normal limits. A QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was positive, prompting referral to Infectious Diseases. He was evaluated by Infectious Diseases approximately six weeks after symptom onset.
Treatment and Followup
Initial empiric therapy following biopsy results included ciprofloxacin, azithromycin, and ethambutol. After PCR confirmation, the regimen was narrowed to doxycycline, azithromycin, and continued ethambutol. Surgical debridement was not required. At 11 weeks, re-epithelialization and resolution of nodules were noted. Treatment was concluded based on the clinical appearance and resolution of the lesions, and the patient was advised to continue close monitoring.
Discussion
The patient’s ulcerative lesion and lack of response to multiple courses of antibiotics prompted consideration of both non-infectious and infectious etiologies. Among non-infectious causes, pyoderma gangrenosum was considered due to its characteristic painful ulcers with undermined borders. Primary cutaneous B-cell lymphoma was also on the differential, as it can present with solitary or multiple skin lesions and requires histopathologic and immunohistochemical analysis for diagnosis. Infectious causes were also explored. Sporothrix schenckii, typically associated with traumatic inoculation, can present with nodular lymphangitis and was a plausible consideration.
Cutaneous tuberculosis was considered due to the violaceous ulceration, surrounding erythema, and lymphatic spread. Nocardia species, particularly N. brasiliensis, can cause ulcerative skin lesions and pustules; however, this is more common in immunocompromised individuals, which did not fit this patient's immune profile. Cutaneous leishmaniasis was considered, given its potential to cause chronic ulcerative lesions with indurated borders, though less likely without relevant endemic travel.
Lastly, Bacillus anthracis was included in the differential due to its ability to cause ulcerative lesions with prominent edema; confirmation would require lesion culture and PCR testing. PCR testing ultimately confirmed Mycobacterium marinum infection.
Notably, AFB cultures failed to yield growth, likely due to the organism’s distinct temperature requirements. While standard AFB cultures are incubated at 35–37°C, M. marinum grows optimally at approximately 30°C, and may not grow at typical incubation temperatures—leading to potential false-negative results. This underscores the importance of clinical suspicion and appropriate laboratory conditions when M. marinum is part of the differential diagnosis.
Although commonly associated with aquatic exposure, M. marinum infections can also arise in the absence of such contact; exotic pets (iguanas and African pygmy hedgehog) are an underrecognized potential source. The incubation period is variable and may be prolonged—ranging from 3 weeks to 9 months—making exposure history difficult to pinpoint. Additionally, false-positive QuantiFERON-TB Gold results may occur due to shared RD1 antigens with M. tuberculosis. There is no standardized treatment regimen for M. marinum; prolonged combination antibiotic therapy is often required. Surgical intervention should be tailored to the individual patient, though early clinical improvement may permit conservative, non-surgical management. This patient’s likely source of infection was his pet ferrets who had died a few weeks prior. Ferrets are known to be a species more innately susceptible to mycobacterial infections and have been used as animal models in studying these infections.
Final Diagnosis
Cutaneous Mycobacterium marinum infection with sporotrichoid spread.
