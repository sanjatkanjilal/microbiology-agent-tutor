History of Present Illness
The patient is a Gravida 3, Para 2 woman in her thirties with a 4 month history of abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding. Her menstrual cycle was previously regular at 28 days, albeit with heavy flow. She first noted lower abdominal pain 4 months prior to admission and presented to primary care. There she was diagnosed with a urinary tract infection and treated with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 3 days with some improvement in her pain. A month later her pain returned and she was again seen in primary care. At that visit it was noted that she had an intrauterine device in place, which had been placed 5 years earlier. She was referred to Obstetrics/Gynecology for intrauterine device removal. After removal she had relief of her abdominal pain and was placed on an oral contraceptive pill.
One month following removal of her intrauterine device she began passing blood clots followed by heavy menstrual bleeding. She was seen in Obstetrics/Gynecology clinic where her cervical exam was notable for purulent discharge. Fluid was obtained for culture. She underwent a pelvic ultrasound, which showed heterogenous and asymmetrically thickened myometrium suggesting adenomyosis. She underwent a cervical pap smear, which was non-diagnostic and showed only blood and inflammation. The next week she called reporting increased abdominal pain limiting her ability to ambulate. She was directly admitted to the hospital for an expedited workup.
She described her pain as 9/10 with movement, but 0/10 when stationary. It was located around her umbilicus without radiation. She reported a loss of appetite and a 12lb (5.4kg) weight loss over the prior 4 months. She had no fevers, chills, rigors, or night sweats. She had intermittent burning with urination, but no increased frequency.
Past Medical History
Urinary tract infections
High grade squamous intraepithelial lesion with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia II/III with loop electrode excision 6 years prior
Medications
Norgestrel-ethinyl estradiol
Epidemiological History
She has a family history of breast cancer and is monogamous with her male partner for more than a decade. She does not use condoms. She endorses anogenital and urogenital sexual practices.
Physical Examination
Vital signs: temperature 98.6°F (37°C), heart rate 103 beats per minute, blood pressure 94/68 mmHg, respiratory rate 20 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation 98% on ambient air.
Abdomen: not distended, active bowel sounds, tenderness in the left lower quadrant and around the umbilicus with a firm mass palpated just below the umbilicus with focal warmth.
Vaginal: yellow mucopurulent discharge, thickened appearing cervix, no obvious mass.
The rest of her examination was unremarkable.
Studies
The white blood cell count was increased at 14.78 x10³ cells/uL (reference range: 4-10) with 81.3% neutrophils (RR 48-76%). A urine HCG was negative. Her urinalysis had 1+ white blood cells with 17 cells per high powered field on microscopy (normal 0-2 cells per high powered field). An antibody test to HIV-1 and -2 was negative.
Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Endometrial biopsy and endometrial curette showed acute and chronic endometritis with necrosis and ulceration. There was no viral cytopathic effect but small organisms were seen. Actinomyces colonies were present; Methanamine silver stain and gram stain were positive (Figures 1-3).
Pelvic MRI showed a mass involving the full length and near total circumference of the cervix, concerning for cervical malignancy, with multiple prominent lymph nodes bilaterally.

Treatment and Followup
With evidence of Actinomyces on biopsy and cervical culture, she was started on penicillin G 4 million units IV every 4 hours. Her abdominal pain quickly improved while on antibiotic therapy and she was discharged home. She followed up in Infectious Disease clinic 1 month later and her pain had completely resolved. A repeat MRI at that time showed marked interval improvement in the inflammatory changes previously seen, now with only residual edema in the anterior abdomen, pelvic periuterine soft tissues and cervix; her inguinal, external iliac and mesorectal lymphadenopathy persisted. After 6 weeks of intravenous therapy she was transitioned to amoxicillin 1 gram twice daily and probenecid 500 milligrams twice daily. Six months later her probenecid was discontinued, but she remained on amoxicillin 1 gram twice daily. After 12 months of treatment a repeat pelvic MRI was obtained that showed mild residual changes from her prior pelvic infection with no evidence of active suppurative actinomycoses. Her amoxicillin was discontinued.
Discussion
At the time of initial consultation we were aware of the endometrial biopsy results with evidence of acute and chronic endometritis with Actinomyces colonies present. Despite this, we thought that it was important to rule out other causes of endometritis, cervicitis and urethritis and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) while starting treatment for Actinomyces. PID is a spectrum of inflammatory disorders of the upper female genital tract, including any combination of endometritis, salpingitis, tubo-ovarian abscess and pelvic peritonitis. Acute PID (symptoms < 30 days) is often due to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis and Mycoplasma genitalium, and can be polymicrobial with enteric pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Bacteroides fragilis, Campylobacter spp. and group B streptococci. STIs account for over 85% of all acute presentations of PID.[1] Chronic pelvic inflammatory disease can be caused by indolent and difficult to culture organisms, like Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Actinomyces spp.[1] Her vaginal swab was negative for Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis PCR and urine culture was negative.
Actinomyces is an anaerobic, gram-positive organism that is known to colonize the female genital tract and can often colonize intrauterine devices.[2] The initial description of Actinomyces causing an ascending pelvic infection was published in 1895, with additional reports over the next 100 years describing infections in the setting of intrauterine foreign bodies.[3] Despite this association, diagnosis of Actinomyces pelvic infection is difficult. Actinomyces -like organisms can be seen on Pap tests in up to 7% of women with intrauterine devices, but true infection and abscess formation is rare.[4] When an abscess forms, the radiographic appearance can often be mistaken for malignancy, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulitis, or appendicitis.[5] Actinomyces is an indolent organism and, thus, many women may not have symptoms of infection, but will instead present with an abdominal mass that may mimic malignancy.[6] Therefore, a combination of symptoms, radiographic appearance, culture and immunohistochemistry are used to make the diagnosis of Actinomyces pelvic infection.
Intrauterine device removal is critical in management of Actinomyces pelvic infections; surgery is reserved for extensive disease, given the risk of infertility.[6] Antimicrobial therapy with a beta-lactam is considered the mainstay of treatment for Actinomyces pelvic infections and usually includes an induction with intravenous therapy followed by prolonged oral treatment.
Final Diagnosis
Acute and chronic endometritis due to Actinomyces spp. in association with an intra-uterine device.
