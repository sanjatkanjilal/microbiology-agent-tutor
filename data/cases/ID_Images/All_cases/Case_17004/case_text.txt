History of Present Illness
A woman in her fifties from Cameroon who intermittently visits family in the United States presented to the Emergency Department, 5 days after arrival, with acute hepatic failure with grade 3 hepatic encephalopathy and an international normalized ratio (INR) of 8.6. The acute hepatic failure was initially attributed to either an antimalarial agent presumably dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (which she had recently received for malaria prophylaxis) or a single dose of commercial Moringa tea, and she was managed and intubated in intensive care. Six days after admission, the primary service noted that she had a rising absolute eosinophil count to 2,200 cells per mcL (19.8%) and consulted the Infectious Diseases service.
Past Medical History
Hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c of 8%), and uncomplicated malaria treated with an unspecified antimalarial agent taken 4 months prior to admission.
Medications
Amlodipine, valsartan, and sitaglipin. She had also recently received a 3 day course of an antimalarial agent (presumably dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine) for malaria prophylaxis, which she had completed 7 days before admission. She drank Moringa tea.
Epidemiological History
Born and raised in Cameroon. She denied any smoking, alcohol, or illicit drug use.
Physical Examination
The patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. She remained intubated and mildly sedated. She was jaundiced and her sclera were icteric. No conjunctival lesions. No lower extremity edema bilaterally. The remainder of the exam was unremarkable.
Studies
The leukocyte count was 11,200 cells per mcL (reference range 4,500 to 10,500) with 53% neutrophils, 11.5% lymphocytes, 14.6% monocytes, and 19.8% eosinophils. The absolute eosinophil count was 2200 cells per mcL (reference range <500). The hematocrit (27.6) and platelet count (123) were abnormally low but stable. The creatinine, alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin were elevated but decreasing. No ova or parasites were seen on three separate stool specimens.
She was HAV and HBV immune; HCV antibody negative; HDV IgM and IgG negative; Schistosoma IgG negative; Strongyloides IgG negative; Toxocara IgG positive; HIV negative. Her stools were negative for ova and parasites.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
The peripheral smear prepared using Knott’s technique revealed a sheathed, 280 micrometer microfilaria with nuclei at the tip of its tapered tail consistent with Loa loa. The microfilarial load was significantly less than 8,000 microfilariae (MF) per mL. The serologic test (OV16 IgG antibody) performed by the National Institutes of Health to evaluate for co-infection with Onchocerca volvulus was negative.
Treatment and Followup
After the patient’s hepatic abnormalities resolved completely, she received a 21 day course of diethylcarbamazine (DEC). Nine days after completing the course of DEC, her peripheral eosinophilia had resolved. She did complain of transient urticaria but a repeat peripheral smear prepared using Knott’s technique was negative for any microfilariae.
Discussion
The identification of the filarial nematodes requires knowledge of their geographic distribution, clinical presentation, and the microscopic appearance of their progeny, the microfilariae, in peripheral smears. The most commonly encountered agent of lymphatic filariasis (LF), Wuchereria bancrofti, is endemic to tropical areas throughout the world. In contrast, the geographic distribution of the two other agents of LF is more limited: Brugia malayi is endemic to Asia including the Indian subcontinent and Brugia timori is endemic to Indonesia. The other three genera of filarial nematodes, namely Loa, Mansonella, and Onchocerca, share a tropism for subcutaneous and neuro-ocular tissues and if symptomatic, result in localized subcutaneous angioedema and varying degrees of ocular impairment and encephalitis. In addition, these three genera of filarial nematodes also share a geographic distribution and are endemic to central and western Africa. The only exception is Mansonella ozzardi, which is endemic to Latin America.
In regard to the appearance of the microfilariae in peripheral smears, there are three elements to consider: (1) their size, (2) the presence or absence of a sheath, and (3) the morphology of their tail. In general, the microfilariae of Brugia, Loa, and Wuchereria are sheathed whereas the microfilariae of Mansonella and Onchocerca are unsheathed. The sheath is not always easily visible though. For the question accompanying this case: Options (1) B. malayi and (3) M. ozzardi can be discarded immediately because of their geographic distribution. Options (4) Mansonella perstans and (5) Onchocerca volvulus can also be discarded because they are unsheathed and because the size of their microfilariae are different than that of Loa loa measuring 180-225 µm and 300-315 µm, respectively. Furthermore, the tail of L. loa is tapered whereas the tail of M. perstans is blunt and the tail of O. volvulus is frequently bent at a sharp angle.
Loiasis or “African eye worm” is a neglected tropical disease caused by the filarial nematode Loa loa. The first case of Loiasis was reported in 1770 by the French surgeon Mongin who observed subconjunctival migration of adult Loa loa, a symptom termed “eye worm,” in a West African slave in Santo Domingo. In 1895, the Scottish ophthalmologist Douglas Argyll-Robertson first described transient localized cutaneous angioedema, or “Calabar swellings,” caused by the migration of adult Loa loa through the subcutaneous tissues of several patients in Calabar, Nigeria. [Grutzig 1977, van Boagert 1955].
The vector of Loiasis, the Chrysops fly, was identified in 1912 by the British parasitologist Robert Thompson Leiper. Larvae develop in the fly and are transmitted to humans during a blood meal. Adults mature over 6 months and migrate to the subconjunctiva and subcutaneous tissues. Microfilariae are released into the bloodstream during the day because this is when the Chrysops fly feeds. Since the vector of Loa loa is only found in the tropical rainforests of central and western Africa, Loiasis is confined to this region. It is particularly common in Cameroon, the Central African Republic, Congo, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Equatorial Guinea, and Gabon. The prevalence of Loiasis in endemic areas is 40% [Zouré 2011]. In a survey of 271 patients presenting to GeoSentinel sites with filarial infections, 25% had Loiasis [Saito 2015].
The clinical manifestations of Loiasis differ between travelers and migrants. Travelers are more likely to develop eye worm and migrants are more likely to develop Calabar swellings and urticaria. Peripheral eosinophilia is common among both groups and is present in up to 86% of cases. These symptoms may worsen during treatment [Saito 2015]. Fatal encephalitis is a well-documented phenomenon in patients with very high microfilarial loads receiving antifilarial therapy [Boussinesq 1998, Carme 1991, Gadon 1997, van Boagert 1955].
Diagnosis is made by visualization of adult worms in the subconjunctiva or microfilariae in peripheral blood smears. Adults measure 30-40 mm (male) or 40-70 mm (female). The cuticle (outer layer) is ridgeless but has irregularly spaced protrusions called “bosses.” Microfilariae are sheathed and measure 230-250 µm (Giemsa-stained smears) or 270-300 µm (smears prepared using Knott’s technique). The tail is tapered and contains nuclei [cdc.gov/dpdx]. Giemsa-stained smears are used for screening for microfilariae and, if calibrated, for quantifying the microfilarial load. For increased sensitivity, concentration techniques such as centrifugation and blood lysis in 2% formalin (Knott’s technique) may be used. The optimal time of blood specimen collection is 10:00 to 16:00 [Boussinesq 2012].
The recommended treatment for symptomatic Loiasis is DEC orally for 21 days. DEC is the only agent that is efficacious against adult worms and microfilariae. In contrast, the activity of albendazole (ALB) and ivermectin (IVM) are limited to microfilariae. Recrudescence is common and up to 50% of patients require repeated courses of treatment. Prior to starting DEC, 2 conditions must be met: First, the administration of DEC (and IVM) leads to a rapid decline in the microfilarial load. The rapidly dying microfilariae precipitate an inflammatory response that may result in fatal encephalitis, particularly in patients with microfilarial loads in the tens of thousands [Boussinesq 1998, Carme 1991, Gadon 1997, van Boagert 1955]. Thus, the microfilarial load must be decreased to less than 8,000 MF per mL prior to starting DEC. This may be done by the administration of ALB orally for 21 days or apheresis. ALB has an embryotoxic effect on adult Loa loa and thus decreases the microfilarial load more slowly than DEC or IVM [Boussinesq 2012]. Second, co-infection with Onchocerca volvulus must be ruled-out. Onchocerca volvulus is the filarial nematode that causes “African river blindness.” It shares the same geographic distribution of Loa loa and has similar clinical manifestations [Zouré 2014]. Treatment of Onchocerca volvulus with DEC may precipitate a Mazzottii reaction, an inflammatory reaction caused by rapidly dying microfilariae that results in blindness and cardiopulmonary collapse [Awadzi 1992].

Loaisis
