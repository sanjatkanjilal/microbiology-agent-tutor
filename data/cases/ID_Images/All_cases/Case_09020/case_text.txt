History of Present Illness
2009 IDSA Pediatric Fellows' Day Case Presentation Summary: A teenaged girl, receiving chemotherapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia, presented with nodules on her extremities.
A teenaged girl was diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia three weeks before this presentation. Induction chemotherapy, which included vincristine, daunorubicin, prednisone, pegylated-asparaginase, intrathecal methotrexate and Ara-C, was administered.
Two weeks before admission, neutropenia developed. On the last day of induction chemotherapy, she was seen in the Hematology Oncology Clinic because of “painful red bumps” on her left shoulder and right knee, of one day's duration. The nodules had initially appeared like a “bug bite”, and later became more hyperpigmented with an erythematous base. One day after the clinic visit, the temperature rose to 100°F (37.8°C). She reported pain in the back, bilateral elbows and legs, for which she was taking multiple pain medications, as well as generalized malaise and mild nasal congestion. There was no history of cough, vomiting, diarrhea, headaches, night sweats, chills or extremity weakness. The number of lesions had progressively increased, and involved her back and all extremities.
She was admitted to the hospital and broad-spectrum antibiotics, including vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam and gentamicin, were administered.
Past Medical History
A left ovarian cyst, which was asymptomatic, had been diagnosed incidentally by computed tomography (CT) of her abdomen during evaluation for leukemia.
Medications
Recent chemotherapy is noted above. She was also on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis, and had received vancomycin, gentamicin and piperacillin-tazobactam while in the hospital.
Physical Examination
She appeared tired. The temperature was 97.2°F (36.2°C), blood pressure 115/65 mm Hg, pulse 80 beats per minute and respirations 15 breaths per minute, with an oxygen saturation of 100% while breathing ambient air. The mucous membranes were moist, with no evidence of mucositis. The nares revealed clear nasal drainage with blackish necrotic areas on the nasal septum and turbinates. There was an erythematous painful papule (2 mm in diameter) on the scalp. There were multiple small erythematous papular lesions over the right knee, left thigh, right shoulder, right arm and back, most measuring approximately 5-6 mm in diameter, some with hyperpigmented centers, erythematous bases, or early ulcerations (Figure 1). All lesions were tender to palpation. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Figure 1. Skin lesion.
Studies
The white blood cell count was 1900 cells/mm³ (reference range (ref) 4500-13,500), with neutrophils 2.5% and lymphocytes 97%), hemoglobin 9.3 g/dL (ref 13.0-16.0) and platelets 34,000 cells/mm³ (ref 150,000-450,000). Tests of liver function, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase, and gamma glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), and of renal function were normal.
Clinical Course Prior to Diagnosis
A lesion on the left thigh was biopsied, and the tissue cultured; micropathological examination of the tissue is shown in Figure 2. Cultures of the blood, obtained from a peripherally-inserted central catheter, and urine were sterile.
Computed tomography (CT) of her sinuses, chest (Figure 3), abdomen (Figure 4) and pelvis, with and without contrast, revealed pansinusitis, pulmonary and renal nodules. An ophthalmological examination revealed endophthalmitis and a vitreal lesion in the left eye (Figure 5).

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Histopathological examination of the skin biopsy demonstrated angioinvasive filamentous fungi, which were subsequently identified as Fusarium spp (Figure 2). Computed tomography (CT) revealed nodules consistent with disseminated fusariosis (Figures 3 and 4) and ophthalmological examination revealed a vitreal fungal lesion (Figure 5) in the left eye.
Treatment and Followup
Amphotericin B was initially started, and voriconazole added to the regimen. The fungal isolate was sent for antifungal susceptibility testing and found to be sensitive only to amphotericin B with mean inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 0.5 to 1. There were necrotic lesions on the inferior turbinates including the right and left anterior nasal septum (Figure 6), and four debridement procedures of the sinuses were performed. Two intravitreal injections of amphotericin B and voriconazole were followed by vitrectomy. The clinical symptoms and cutaneous, sinus, renal, and pulmonary lesions improved.

Discussion
Fusarium is a common soil saprophyte and plant pathogen. It is present ubiquitously in the environment, especially in soil and water. Its tissue form is hyaline in nature and is similar to other filamentous fungi (Aspergillus, Scedosporium, Scopulariopsis, Acremonium spp.) in that it has septate hyphal forms [Ammari et al. 1993]. However it may also have unpigmented toruloid forms [Anaissie et al. 2001].
Many species of Fusarium exist. Most infections in humans are associated with the Fusarium solani species complex (FSSC) and the F. oxysporum species complex [Boutati and Anaissie 1997]. In immunocompetent patients, it may cause keratitis, especially in contact lens wearers. It is also known to cause onychomycosis, peritonitis and cellulitis in the immunocompetent host [Dignani and Anaissie 2004]. In the immunocompromised patients, particularly in patients with hematological malignancies and hematopoetic stem cell transplant recipients, Fusarium causes disseminated disease [Boutati and Anaissie 1997, Dignani and Anaissie 2004, Nucci and Anaissie 2002, Nucci and Anaissie 2007].
The mortality rate from disseminated fusariosis in the immunocompromised host ranges between 50-80% [Dignani and Anaissie 2004, Nucci and Anaissie 2002, Nucci and Anaissie 2007]. The clinical spectrum most commonly includes refractory fever, followed by cutaneous manifestations, sinusitis and pulmonary involvement [Dignani and Anaissie 2004, Nucci et al. 2003]. Cutaneous manifestations range from painful erythematous papules with or without central necrosis to target lesions and those mimicking ecthyma gangrenosum [Nucci et al. 2003]. In contrast to disseminated aspergillosis, in which blood cultures are rarely positive, fusariosis can be diagnosed by blood culture in 40% of these patients [Dignani and Anaissie 2004]. Endophthalmitis and endocarditis are some of the other complications. Diagnosis is based on histopathology and blood cultures. If suspicious of fungal disease, CT scan of the sinuses, chest, abdomen and pelvis, ophthalmological examination and echocardiogram should be considered.
Management of disseminated fusariosis includes debridement of the affected tissues, appropriate antifungal therapy, granulocyte or granulocyte-monocyte stimulating factor for persistently neutropenic patients and removal of infected catheters [Boutati and Anaissie 1997, Dignani and Anaissie 2004, Nucci et al. 2003]. Fusarium spp. have varied susceptibility to antifungal agents. Depending on the species, Fusarium may have low in-vitro susceptibility to 5-flucytosine, fluconazole and amphotericin B and variable susceptibility to the newer azoles [Dignani and Anaissie 2004]. Thus combination therapy may be required for a successful outcome. Poor prognostic factors are persistent neutropenia and corticosteroid therapy [Boutati and Anaissie 1997, Dignani and Anaissie 2004, Nucci and Anaissie 2002, Nucci et al. 2003].
Final Diagnosis
Disseminated fusariosis in an immunocompromised patient.
