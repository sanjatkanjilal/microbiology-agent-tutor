History of Present Illness
2015 July-August Featured Case: A man in his thirties with a history of HIV-1 infection developed a severe bifrontal headache, diffuse body aches, nausea, and vomiting, associated with feeling weak and dizzy. On the 5th day of his illness, he saw his doctor, and then was referred to an emergency department. On evaluation, he reported headache that rated 10 (on a scale of 0 to 10 where 10 is the most severe), photophobia, neck stiffness, and decreased urine output, with no diarrhea.
Past Medical History
HIV-1 infection had been diagnosed 8 years earlier; at the time of diagnosis, the CD4 cell count was 173 per cubic millimeter. Routine testing for co-infections was positive for IgG antibodies to cytomegalovirus and toxoplasma, and negative for hepatitis B surface antigen and antibodies, and hepatitis C virus antibodies. The next year, therapy with efavirenz, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate was begun. Four years later (three years before this presentation), the level of HIV RNA rose to 69,300 per milliliter. HIV genotyping revealed a resistance to first-generation non-nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) and his antiretroviral medications were changed to darunavir, raltegravir, ritonavir, emtricitabine and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate. There was a history of intermittent non-adherence to antiretroviral medications during the 6 months before this presentation, reportedly because of insurance issues. Two months before this evaluation, he presented with weight loss, fevers and diarrhea. The CD4 cell count was 17 per cubic millimeter (1.3%) and HIV-RNA level was 74,000 copies per milliliter, and a diagnosis of cytomegalovirus infection, including esophagitis and colitis, was made. He was instructed to resume taking his prescribed antiretroviral medications.
He also had a history of alcohol abuse, with symptoms of withdrawal but no seizures, for which he had attended Alcoholics Anonymous meetings in the past.
Medications
Medications included darunavir (800mg daily), raltegravir (400mg twice daily), ritonavir (100mg daily), emtricitabine (200mg daily) and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (300 mg daily); however the patient reported he had been unable to keep his medications down during the previous 4 days because of vomiting. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (double-strength tablet daily) and azithromycin (1200mg weekly) had been prescribed 19 days earlier but the patient had not begun taking them. Prochlorperazine and ibuprofen were taken as needed.
Allergies
He had no allergies.
Social History
He lived with his wife (who was also HIV positive) and children.
Epidemiological History
He was born in Central America, and moved to the United States 13 years earlier. He had no ill contacts or recent travel, and had worked outdoors, in gardening and construction. He was a former smoker, and reportedly drank alcohol occasionally.
Physical Examination
The patient looked ill and pale; the blood pressure was 95/52 mm Hg sitting and 89/52 mm Hg standing, and the pulse rose from 59 sitting to 66 beats per minute standing. Respirations were 20 per minute with 98% oxygen saturation, and the temperature was 97.7°F (36.5°C). The tongue was dry and the skin had decreased turgor. The neck was stiff and painful with motion; the mid-abdomen was mildly tender and soft, with normal bowel sounds and no organomegaly. Fundoscopic examination revealed normal optic discs. There was no rash. The remainder of the examination was normal.
Studies
The white cell count was 4500 per cubic millimeter (reference range 4500-11,000, with 43.3% neutrophils, 37.6% lymphocytes, 14% monocytes, 4.7% eosinophils and 0.2% basophils) and the CD4 cell count was 35 per cubic millimeter (2%). The erythrocytes sedimentation rate was 39 mm per hour (reference range 0-13). Blood cultures were sent. Computed tomography of the head without the administration of contrast (Figure 1) revealed a large confluent area of hypoattenuation involving the right temporal and parietal white matter with extension to the external and internal capsule, 4 mm of leftward midline shift, medialization of the right uncus and mild right uncal herniation.

Normal saline and ondansetron were administered intravenously. After the results of the brain imaging were known, lumbar puncture was deferred and dexamethasone, vancomycin and ampicillin were given. The patient was admitted to the hospital for further evaluation and treatment.

Diagnostic Procedure(s) and Result(s)
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain FLAIR images revealed lesions with surrounding edema (Figures 2 and 3) in the cerebral hemispheres (particularly in the right temporal lobe) and cerebellum. A dominant lesion in the right temporal lobe measured 4.2 by 3.1 by 2.4 cm and a lesion in the left cerebellar vermis measured 1.9 by 1.9 by 1.6 cm. There were additional lesions throughout the cerebral hemispheres, bilateral basal ganglia, left thalamus and the cerebellar hemispheres, and peripheral restricted diffusion associated with several of these lesions. Supratentorially, there was approximately 2 mm leftward midline shift, effacement of the ambient cisterns and mild mass effect on the midbrain. Infratentorially, there was marked edema in the cerebellar vermis and mass effect, with partial effacement of the fourth ventricle. There was no evidence of hydrocephalus.
After the administration of gadolinium, the lesions demonstrated target-like enhancement with central enhancement and a peripherally enhancing halo (Figures 4 and 5).
Testing revealed no evidence of Epstein Barr virus (EBV) DNA in the blood; EBV serologies were consistent with prior infection; galactomannan, antibodies to histoplasma, blastomyces, and coccidioides, and cryptococcal antigen were not detected, and testing for 1, 3 beta D glucan and a an interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis was negative. Testing for toxoplasma IgG antibodies was positive and for IgM antibodies was negative. Four sets of blood cultures drawn from different sites and at two different times were sterile.
Treatment and Followup
Additional initial treatment was administered and included sulfadiazine (2gm four times daily), pyrimethamine (200mg once then 75mg daily), and leucovorin (10mg daily). His symptoms rapidly improved, with resolution of headache, nausea and vomiting by the third hospital day. Blood cultures remained negative, and ampicillin and vancomycin were stopped after 3 days. Antiretroviral medications (darunavir, ritonavir, emtricitabine and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate) were resumed. Raltegravir was discontinued because resistance testing showed no evidence for clinically significant protease inhibitor mutations.
Medications on discharge included sulfadiazine, pyrimethamine, leucovorin, darunavir, ritonavir, emtricitabine, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, azithromycin for prophylaxis and fluconazole for oral candidiasis.
The patient improved after discharge, with intensive medication adherence support from community health center staff. Additional medication adjustments included discontinuation of fluconazole, and administration of dapsone for pneumocystis prophylaxis.
On repeat CT imaging 6 weeks later (Figure 6), there was an interval decrease in the size of innumerable supratentorial and infratentorial enhancing lesions with decreased surrounding edema, consistent with response to treatment for toxoplasmosis infection. No new lesions were identified. The patient later missed subsequent infectious disease clinic appointments and was lost to follow-up.

Discussion
This severely immunocompromised patient with HIV infection and a CD4 cell count less than 50 per cubic millimeter presented with severe headache and symptoms suggestive of increased intracranial pressure, and was found to have multiple ring-enhancing lesions with mass effect on MRI scanning of the brain. This presentation is highly suggestive of toxoplasmosis of the central nervous system, the most frequent opportunistic infection identified in immunocompromised patients with HIV infection and focal intracerebral lesions [Porter and Sande, 1993; Pereira-Chioccola et al., 2009]. Additional corroboration of the clinical diagnosis was obtained with positive evidence of anti-toxoplasma IgG antibodies indicating prior exposure and significant symptomatic and radiologic response to treatment for the protozoa Toxoplasma gondii [Cho et al., 2012].
Other diagnoses in patients with HIV-infection and focal brain lesions to consider include central nervous system non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (the second most common cause of intracerebral mass lesions in immunosuppressed HIV-positive patients [Cho et al., 2012] but which is less likely to have multiple lesions or respond to antiretroviral therapy and anti-toxoplasma medications alone) and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (which is less likely to exhibit a mass effect or present with ring-enhancing lesions [DHHS Guidelines 2013]). Examination of a patient with bacterial endocarditis with central nervous system emboli and infarcts would have likely revealed evidence of multiple peripheral emboli, and blood cultures might have been positive, in the absence of antibacterial therapy. Chagasic encephalitis may mimic toxoplasma encephalitis in an HIV-infected individual who has possible geographic exposures, such as in this patient from Central America, and should be considered in a patient who does not respond to appropriate anti-toxoplasma therapy [Lambert et al., 2006].
Final Diagnosis
Cerebral toxoplasmosis in a patient with HIV infection and a history of antiretroviral non-adherence.
